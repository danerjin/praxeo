# WE-042 / Analysis: Aluminum tariff with export drawback

Analyst: core-only re-run (Handbook 0.8)  |  Rules: core rules CR-1 to CR-12 only  |  Status: compared

## 1. Policy record

**Vessland Aluminum Safeguard and Drawback Act.** Vessland is a trading country of about 9 million people with its own currency, the crown, and a floating exchange rate. It uses about 1.0 million tonnes (t) a year of primary aluminum and semi-finished products at a world price of about $2,500 a tonne. Two domestic smelters (capacity 470,000 t, about 3,800 employees) produce about 400,000 t; electricity, bought from the state grid, is about 35% of their cost. The other 600,000 t is imported. Vessland buys about 1.5% of the aluminum traded in the world. About 1,200 firms use aluminum, employing about 60,000 people. The aluminum content of their exports is about 250,000 t a year (100,000 t imported metal, 150,000 t domestic); the remaining 750,000 t goes into goods sold in Vessland (500,000 t imported, 250,000 t domestic). Machined and stamped parts leave 25% to 40% of the metal as process scrap; cans, foil and cable 2% to 5%. Remelters make about 120,000 t a year from scrap; Vessland exports about 60,000 t of scrap, and the domestic scrap price sits about $40 below the export price. The main industrial region lies 30 to 60 km from Ostrel, which has no tariff on aluminum; trucking costs about $30 a tonne each way.

Customs' static projection: 600,000 t times $500 = $300 million a year, less drawback of at most about 250,000 t times $495 = $124 million.

Counterfactual: Vessland without the Act (no tariff, no drawback) at the same world price. The counterfactual law has no charge on importing and no refund on exporting.

Unclear provisions:
- **U1, substitution.** U1-a (direct identification): the claimant must trace the tariff-paid metal into the exported goods. U1-b (substitution): aluminum of the same grade used in exported goods within three years of an import qualifies against that import's entry, whatever its origin. No guidance, no ruling. Each reading's sign is reported where it matters.

**Final check before the report.**
1. *Uncombined chains.* Every (class, variable, horizon) with chains of both signs is in the table above. The smelters' asset value (C-6) has no opposing chain during the term; the loss of the premium as expiry nears is a change in the size of a gain against the counterfactual, not a reversal (section 8). Home fabricators' quantity (C-14) and their exports (C-16) are different variables for overlapping firms and are not netted (CR-12c).
2. *Group signs.* Customs' fixed budget (C-25) and the payers and recipients of the fund's net receipts (C-47) are groups.
3. *Baseline.* Every sign is against Vessland without the Act; C-28 and C-29 are signed against Customs' stated ceiling, and C-27 against the static projection, as their variables say.
4. *Worse off is not poorer.* `resources_available_other_uses` is signed only for home users who keep buying (A-22). Exporters who claim bear only the unrefunded 1%, the claim cost and the time money is held (CR-3 note), which enter C-20, not a resources consequence.
5. *Timing shifts.* Imports and stocks before 1 Sep are signed per window (C-10, C-11, C-12).
6. *Both sides of every transfer.* Tariff: users pay (C-1, C-46), fund receives (C-3) and smelters receive the price rise (C-2). Drawback: fund pays (C-4), claimants receive (C-20). Net receipts: C-30 and C-47. Revaluations: smelters gain (C-6), home fabricators lose (C-15), stock holders gain (C-13); the buyers of those assets after announcement pay the new price and earn the normal return (CR-7c), so no further consequence.
7. *Every class, every core rule.* CR-4 pairs, CR-6, CR-7 (including CR-7c) and CR-9 were applied to each class in section 3. Foreign smelters' price was checked under CR-6 (0.7, wider market) and needs A-18 (not reported). Customs' enforcement cost and the users' payers' side are recorded.

## 8. Time structure

- **Announcement window (3 Feb to 31 Aug 2027).** The tariff becomes expected at announcement and certain at passage on 20 May. Smelters' values rise and home fabricators' values fall over that period (CR-7c with rising probability; C-6, C-15). Users import and store ahead of 1 Sep (C-10, C-11), and holders of metal in Vessland gain (C-13). Metal stocked before 1 Sep paid no tariff, so under U1-a it earns no drawback when exported; under U1-b any later entry of the same grade can be claimed against it.
- **First months after 1 Sep 2027.** Imports are lowest while stocks are drawn down (C-12). Under U1-a exporters using domestic metal pay import parity with no refund (C-17) and switch to traced imported metal within about a year (C-18, A-20); the smelters move those sales to home users (C-19). Claims are filed only after export, and paid up to 120 days after acceptance, so the drawback outlay lags the tariff receipts.
- **Medium (to 31 Aug 2031).** Fabricators shift sales toward exports (C-16), round trips near the Ostrel border grow (C-22, C-23), and some home-market fabrication moves to Ostrel (C-14, C-48). The 2030 review is a focus for lobbying (C-24).
- **Expiry.** The smelters' premium is capitalized only for the remaining term unless renewal is expected (CR-7c, CR-10c): as 31 Aug 2031 approaches, the asset-value gain against the counterfactual shrinks, and rises again if renewal becomes likely. Capacity beyond 470,000 t is not reported to be built (A-19, p 0.35). Restarted pots can be idled again (A-4); fabrication moved to Ostrel partly stays (C-48, CR-10b, A-N2). Drawback claims can be filed for three years after each import, so payments continue after expiry; K3 repayments likewise.
- **Persistence.** Not a repeal; no persistence field is given.

## 11. Report

**The policy:** From 1 September 2027 to 31 August 2031, Vessland charges 20% (about $500 a tonne) on imports of unwrought and semi-finished aluminum, but not on scrap or on finished goods and parts. Exporters of goods made in Vessland with metal on which the tariff was paid get 99% of it back on claim; Customs' yield tables count process scrap as metal used. Goods re-imported after drawback must repay it. Whether domestic metal can be claimed against import entries of the same grade (U1-b) or must be traced (U1-a) is undecided.

**Visible consequences:** Aluminum costs firms in Vessland about $500 a tonne more (C-1). The smelters receive the higher price (C-2), restart idle capacity (C-5) and employ more people (C-40). The general fund collects the tariff (C-3), pays drawback (C-4) and keeps net receipts (C-30). Covered imports fall (C-12). Goods with no imported substitute cost buyers more (C-37).

**Invisible consequences:**
- *Users of aluminum:* They buy less covered metal (C-7), more secondary metal (C-8) and more reshaped imports (C-9), and those who keep buying have less for other things (C-46). Fabricators selling at home lose output (C-14) and plant value (C-15) to tariff-free imports and to Ostrel plants (C-36); some of that move outlasts the tariff (C-48). Fabricators shift sales toward export (C-16), lowering regional parts prices (C-38, C-39).
- *Exporters:* Whether drawback leaves their metal cost above or below the world price is ambiguous (C-20). Under U1-a, those using domestic metal pay more at first (C-17) and switch to traced imports (C-18), and the smelters move those sales home (C-19). Claims cost paperwork (C-21). Near the border, some goods make round trips to Ostrel to collect the refund and come back undeclared (C-22, C-23).
- *Scrap and secondary metal:* Remelters produce more at a higher price (C-31, C-32); scrap fetches more at home (C-33) and less is exported (C-34).
- *Customs and the budget:* Tariff revenue falls short of the static projection (C-27). Drawback is below Customs' ceiling in the first year under U1-a and above it under U1-b (C-28), and above it in later years (C-29). Customs' fixed budget means slower claims, slower clearance or fewer other checks (C-25, C-26). Taxpayers or recipients of other spending gain from the net receipts (C-47).
- *Timing and assets:* Users stock up before 1 September (C-10, C-11); holders of metal (C-13) and smelter owners (C-6) gain when the tariff becomes expected.
- *Others:* Foreign smelters sell less to Vessland (C-35); the state grid sells more power to smelters (C-44); everyone involved lobbies (C-24).

**Ambiguous effects:** Exporters' net metal cost (C-20): the unrefunded 1%, claim costs and money held push it up; refunds on process scrap and resale of that scrap at higher prices push it down. Total employment in aluminum-using firms (C-43): home-market jobs fall, export jobs rise. The exchange rate (C-45): fewer metal imports and more exports push the crown up; more finished-goods imports push it down. Drawback against Customs' ceiling in the first year depends on the reading of U1 (C-28).

**Key conditions:** Import parity (A-1, 0.9) and foreign smelters' better alternatives (A-14, 0.9) decide that users pay and smelters gain. Specific capital and import competition for finished goods (A-15, 0.75) drive the losses of home fabricators and the shift to exports, with A-8 (0.6). The round-trip result rests on cheap freight and weak audits (A-10, 0.6; A-9, 0.65). Two premises were added: some claimants beat the yield tables (A-N1, 0.6) and fabrication moved to Ostrel costs something to move back (A-N2, 0.65).

**What this analysis cannot say:** How large any effect is; whether home-market job losses exceed export gains; whether the crown rises or falls; whether the drawback leaves exporters better or worse off than free trade; whether the policy is good. Health, safety and environmental effects, including smelter emissions and energy use, are out of scope.

## Gaps found

**(a) Steps no core rule covers.** None. Every step was derived from CR-1 to CR-12, LW-7 and LW-10, and arithmetic on the case's figures.

**(b) Core-rule wording that was hard to apply.**
- CR-4 states the response when x is made worse *relative to* y, but here both options (home sales and export sales) are made worse, by very different amounts. The sign of exports (C-16) needed CR-12e arithmetic on top of CR-4; CR-4 does not say how to treat a relative change when both options worsen and one of the two worsenings is itself ambiguous (C-20).
- CR-3's note covers a charge "later refunded" in full. It does not say how to treat a partial refund (99%), a refund on more metal than was used (yield tables counting process scrap), or a refund on metal that is then sold again as scrap. C-20 was handled with CR-12b.
- CR-6 (0.7, wider market) says to record the wider-market price's sign "with a premise if the effect may be negligible". With the case premise at p 0.15 (A-18), the consequence is not reported, but the rules do not say whether the foreign smelters' price should instead be recorded as `no_change`.
- CR-9 (currency) gives the direction of each flow but no guidance where the *form* of imports changes (metal replaced by finished goods that carry more value per tonne), so the total value of imports, and the exchange rate, could not be signed (C-45).
- Under U1-b, K2 creates a transferable claim (designation of entries) in excess supply: about 600,000 t of entries against about 250,000 t of export content. CR-7c says a policy-created asset's value goes to whoever receives it at creation, and CR-6 that a price in excess supply falls toward cost; together they suggest the designation price falls toward the cost of designating, so exporters, not importers of record, keep the refund. No consequence is reported on the designation price because no variable fits it well (see (c)), but the combination of CR-6 and CR-7c for a created right in excess supply is not spelled out in either rule.

**(c) Vocabulary missing from the ontology.**
- No variable for exports as such; `quantity_supplied (exports of ...)` was used.
- No variable for the net cost of an input per unit of output after refunds and by-product sales; `price_paid` with a qualifier was used (C-20).
- No variable for the price of a transferable claim created by a policy other than `asset_value` and `right_lease_price` (designation of entries under U1-b).
- No actor class for importers of record as distinct from users; `importers_of_record` and `importers.covered` were introduced.
- No variable for the time a refund is held before payment; it was folded into `wait_time (drawback payment)` in C-25.

## Comparison with the pattern-based analysis

### Findings

- **Pattern overclaims (OC-3, fixed-budget clause).** OC-3 says that from a fixed budget "enforcement of other rules falls instead". CR-8a gives only that the staff time is withdrawn from some other use. The margin can be the new task itself (refunds paid more slowly) or other service (clearance), and CR-6's 0.7 public-supply clause asks for a premise per margin. OC-3 should record the margins as a group with `at_least_one_as_stated`, or name a premise for the one it signs (C-44).
- **Pattern hides a premise (SB-10 for a drawback; the version's own finding confirmed).** SB-10's domestic-price clause assumes that domestic buyers cannot import the good untaxed, and its "combine with CR-12b" does not fit a check on the return crossing. The core version reached the same results without SB-10: imports cap the domestic price of the parts (C-14, C-36), and round trips follow from CR-4 with an enforcement premise (C-22). The core version confirms both points the pattern version raised.
- **Errors in the pattern version.** (1) C-18 and C-47: the export-subsidy element leaves out the cost of holding the tariff until the refund arrives, which C-17 counts. A-6's $19 threshold covers only the 1%, so the sign is not established. (2) C-17 (and C-46): it misses the chain from claiming by the tables at the claimant's option (core A-N1), which can reverse the sign for low-scrap claimants who beat the tables. (3) C-19 and C-34 rest on SB-10 alone. The core version's home-to-export chain supports them without A-6 and should be added. (4) Omissions: holders' stock values (core C-13), exporters of domestic metal under U1-a (core C-17), drawback against the ceiling (core C-28), the price of secondary metal (core C-32), foreign smelters' and regional parts makers' sides (core C-35, C-39), employment in export fabrication (core C-42).
- **Errors in the core version.** (1) C-19 under U1-b: `no_change` ignores export volume, so it should be `ambiguous`. (2) C-20: it does not split claimants by scrap ratio (CR-1 recording), and it nets claim costs into `price_paid` (CR-12c). (3) Final check 4: it misreads CR-3's note and so drops exporters' `resources_available_other_uses`. (4) C-29 overclaims: total exports are not signed. (5) C-40, C-41 and C-42 sign `employment` alone where the group form is derivable. (6) Omissions: the two `tax_share_borne` splits (pattern C-38, C-39), importers' tariff-entry compliance (pattern C-45), total exports (pattern C-36), and the opposing chain on the scrap price (pattern C-27).
- **Error in both versions.** The core version signs exports of mixed fabricators and parts makers up (C-16, and C-42 through it). The pattern version signs high-scrap exports up (C-19). Neither combines the currency chain, which each records as `ambiguous` in its own right (core C-45, pattern C-35). By CR-12's 0.8 clarification (kind 3), an ambiguous chain makes the combined sign `ambiguous` unless a premise covers the other chain against both of its directions. Both versions should give such a premise or report these as `ambiguous`. The pattern version applied kind (3) to C-35 and C-36 but not to C-19.
- **Test of 0.8 (the pattern version's verdicts).** *TX-8, both directions (needed interpretation):* confirmed. The core version also found the currency chain ambiguous when one policy moves imports both ways, and it notes that CR-9 says nothing about a change in the form of imports. *SB-10, round trips and checks (needed interpretation); domestic-price clause (failed):* both confirmed. The core version never needed CR-12b for the check, and it never derived a domestic price rise for goods not covered at import. *CR-12e, a second change (needed interpretation):* not tested. The core version did not record the opposing chain on the scrap price that raised the question. *Drawback as close to an export subsidy (recorded as "worked"):* the core version contradicts it. Once the holding cost is counted, the subsidy element is not signed on A-6 as stated.
- **Limits of the core rules.** None produced a difference (no K). Wording that caused trouble: CR-3's note covers a refund in full, but not a partial refund, a refund on more metal than was used, or what happens to claimants' resources (the core version misread it); CR-12c leaves open whether holding and claim costs belong in a net `price_paid`; CR-9 gives no guidance when the form of imports changes; CR-6's 0.7 wider-market clause does not say whether a negligible effect is `no_change` or not reported; and no rule states how CR-6 and CR-7c combine for a right the policy creates in excess supply (designations under U1-b).
