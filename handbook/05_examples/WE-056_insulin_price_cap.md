# WE-056 A cap on what patients pay for insulin at the pharmacy counter, with the balance paid by insurers or a state fund

Analyst: draft (Handbook 1.0 held-out test, corrected under 1.1)  |  Rule catalog version: 1.1  |  Status: awaiting expert review

A stylized policy in a fictional state. Several real states cap patients' out-of-pocket cost for insulin; this one is not modeled on any of them. It is a held-out test of catalog 1.0 on a case none of the 1.0 changes were drawn from. It probes PC-1 and PC-16 where the cap falls on what the buyer pays, not on what the seller receives, because a third party pays the balance; CR-6's 1.0 note that small effects in a wider market are signed (national list prices, private-label human insulin, manufacturers' shares); RS-16's 1.0 clause on prices per unit of quality (patients move to pens and newer analogs, and from over-the-counter human insulin); OC-1's and CR-8's 1.0 clauses on payers and on receipts into the same budget (a fund paid from general revenue, which also receives premium tax on the higher premiums and the fines); RS-4 for mail-order pharmacies outside the state; and CR-12's 1.0 notes on what the response argument needs and on the baseline of patterns. The unclear provision is whether out-of-state mail-order pharmacies that ship to residents are bound. The counterfactual throughout is **Tarrenmoor without the Act**: the same plans, pharmacies, manufacturers' programs and list prices as they would otherwise have been. Health effects (fewer missed doses, fewer emergency admissions) are outside the scope of version 1. The probabilities are illustrative; the evidence column says what a real analysis would cite. After its core-only re-run (`05_examples_core/WE-056_insulin_price_cap_core.md`) the analysis was corrected under catalog 1.1; the changes are listed before "Gaps found".

## 1. Policy record

**Tarrenmoor Insulin Affordability Act.** Tarrenmoor is a state of about 4.3 million people. About 120,000 residents use insulin. By source of coverage:

| Group | Insulin users | What they pay now for a 30-day supply |
|---|---|---|
| Members of plans regulated by the state insurance code (fully insured group plans, 39,000; individual-market plans, 9,000) | 48,000 | Average $88. Members of high-deductible plans (about 40%) pay the plan's contracted price, about $290 for an analog insulin, until the deductible is met; others pay copays of $25 to $75 or coinsurance. About 70% pay more than $35 in a typical month. |
| Members of self-funded employer plans, governed by the national Employee Benefit Plans Act, which overrides state insurance law | 30,000 | Plan terms; not reachable by the state. |
| National old-age and low-income programs | 32,000 | Already $35 or less under national rules. |
| Uninsured | 10,000 | 2,500 receive analog insulin free through manufacturers' patient-assistance programs (income test, yearly re-application, shipped by the program's pharmacy); 3,000 buy human insulin without a prescription at about $25 a vial (about $50 a month); 4,500 buy analog insulin on prescription at pharmacy cash prices, about $320 (wholesale acquisition cost about $295 plus a markup of about $25). In a 2026 state survey, about 40% of the last group and 15% of high-deductible members said they had used less insulin than prescribed because of cost. |

List prices (wholesale acquisition costs) are set nationally by three manufacturers; about 8 million people in the country use insulin, so the 58,000 users the Act reaches are about 0.7% of the national total. Private-label human insulin is sold by national pharmacy chains at one national price. The manufacturers' assistance programs exclude people whose insulin is paid for by "any public program or fund" (their standard payer-of-last-resort clause), but apply it to state funds only at their discretion.

Tarrenmoor has about 1,050 pharmacies licensed under chapter 18, §18-4 of the pharmacy law (pharmacies located in the state). Under §18-9 the Board of Pharmacy issues about 140 "nonresident pharmacy permits" to pharmacies outside the state that ship prescriptions to residents; §18-9(3) says "a holder of a nonresident permit is subject to the provisions of this chapter governing dispensing". About 20% of insulin fills for state-plan members are by mail, three quarters of them from two mail-order pharmacies owned by pharmacy benefit managers in Lisvane, the neighboring state. Plans may require mail order for maintenance drugs only if members may opt out to a retail pharmacy. Contracts between pharmacy benefit managers (PBMs) and pharmacies run by calendar year and were signed for 2028 in the autumn of 2027.

Premiums of state-regulated plans total about $9.4 billion a year for about 1.4 million members. The state levies a 2% premium tax on them, paid into the general fund. Prescription drugs are exempt from sales tax. Many individual-market buyers receive national premium credits that cap their net premium as a share of income; about a third do not.

The Act was signed on 12 April 2027 and takes effect on 1 January 2028. No expiry.

Unclear provision:
- **U1, mail-order pharmacies outside the state.** §3 binds "a pharmacy licensed by the Board of Pharmacy under chapter 18". Chapter 18 "licenses" in-state pharmacies under §18-4 and issues "permits" to nonresident pharmacies under §18-9, while §18-9(3) subjects permit holders to the chapter's dispensing provisions. Reading U1-a: a nonresident permit holder is a pharmacy licensed under chapter 18; out-of-state mail-order pharmacies shipping to residents are bound by §3 and may bill the plan or the Fund for the balance. Reading U1-b: only §18-4 licensees are bound; out-of-state pharmacies collect the plan's ordinary cost sharing (or their cash price from the uninsured) and cannot bill the Fund. The Board has issued no guidance. Readings get no probability; where a sign depends on U1, one sign per reading is given.

**Figures.** State-plan members: the cap lowers average out-of-pocket cost from about $88 to about $34 per fill, so plans pay about $54 more per fill: about 48,000 × 12 × $54 ≈ $31 million a year, plus the extra fills of members who rationed, about $34 million in all, or about 0.36% of state-regulated premiums. Premium tax on that: about $0.7 million. Uninsured: if pharmacies set the usual and customary price at the Fund's limit (about $354 on a $295 acquisition cost), each fill costs the Fund about $319. The 4,500 current cash buyers cost about $17 million, or about $19 million with the end of rationing; 2,000 over-the-counter buyers who obtain a prescription would add about $8 million, and the 2,500 program enrollees about $10 million if the manufacturers drop them; claims administration adds $3 million: about $39 million against the fiscal note's $19 million (4,500 buyers at the current $320 price less $35, plus administration). Fines: at most a few hundred thousand dollars a year.

**Why PC-1 and PC-16 are not applied.** If K1 is recorded as a `price_ceiling` on insulin (the money price paid at the counter is fixed below what it would be), PC-1's If clause is met and gives `excess_demand`; PC-2 would then give rationing by waiting or favoritism, and PC-16 says the buyer pays less and the seller receives less, "by the same amount". Derived from the core rules, none of this follows. CR-6's pressure exists only where the quantity buyers wish to buy differs from what sellers wish to sell at the price *sellers receive*. Here the pharmacy receives its contract rate from patient and plan together (K2) or its own usual and customary price up to a limit from patient and Fund together (K3): the cap moves who pays, not what the seller gets. At what pharmacies receive, they wish to sell every fill demanded (insulin is stocked by every pharmacy, and the payment exceeds acquisition cost, A-1), so no shortage forms and no rationing follows. The transfer runs from plans and the Fund (and through them from premium payers and the general budget) to patients, not from sellers to buyers (CR-8b). The component is analyzed as a purchase subsidy with a mandated payer: SB-2 for the buyer's price and quantity, SB-1 and OC-1 for who pays. Under catalog 1.1 this is PC-20 (a cap on the buyer's share): PC-1's and PC-16's 1.1 clauses exclude a cap that a third party makes up, and CR-6's 1.1 note says the seller's receipt is set by the payer's terms. The one place where a price is capped for the seller is the Fund's limit on the usual and customary price (wholesale cost plus 20%). It lies above the price pharmacies charge today, so it does not bind against the counterfactual; it binds only against the higher price pharmacies would set for a payer that does not respond to price (C-6; PC-20 and CR-6 1.1: the posted price is drawn toward the limit). Gaps found, (c) 1.

C-1 to C-3 are what the Act's supporters expect: patients pay $35, and plans and the Fund pay the rest. C-3 is an outlay that exists only under the Act, an increase against none (CR-12, 0.9); its size is in section 1. Members of self-funded plans, of national programs and non-residents are not covered and are not in C-1.

**What the pharmacy receives (C-4).** For a state-plan member, §4(a) has the plan pay the balance "on the terms of its pharmacy contract": the pharmacy receives the contract rate, from patient and plan together, as it would without the Act. The 2028 contracts were signed in the autumn of 2027 and fix the rate for the year (CR-6, 0.7: terms fixed by existing contracts count as regulated while they last; A-2). So the pharmacy's price per insured fill does not change in 2028. That is the sign PC-16 got wrong under 1.0: the buyer pays less, and the seller does not receive less (section 2). PC-20 (1.1) now says it: the seller's receipt is set by the payer's terms. Renewals from 2029 are C-33.

**More insulin bought (C-5).** At $35, those who used less than prescribed because of cost buy more (CR-4c: the last units, given up at the old price, are now worth their cost to the buyer; A-3, from the 2026 survey). Two other responses touch the quantity and do not oppose it: prior authorization (C-9) changes which product a member receives, not whether a month's supply is filled (A-6), and members who drop individual coverage (C-21) still buy insulin, as uninsured residents at $35.

**Plans' outlay (C-2).** The cap shifts to plans what members paid above $35, about $54 a fill on average (+). Plans respond by steering members to cheaper preferred products (C-9), which lowers the rate per fill (−). The response argument does not settle this: the plans' steering is a response to the share of each dollar of the rate they now bear, which steering does not undo (CR-12e, 0.8). A-24 is the dominance premise (arithmetic on the case's figures with an empirical premise): the contracted rates of the analog insulins a formulary can steer between differ by much less than the $54 shifted. So C-2 is an `increase`.

**The price the Fund pays (C-6 to C-8).** The Fund pays the pharmacy's own usual and customary price, less $35, up to wholesale cost plus 20%. The uninsured patient pays $35 whatever that price is, so a higher usual price loses the pharmacy no Fund-paid sale. Its only cost is to cash customers who are not covered residents, who pay that price (CR-4: x: keep the usual price at about $320, y: raise it toward about $354). Most pharmacies have few such customers (A-4), so the usual price rises toward the limit (C-6), and so does the price those few customers pay (C-7); some of them buy less at Tarrenmoor pharmacies, filling at home or elsewhere (C-48; CR-4c). This is the mirror of PC-16: the seller receives more, not less, and the payer that does not respond to price (the Fund) pays it. Catalog 1.1 states the move: where a payer pays the seller's own posted price up to a limit, the price is drawn toward the limit (CR-6 1.1, PC-20, SB-2 1.1). The Fund's payment is then above what pharmacies would accept for a fill. That is CR-6's pressure from the sellers' side: pharmacies compete for Fund-paid customers on the margins still open, better service or inducements, which state pharmacy rules forbid but cannot fully police (C-8, a group; CR-6 gives at least one margin). The Fund pays any product prescribed, so the gap is largest on the costliest products.

**Which insulin (C-9 to C-11).** With the patient's payment fixed at $35 for every product, the patient's (and prescriber's) cost of choosing a pen over a vial, or a newer analog over an older one, falls to zero (CR-4). For the uninsured, the Fund pays for any insulin prescribed, so the mix moves toward the products patients rank higher (C-10; A-7). For state-plan members the plan now bears the whole difference in rate between products, so plans restrict the costlier ones by prior authorization and step therapy, which the insurance code allows (C-9; A-6); members whose prescriptions need authorization wait for the decision (C-45; CR-9, the insurance contract; CR-8c). The two pushes oppose, and no premise ranks them: C-11 is `ambiguous`. By RS-16's 1.0 clause, prices are signed per product: as the mix moves toward pens and newer analogs, the average amount paid per fill rises, but that is a change in mix, not a price change, and it is not recorded as one. Under 1.0, RS-21 was considered for C-10 and not cited, since K1 is not a cap or right counted in physical units. RS-21's 1.1 clause covers a payment counted in units: the patient's payment is fixed per 30-day supply whatever the product, so the value per counted unit rises (C-10), which CR-4 also gives directly.

**Human insulin without a prescription (C-12).** An uninsured buyer of over-the-counter human insulin pays about $50 a month; with a prescription, an analog insulin costs $35 (RS-1 in reverse; RS-16, mirror case). The Act covers only insulin dispensed on prescription, so the switch needs a clinic visit (C-31); some make it (A-8). Some high-deductible members who bought human insulin to avoid the deductible switch too.

**Assistance programs (C-13, C-14).** The manufacturers' programs give insulin free to uninsured residents below an income line. Once the Fund pays wholesale cost plus 20% for the same patient, a manufacturer compares giving a unit free with selling it to a pharmacy that the Fund pays (CR-4); its payer-of-last-resort clause lets it drop Tarrenmoor residents (A-9). Enrollees then pay $35 where they paid nothing (C-13): they are worse off by their own ranking and in money (CR-3a), and they are the class the Act's supporters least expect to lose. The manufacturers' paid units rise (C-14), through this chain and through the end of rationing (C-5); the chains push the same way, and either suffices (CR-12a, CR-12d).

**List prices (C-15, C-16).** List prices are not regulated. A manufacturer that raises its list price loses some sales to patients and payers who respond to price. In Tarrenmoor, covered patients pay $35 whatever the list price, and the Fund's limit moves with the wholesale cost, so a higher list price loses fewer sales there than it would have (CR-4: x: a list price, y: a higher one; the Act makes y better). The direction is a priori; that a manufacturer's choice is close enough to the margin for this to move it is A-10. The 58,000 users are about 0.7% of national users, so the effect is small; CR-6's 1.0 note says to sign it, not to leave it unsigned as negligible. It falls on buyers and payers in other states (C-16). No opposing chain is derivable; pressure on manufacturers from the Act's cost to the state is a Tier C note only (TS-4 is not derivable).

**Timing (C-17, C-18).** Between signing (April 2027) and 1 January 2028, a fill bought in December costs the patient the old price and the same fill in January costs $35. Insulin keeps for months unopened, so patients who can, buy only what they need in December and fill again in January (RS-8; CR-10a; A-11). High-deductible members who would have stocked up in December after meeting the deductible have less reason to. Fills in November and December 2027 fall (C-17); in January and February 2028 they rise, from the deferred fills and from the lasting increase (C-18; either chain suffices). Each window is signed against the counterfactual (02_ontology.md, 2.5), on the flow of fills.

**Premiums and the plans' members (C-19 to C-21).** Plans pass their added outlay (C-2) to their premium payers when 2028 rates are filed in mid-2027, after the Act was signed (CR-9, the insurance contract; CR-6 0.7: premiums are fixed for the plan year; A-12). A member who uses insulin pays less at the counter; every member pays a little more in premium: both sides of the transfer are recorded (C-1, C-19). A second chain could oppose C-19 in the individual market: insulin users for whom insurance was worth buying mainly for insulin now get it for $35 uninsured, and if some drop coverage the pool loses high-cost members. Its premise (A-25) has p = 0.4, below the cut-off, so C-19 is reported as an `increase` and the chain is recorded in section 10 (CR-12, 0.8, kind 2). An employer near the line between buying a fully insured plan and self-funding finds the insured plan a little dearer (C-20; RS-5; A-13). Individual-market buyers without premium credits face a dearer plan; some at the margin go without (C-21; CR-4c; A-14).

**Evasion (C-22, C-23).** A member of a self-funded high-deductible plan pays about $290 a fill; by attesting to having no coverage, she pays $35 and the Fund pays the rest. A Lisvane resident who gives a Tarrenmoor address does the same. Pharmacies cannot easily check (RS-3; A-15). Some pharmacies collect more than $35 (C-23): a breach that exists only under the Act, an increase against none (CR-12, 0.9; A-16).

**Lobbying (C-24).** What patients pay for insulin and other drugs was contested before the Act, and would have stayed so (CR-4 1.0, RS-7 1.0). By CR-4's 1.1 note, "the same question" is that policy field, not the exact provision: lobbying over the level of the cap and over extending it to self-funded plans' members or to other drugs belongs to it, and is not signed. Lobbying over provisions that exist only under the Act is an increase against none: the reading of U1 (in-state pharmacies against PBM-owned mail order), the Fund's payment rule and limit (pharmacies against the treasury), and the attestation and balance-billing rules (A-17).

**Mail order outside the state, by reading (C-25 to C-28).** RS-4 asks which side of the boundary the policy favors.
- *U1-b.* Only in-state pharmacies are bound. A state-plan member who fills by mail from Lisvane pays the plan's ordinary cost sharing (about $88 on average), and at an in-state pharmacy pays $35. Plans that require mail order must let members opt out to a retail pharmacy, where the cap applies (A-19). So members paying more than $35 move their fills inside the state, and uninsured buyers, who can use the Fund only at an in-state pharmacy, do the same: cross-border fills fall (C-25) and in-state fills rise (C-26). Members who move give up home delivery and travel to a pharmacy (C-28; CR-3a: the option they chose is now dearer, so they take one ranked lower before). The Board's reach does not matter under U1-b, since nothing binds the out-of-state pharmacy.
- *Both readings: retail pharmacies in Lisvane.* Lisvane's retail pharmacies hold no Tarrenmoor license and are bound under neither reading. Residents near the line who filled insulin there (A-29) pay $35 at a Tarrenmoor pharmacy and the plan's cost sharing or the cash price in Lisvane, so they fill at home (C-47; RS-4, CR-4), which adds to in-state fills (C-26).
- *U1-a.* Out-of-state permit holders are bound and may bill plans and the Fund like in-state pharmacies. The patient's payment is then the same on either side of the line, so CR-4 gives the patient no reason to move. Two chains reach the out-of-state fills. More insulin is bought overall (C-5), some by mail (+). Complying with Tarrenmoor's balance billing, Fund claims and usual-price rule is a cost fixed per pharmacy for the state's patients (CR-4, 0.8: it bears on whether to serve them), and some nonresident permit holders with few Tarrenmoor insulin patients stop shipping insulin there (A-18) (−). No premise ranks them: `ambiguous`, as RS-4's 1.1 clause on two chains asks (CR-12b). RS-4's 0.9 clause on the reach of enforcement applies: the Board's power over the out-of-state pharmacy runs through the nonresident permit it can suspend, so under U1-a the boundary that matters is the permit, not the state line, and it is enforced (A-1). C-27 is the nonresident pharmacies' added compliance work under U1-a (none under U1-b); C-26 is an increase under both readings, from C-5 and C-47 under U1-a (either suffices, CR-12d).

**Human insulin (C-29, C-30).** Some buyers leave private-label human insulin for an analog at $35 (C-12). Human insulin is sold nationally at one price, and Tarrenmoor's buyers are a small part of that market. In the short run the chains' contracted volumes cannot be cut at once (A-20), so the pressure goes down on the price or up on their stocks (RS-16, mirror case; CR-6). The effect may be very small; CR-6's 1.0 note says it is signed, with its size in the evidence column. The price is per vial of a given product (RS-16, 1.0), not an average over a changing mix. With the chains' purchases fixed by contract (A-20), the fall in demand goes to the national price, to the chains' stocks, or to both; CR-6's 0.9 note asks for a premise on that split, and the case has none, so C-29 is a group: price down, stocks up, `at_least_one_as_stated`. In the long run, producers contract. CR-12's response argument says the contraction cannot push the price above its old level, but its 1.0 note says what that needs: a premise that cost per unit does not rise as the activity contracts. Human insulin is made in a few plants with large fixed costs, so cost per vial rises as output falls (A-21), and equal yields can be restored at a price above the old one. Without the constant-cost premise the long-run price is `ambiguous` (C-30). The argument bounds the national price of private-label human insulin, all brands together; CR-12's 1.1 note confirms that it binds a price fall in the same way as a rise.

**Complements (C-31, C-32).** The cap applies only to insulin dispensed on prescription, so uninsured buyers who switch from over-the-counter human insulin need a prescriber's visit (C-31; RS-12, `complement`; A-8). More insulin used means more pen needles and syringes (C-32; RS-12; A-3).

**Pharmacy contracts after 2028 (C-33).** Before the Act, a high-deductible member in the deductible paid the whole contract rate, so a lower rate saved the member, not the plan. Now the plan pays all but $35 of it. A PBM negotiating 2029 rates for insulin therefore gains more from each dollar off the rate (CR-4; CR-5c: the bargaining range moves). Some PBMs press insulin rates down at renewal (A-22). The 2028 rate is C-4.

**The deductible (C-44).** In a high-deductible plan only the member's own payment counts toward the deductible (A-28). A fill that cost the member $290 counted $290; at $35 it counts $35. So the deductible is met later in the year, and the member pays in full for other covered care for longer (CR-9, the insurance contract). The saving at the counter comes back in part through another term of the same contract (CR-3, 1.1), which is why the means of these members are `ambiguous` (C-43, section 9).

**Premium credits, false attestations and the PBM pharmacies (C-49 to C-51).** Many individual-market buyers receive national credits that cap their net premium as a share of income; for them a higher premium is paid by the national government (C-49; CR-9, CR-8b). When a self-funded member attests falsely (C-22), the Fund pays and the self-funded plan pays less than it would have (C-51; CR-8b), the other side of that transfer. The PBM-owned mail-order pharmacies' value follows their expected Tarrenmoor fills (CR-7c, CR-10c): under U1-b fills move in-state (C-25), so it falls; under U1-a more fills overall (C-5) meet the compliance cost and the loss of small shippers' business (C-27, A-18), so it is `ambiguous`. The effect on a national business is small and signed (CR-6, 1.0).

**Manufacturers' value (C-34).** Their paid units rise (C-14) and the list price may rise (C-15); both raise expected yields, capitalized when the Act became expected (CR-7c, CR-10c; A-23: the Act was not expected before its introduction in January 2027). The effect on a national firm's shares is small and signed (CR-6, 1.0).

**Receipts into the same budget (C-35, C-36).** The Fund is appropriated from the general fund. The general fund receives 2% of the higher premiums (A-12), and the Fund receives the fines (C-36; A-16). The premium tax has a second chain: employers that convert to self-funding (C-20; A-13) and individual buyers who drop coverage (C-21; A-14) take whole premiums out of the tax base, against 2% of a rise of about 0.36%, and employers renewed for 2028 after the Act was signed. No premise ranks the two, so the premium tax is `ambiguous` (C-35; TX-10, CR-12b); at most it rises by about $0.7 million. By CR-8's 1.1 note the analysis says how it treats the two funds: the general fund and the Fund it feeds are treated as one budget for netting. Its items are the Fund's outlay (C-3), the fines (C-36) and the premium tax (C-35), and the net is recorded separately (C-37, section 9). Arithmetic on the case's figures settles the net whatever the sign of C-35: about $39 million out against at most about $0.7 million of premium tax and fines of a few hundred thousand dollars.

Stopping points. Premium increases borne by employers pass, through employment contracts, to wages and benefits of employees in fully insured groups (CR-7b, DM-1's horizon note on factor markets): depth 3 and diffuse across 1.4 million members; stopped. The spending of patients who pay less (C-39) and of premium payers who pay more (C-38): depth 3, diffuse; stopped. Lisvane's mail-order staff under U1-b: depth 3; stopped.

## 8. Time structure

- **15 January to 12 April 2027: introduction to signing.** Manufacturers' shares move as passage becomes likely (C-34; CR-7c's probability clause). Insurers begin to price the cap into 2028 rate filings.
- **12 April to 31 December 2027: the window.** 2028 premiums are filed and approved with the added outlay (C-19), and with them the national credits for credit recipients (C-49). PBM contracts with pharmacies for 2028 are signed in the autumn with the Act known, but the 2028 rate structure is set before the first year's experience (C-4, A-2). Patients defer storable purchases to January (C-17). Manufacturers decide on their programs for 2028 (C-13). Employers renewing for 2028 consider self-funding (C-20).
- **2028.** Fills rise at once and are bunched in January (C-5, C-18); pharmacies raise their usual price for Fund claims (C-6); plans tighten formularies over the year (C-9, C-45); high-deductible members meet their deductibles later in the year (C-44); the Fund's outlay is about double its fiscal note.
- **2029 onward.** PBM contracts are renewed with the plans bearing the insulin cost (C-33). The $35 cap is fixed in money: as list prices and pharmacy costs rise (A-30), the gap between what patients pay and the price widens, and the plans' and the Fund's share grows (TS-3, 1.1: the path is a premise). Producers of human insulin contract (C-30).

## 11. Report

**The policy.** From 1 January 2028, a pharmacy in Tarrenmoor may not collect more than $35 for a month's insulin from a resident who is uninsured or covered by a state-regulated plan. The pharmacy bills the rest to the plan, or for the uninsured to a state fund paid from general revenue, which pays the pharmacy's own usual cash price up to wholesale cost plus 20%. Manufacturers' list prices are not regulated. It is unclear whether out-of-state mail-order pharmacies are bound.

**Visible consequences.** Patients who paid more than $35 pay $35 (C-1) and buy more insulin (C-5). Plans and the Fund pay the balance (C-2, C-3).

**Invisible consequences.**
- *Who pays.* Premiums of state-regulated plans rise (C-19), and premium payers have less for other things (C-38); for individual buyers with national credits, the national government pays the rise (C-49). Some employers self-fund (C-20) and some individual buyers drop coverage (C-21). The Fund costs the general budget about twice its fiscal note, offset only by fines and by a premium tax that may rise or fall (C-35, C-36, C-37).
- *Patients' means.* Patients outside high-deductible plans have more for other things, net of their premium share (C-39). High-deductible members' insulin now counts less toward the deductible, so they pay in full for other care for longer (C-44), and whether they come out ahead depends on their other medical spending (C-43). Members whose insulin needs prior authorization wait for decisions (C-45).
- *Pharmacies.* They receive the same contract rate for insured fills in 2028 (C-4), but more for uninsured fills, since they raise the cash price the Fund pays (C-6), which non-covered cash buyers then pay too (C-7), some of whom buy elsewhere (C-48); they compete for Fund-paid customers on service (C-8). Residents near the border fill at home rather than in Lisvane (C-47). From 2029, PBMs press their insulin rates down (C-33). Their paperwork rises (C-40).
- *Manufacturers.* They sell more paid insulin (C-14) and may drop their free programs for residents (C-13), whose enrollees then pay $35 where they paid nothing, while the manufacturers are paid for what they gave (C-46). List prices rise slightly, for buyers in every state (C-15, C-16), and manufacturers' shares gain a little (C-34).
- *Which insulin.* The uninsured move to pens and newer analogs (C-10); plans add prior authorization (C-9), so for insured members the mix may go either way (C-11). Over-the-counter human insulin loses buyers (C-12), its price dips slightly or the chains hold more stock (C-29), and clinics see more prescription visits (C-31); pen needle and syringe sales rise (C-32).
- *Timing.* Fills fall in late 2027 and bunch in early 2028 (C-17, C-18).
- *Evasion and politics.* Some self-funded members and non-residents claim to be covered residents (C-22), which saves their self-funded plans money (C-51); some pharmacies overcharge (C-23). Lobbying over the Act's own provisions rises (C-24). The Board of Pharmacy's other work slows (C-42).

**Per reading of U1.** If out-of-state mail-order pharmacies are bound (U1-a), they take on billing and compliance work (C-27), and whether out-of-state fills rise or fall is open (C-25). If they are not (U1-b), members move their fills to in-state pharmacies (C-25, C-26) and give up home delivery (C-28), and the PBM mail-order pharmacies lose value (C-50; `ambiguous` under U1-a).

**Ambiguous effects.** The insulin mix for insured members (C-11); out-of-state fills under U1-a (C-25); the long-run price of human insulin (C-30); premium-tax revenue (C-35); high-deductible members' means (C-43); the PBM mail-order pharmacies' value under U1-a (C-50).

**Key conditions.** Compliance by pharmacies (A-1); that pharmacies raise their cash price to the Fund's limit (A-4, p = 0.75); that manufacturers drop residents from their programs (A-9, p = 0.6); that plans pass the cost to premiums (A-12, p = 0.8); that only the member's payment counts toward the deductible (A-28, p = 0.7).

**What this analysis cannot say.** How large any effect is; whether patients' health improves (out of scope for version 1); whether the transfer from premium payers and taxpayers to insulin users is worth it; whether the policy is desirable.

1. *CR-6 and a payer that does not respond to price.* CR-6 is stated over "the quantity buyers wish to buy" and "sellers wish to sell" at a price. Here the price the seller receives and the price the buyer pays are different numbers, with a third party paying the gap. CR-6 had to be read as applying at the price the seller receives (no shortage; C-4) and, for the Fund-paid segment, as pressure from sellers where the payer's price is above what sellers would accept (C-8). A sentence saying "where a third party pays part of the price, apply CR-6 at the price received by the seller and CR-4 to the buyer at the price paid" would help. *1.1:* CR-6's note on a third-party payer now says this.
2. *CR-6 1.0, small effects are signed, for a seller's own price.* The clause speaks of "a chain that moves a price in a wider market". The national list price (C-15) is set by each manufacturer, not cleared by CR-6; the small-effects rule was applied to it by analogy (CR-4 for the direction, CR-6 1.0 for signing what may be tiny). The clause could say it covers any small effect, whatever rule gives its direction.
3. *CR-8 1.0, receipts into the same budget.* The premium tax goes into the general fund, which appropriates the Fund, and the fines go into the Fund itself. The clause was read as covering both (one budget for the purpose of the net). Whether a receipt to the general fund counts as "the budget that funds the policy" when the policy has its own fund is not said. *1.1:* CR-8's note on a dedicated fund asks the analyst to state the choice; it is stated (C-37).
4. *CR-12e and a response that does not erode its own cause.* For the plans' outlay (C-2), the plans' steering responds to the share of the rate they bear per dollar, which steering does not undo, so the response argument does not apply and a dominance premise was needed (A-24). The 0.8 note (a response driven partly by a second change) came closest; the case is a response to the same change that leaves its cause intact. A sentence on this would help.

**(c) Missing or wrong patterns.**
1. *PC-1 and PC-16 when the cap falls on the buyer's payment.* If K1 is recorded as a `price_ceiling` (the money price paid at the counter is held below its level without the Act), PC-1's If clause is met and gives `excess_demand`, and PC-16 says the seller receives less "by the same amount" as the buyer pays less. Both conclusions are wrong here: the pharmacy receives the same (C-4) or more (C-6), no shortage forms, and the transfer comes from plans and a fund. Applied as written, the patterns would have given signs that had to be overridden; they were not applied. Their If clauses should require that the cap bind on the price the seller receives, and a pattern is needed for a cap on the buyer's share with the balance paid by a third party (candidate below). *1.1:* closed by PC-1's and PC-16's 1.1 clauses and PC-20.
2. *SB-2 where the subsidy is "the seller's price less $35, up to a limit".* SB-2 gives `price_paid` down and/or `net_price_received` up. It does not say that when the subsidy pays the seller's own price, the seller's price rises toward the payer's limit (C-6). This was derived from CR-4; a clause would help. *1.1:* closed by SB-2's 1.1 clause and CR-6's 1.1 note.
3. *PC-19 and SB-8.* SB-8 applied as written to patients who would have bought anyway (C-39). *1.1:* the comparison showed it overclaimed where the saving returns through the deductible and recipients pay part of the funding; SB-8's 1.1 clause now nets these (C-39, C-43).
4. *RS-21* was considered for the move to pens (C-10) and not used: its If clause needs a cap or right counted in physical units. *1.1:* its clause on charges counted in units now covers the payment per 30-day supply, and it is cited.
5. *RS-4* worked as written for the mail-order boundary; under U1-a the boundary gave no patient-side reason to move, and the only chains were total demand and a cost fixed per pharmacy for serving the state (C-25), which RS-4 does not prompt. *1.1:* RS-4's clause on two chains now asks for the net under CR-12b. RS-4 also did not prompt the Lisvane retail fills of border residents (C-47), found by the re-run.

**(d) Vocabulary.**
