# WE-050 Ban on sales of new combustion-engine cars from 2035

Analyst: draft (Handbook 0.9 held-out test, corrected under 1.0)  |  Rule catalog version: 1.0  |  Status: awaiting expert review

A stylized policy in a fictional country. It resembles the end-of-sale dates for combustion cars that several countries have announced, but it is not modeled on any one of them. It is part of a held-out test of catalog 0.9: the 0.9 changes were drawn from WE-036 to WE-045, and this case asks whether they work, as written, on a case they were not drawn from. It probes EN-14 (a standard on new units, with the stock exempt), TS-3's clause on product standards (where the counterfactual trend moves toward the standard anyway), RS-8's 0.9 clause on opposing timing chains (a rush by buyers before the deadline against sellers who withdraw models and pre-register stock), CR-7's 0.9 clause on persistence following the stock and CR-10b (the value of used combustion cars), CR-12's 0.9 clause on what the response argument gives (imports of used cars as a partial response), RS-16's 0.9 clause on costs attached to the substitute (a road charge on electric cars), and CR-6's 0.9 clause on splitting a shift between price and quantity. The unclear provision is whether a used car imported from abroad after 2035 counts as "new". Effects on emissions, air quality, health and safety are outside the scope of version 1. The probabilities are illustrative; the evidence column says what a real analysis would cite. It has since been compared with a blind core-only re-run and corrected under catalog 1.0; the corrections are listed before "Gaps found", and the record of the 0.9 test in Gaps found (e) is kept as written.

## 1. Policy record

**Vessmark Clean Cars Act.** Vessmark is a country of about 9 million people with about 4.8 million registered passenger cars, of average age 10 years. Cars are scrapped at about 17 years on average, about 260,000 a year. Vessmark has no car assembly: every new car is imported by about 12 brand importers and sold through about 600 dealers. Vessmark buys about 0.4% of the world's new cars.

In 2025 about 280,000 new cars were sold: 196,000 (70%) with combustion engines only (petrol, diesel, conventional hybrids), 28,000 (10%) plug-in hybrids (PHEVs) and 56,000 (20%) battery-electric cars (BEVs). A typical new combustion compact costs about $30,000 and a comparable BEV about $36,000; the importers' association forecasts the gap at about $2,000 by 2035. Its forecast without new rules is that BEVs reach about 50% of new sales by 2035 and about 80% by 2045, the rest combustion and PHEVs. A five-year-old combustion compact sells for about $15,000.

About 650,000 used cars change hands a year. About 25,000 of them are imported used from Drevland, the larger neighbor to the south (about 3.5 million used-car sales a year), which has no ban and no announced date. Used cars are already imported at import parity: Vessmark's used prices for comparable cars sit about $1,800 above Drevland's, the cost of transport, the state's import roadworthiness inspection ($900) and registration. The inspection is done only at state test centers, whose capacity is about 40,000 cars a year.

Vessmark has about 2,100 fuel stations and 9,000 public chargers. Fuel excise is $0.70 a liter, about $600 a year for an average combustion car, and raises about $2.6 billion a year for the general budget. About 3,500 independent repair shops employ about 14,000 mechanics. Existing rules, in force on both paths: the fuel excise; the import inspection; a resident may drive a foreign-registered car in Vessmark for at most six months.

The bill was introduced on 3 March 2026, passed on 18 June 2026 by a large cross-party majority, and enacted on 30 June 2026. It has no expiry. Section 9 requires the Minister to report on "the readiness of the charging network" by 30 June 2031 and allows the Minister, by order, to postpone the dates in sections 3 and 4 by up to three years.

Unclear provision:
- **U1, "not previously been registered" (s. 2).** The Act does not say registered where. Reading U1-a: previously registered in Vessmark. A car first registered in Vessmark after 2035 is "new" whatever its age, so used combustion cars imported from abroad can no longer be registered. Reading U1-b: previously registered anywhere. A used combustion car registered in Drevland can still be imported and registered after 2035, and so can a new one registered in Drevland first. The Registry has issued no rule and no court has ruled. Readings get no probability; where a sign depends on one, each reading's sign is reported.

**Figures.** On the forecast trend (A-1), counterfactual new combustion sales (excluding PHEVs) fall from about 196,000 in 2025 to about 110,000 in 2035 and about 40,000 in 2045; PHEVs stay near 25,000 a year. With the Act, combustion sales are zero from 2035 and PHEV sales zero from 2038. The combustion fleet (including PHEVs) is about 4.4 million in 2026. With the Act it falls to about 3.9 million in 2035, 2.9 million in 2040 and 1.7 million in 2045; on the counterfactual path it is about 3.9, 3.4 and 2.8 million. Most cars in the fleet in 2026 are scrapped by about 2043. Under U1-b, imports of used and nearly-new combustion cars can grow by at most about 15,000 a year above today's 25,000 until inspection capacity grows, against about 110,000 counterfactual buyers of new combustion cars a year in 2035.

The counterfactual is Vessmark without the Act: no end-of-sale date, no road charge, the BEV share rising on the forecast trend.

A ban on combustion engines in new cars is classed as a `product_standard` rather than a `prohibition`: the good (a car) stays legal, existing units stay legal and tradable, and what is forbidden is new units below a specification (here a yes-or-no one: no combustion engine). That is EN-14's situation.

C-1 and C-2 are the Act's purpose. The two dated steps tighten faster than the counterfactual trend toward BEVs (TS-10): in each window they bind only on the gap from the trend. **Where would-be buyers of new combustion cars go (C-5 to C-8, C-38).** The Act removes their chosen option (CR-3a). By CR-4 they take their next-best: a new BEV, a PHEV until 2037 (C-8; A-25), a used combustion car (C-20), a used BEV (C-23, C-47), or keeping their present car longer (C-15, C-16). Those who buy a BEV pay more for it than they would have paid for the combustion car they would have bought (A-23): EN-14's "new units cost more". That is a change in the good bought, not a change in the price of a given good (RS-16, 1.0: prices are signed per unit of a given quality), so it is not recorded as `price_paid`; 2.4 has no variable for outlay on the car bought, and these buyers are recorded as worse off by their own ranking (CR-3a). Whether they spend more in total, given lower running costs, is not signed either (CR-3, note). New BEVs registered rise (C-5): Vessmark is about 0.4% of the world market and importers have eight years' notice, so the shift in demand is met by quantity (CR-6, 0.9: the split needs a premise on supply, A-5). The price of a given new BEV model is therefore `no_change` (C-38): CR-6's 1.0 clause says a small effect in a wider market is signed, not left unsigned, and on A-5 (world supply at about unchanged cost) its sign is no change; if A-5 failed it would be `increase`. The premise that supply cannot expand at once in 2035 (A-6) is below the cut-off, so no short-run price rise is signed. The road charge (K5) is an opposing chain on C-5: it makes a BEV dearer to own relative to a used combustion car, a PHEV, or keeping one's present car, so some who would have bought a BEV anyway do not (CR-4). Both chains are derived. The charge is $250 a year against about $600 of fuel excise a combustion car pays, and the ban removes the choice of about half of new-car buyers in 2035, so the ban chain dominates (A-22, empirical, p = 0.85).

**Total new cars (C-7).** Every new car a would-be combustion buyer now buys (a BEV or PHEV) was open to them without the Act, and they did not choose it then. By CR-4a no one who chose a BEV switches to buying more new cars because the combustion option was removed. But the Act makes two further changes that send buyers to new cars. Owners can sell their cars at the higher used price (C-20) and buy new (CR-3: keeping a car now costs more in forgone sale price); and under U1-a the Act also removes the option of an imported used combustion car for about 25,000 buyers a year (CR-1, operation 1), some of whom buy a new car (A-30). CR-12's 1.0 clause asks which total the response argument bounds. Cars enter the fleet as new cars or as imported used cars, so the argument bounds new cars plus imports: the responses to the removal of new combustion cars cannot raise that sum above the counterfactual, since buyers move to an entry only while it is the better option and stop once yields are equal; the constant-cost premise is A-5 (new BEVs at about unchanged cost), and the response is partial by A-4 (some rank a used car or keeping their car above a new BEV or PHEV). Under U1-b imports rise (C-13), so new cars alone fall: `decrease`. Under U1-a imports fall by about 25,000 a year, and the bound lets new cars exceed their counterfactual level by up to that amount; the (+) chain from import buyers (A-30) against the (−) chain from the removed combustion option has no dominance premise: `ambiguous` (CR-12b).

**The rush before the deadline, and what opposes it (C-9 to C-11).** RS-8: a buyer who wants a new combustion car and would have bought in 2035 or 2036 can buy in 2033 or 2034 (A-7): purchases rise in the last window before the deadline. Two chains push the other way in the same window, and RS-8's 0.9 clause says to combine all of them. (i) Importers bear a fixed cost per model offered in Vessmark (certification, marketing, parts stocks), recovered over the years the model sells. From 2031 the remaining window is short, so some combustion models are not launched or are withdrawn early (C-10; CR-4, 0.7; A-8); some buyers of those models buy something that is not a new combustion car. (ii) Dealers may register unsold combustion cars before 1 January 2035 so that they remain sellable afterwards, as used cars (C-11; A-9). Sellers thus move earlier and buyers can move later: some buyers who would have bought new in late 2034 wait for pre-registered stock, which dealers must clear in 2035 below the new price (A-29). So for final buyers' purchases in 2033 to 2034 one chain is (+) and two are (−), each with p ≥ 0.5: `ambiguous` (C-9; CR-12b). Registered-but-unsold stock at the deadline exists only under the Act: `increase` (C-11; CR-12, 0.9). That stock, which on the counterfactual path would have been sold as new in 2035, can now be sold only as pre-registered, below the new price (A-29): its value to the dealers who hold it falls (C-41; CR-7, 0.9: inventories are durable assets; the loss settles on the stock that cannot move, CR-7b). What its buyers pay is not signed as a price: a pre-registered car is a different good from a new one (RS-16, 1.0). In 2035 and after, new combustion sales are zero (C-1), so the rush is not netted against a dip: the later window is C-1.

**The earlier windows (C-39, C-40).** TS-10: the whole schedule is capitalized when it becomes expected, so choices change from 2026, not only near the date. New combustion purchases in 2026 to 2032 (C-39) meet three chains. (i) The road charge, expected from 2035, lowers the yield of a BEV bought now, since it will be owned after 2035 (CR-10a, CR-4): (+) on combustion purchases. (ii) A combustion car bought now carries the scarcity value of the stock from 2035 (CR-7c), but it stays in use past 2040, where thinning of fuel and repair opposes that value (A-10; C-22). A-11 covers cars in the fleet at enactment, not cars bought later and kept longer, so this chain is itself `ambiguous`: kind 3 (CR-12, 0.8). (iii) From 2031, models are withdrawn (C-10; A-8): (−). A kind 3 chain makes the combined sign `ambiguous` unless a dominance premise covers both its directions, and none does: `ambiguous` (C-39). New BEV purchases in 2026 to 2034 (C-40): the expected charge lowers a BEV's yield, and a combustion car bought instead gains (−, with chain (ii) above, kind 3); displaced buyers will want used BEVs from 2035 (A-27), which raises the resale value of a BEV bought now (+, CR-7c, CR-10c): `ambiguous`.

**Used imports (C-12 to C-14).** Imports already run at import parity (A-20): Vessmark's used price for a comparable car is Drevland's plus about $1,800. When Vessmark's used combustion prices rise (C-19, C-20), imports rise (CR-7a across the border; RS-4). Before 2035 this holds under both readings, because the premium on cars that will still be in use after 2035 is capitalized from 2026 (C-12). From 2035 the readings part. Under U1-a, a used car first registered in Vessmark after the date is "new", so combustion imports stop against about 25,000 a year on the counterfactual path: `decrease`. Under U1-b they continue and rise with the price gap: `increase` (C-13). Under U1-b a further route opens: a new combustion car registered for a day in Drevland is "previously registered" and can be imported and registered as used (C-14; RS-5; A-21). Under U1-a such a car is "new" and refused: `no_change` against the counterfactual, where no one does this. These signs are each against the counterfactual, not U1-a against U1-b.

**The stock kept longer (C-15 to C-17).** EN-14: the existing units are kept in use longer (CR-10b). A combustion car's value now exceeds the cost of repairing it in more cases (A-19), so owners repair rather than scrap: repair spending rises and scrapping falls in 2035 to 2040 (C-15, C-16). Scrapping happens with and without the Act, at different times, so it is signed per window (02_ontology.md, 2.5; CR-12g). After 2040 two chains meet: the retained cars are scrapped later (+), and fewer combustion cars were bought, so fewer reach scrapping age (−): `ambiguous` (C-17). With no new combustion cars entering from 2035 and old ones kept longer, the combustion fleet is older than on the counterfactual path, where new combustion cars keep entering (C-48; CR-10b). That age mix is what carries repair demand past 2040 (C-30, A-31).

**Lobbying (C-18).** The 2031 review is a concentrated stake on both sides: dealers, fuel retailers and motoring clubs gain from postponement, BEV importers and charging operators from keeping the date (RS-7; A-15). Under CR-4's 1.0 clause, lobbying is signed against whatever contest the counterfactual path would have had. The review exists only under the Act, and without the Act no end-of-sale date would have been on the agenda in 2026 to 2031 (A-34, p = 0.6), so each side's lobbying is signed against none.

**Evasion.** Residents could keep a combustion car registered in Drevland; the six-month rule makes it an offence (A-14, p = 0.3, below the cut-off: noted, not reported).

**Used combustion cars: the stock exempt (C-19 to C-21).** EN-14: existing units now face no competition from new combustion cars, so their value rises (CR-7c). The used market is where displaced buyers go (RS-16): after 2035 the stock of combustion cars can only shrink, so the shift in demand goes mainly to price (CR-6, 0.9: the supply of the stock is fixed in the period; the split is signed on that premise). Under U1-a nothing else can add to it after the date. The rush and pre-registration (C-9, C-11) add cars before it; CR-12's 1.0 clause treats such a stock built in advance as a second chain, which can hold the price down for a while, not as part of the response the argument bounds. It is combined by A-33 (p = 0.75): the cars added in advance are a fraction of one or two years' sales, against about 110,000 displaced buyers a year for six years, so they hold the price down at most in part of 2035. Under U1-b imports respond (C-13, and grey imports, C-14, a second response of the same kind). CR-12's 1.0 clause names the total the argument bounds: used combustion cars available to Vessmark's buyers, the domestic stock plus imports. Imports cannot push Vessmark's used price below its no-Act level, since they stop once the price gap is no more than the cost of importing; that holds only if the cost per imported car does not fall as imports rise (A-32, p = 0.8: transport, inspection and registration are paid per car). To report `increase` rather than an increase or no change, the response must be partial (CR-12, 0.9). Here that follows from the case's figures: inspection capacity allows about 15,000 more imports a year against about 110,000 displaced buyers a year, and A-12 (p = 0.6) says capacity is not expanded enough by 2040 to close the gap. So used prices, per car of a given age and model (RS-16, 1.0), rise in 2035 to 2040 under both readings (C-20), by less under U1-b. Sellers receive what buyers pay (C-21; CR-8b, OC-7).

Three chains oppose the rise. (i) Complements thin (EN-14, 1.0) as the combustion fleet shrinks (fewer stations, scarcer combustion repair: C-25, C-31), lowering a combustion car's value in use (A-10, p = 0.6). The fleet with the Act is about 3.9 million in 2035 against 3.9 million counterfactual, and 2.9 against 3.4 million in 2040, so thinning is slight before 2040; A-11 (p = 0.7) states that for 2035 to 2040 the scarcity chain dominates. (ii) Owners may expect further restrictions on using combustion cars (A-13, p = 0.3): a chain the rules derive but whose premise falls below the cut-off, so the derived sign is reported and the chain recorded in section 10 (CR-12, 0.8, kind 2). (iii) The road charge makes BEVs dearer to own, which pushes buyers toward used combustion cars: same direction, (+).

**Cars held at enactment (C-19).** Their value rises from June 2026 for the yields they will bring after 2035 (CR-7c, CR-10c), part of the way at once and more as the 2031 review passes without postponement (CR-7c, probability clause; A-3). Most of these cars are scrapped by 2043, so the thinning after 2040 bears on a small tail of their yields (A-11). TS-10: the whole schedule, both dates, is capitalized when it becomes expected. SB-4 says buyers after enactment "neither gain nor lose"; that compares with the capitalized price they paid, not with the no-Act path (CR-12, 1.0: the baseline of patterns). Against the no-Act path, buyers after enactment pay the premium and recover it only if they resell while it lasts (C-20 for 2035 to 2040).

**After 2040 (C-22).** The scarcity chain continues while the stock gap lasts, and the thinning chain grows; both have premises with p ≥ 0.5, so the combined sign is `ambiguous` (CR-12b). The premium also follows the gap between the actual and counterfactual stock (CR-7, 0.9, read on this path; CR-10b): that gap grows to about 1.1 million cars in 2045 and then narrows as counterfactual combustion sales fall toward zero on the trend (TS-3, product standards: the share of designs the standard binds on shrinks). See section 8.

**Existing BEVs (C-23).** A used BEV is a substitute for a new combustion car for some displaced buyers (A-27), and the stock of used BEVs is fixed in the short run, so RS-16 gives a rise in their price. RS-16's 0.9 clause applies: the policy also attaches a cost to the substitute itself (the road charge, capitalized into BEVs' value from enactment, CR-7c). Two derived chains with p ≥ 0.5: `ambiguous` (CR-12b). The same two chains reach the price of a used BEV of a given age and model in 2035 to 2040 (C-47): displaced demand (A-27) against the charge, which lowers the yield of owning any BEV. RS-16's 1.0 clause says a cost on owners combines with the quantity bought, not with sellers' net price; that is for a substitute whose supply expands (new BEVs). The stock of used BEVs is fixed in the period, so a lower yield of owning one is capitalized into its price (CR-7c): `ambiguous`.

**New BEVs: price `no_change` (C-38).** RS-16 gives a price rise for the substitute only where its supply cannot expand at unchanged cost; with A-5 (world supply expands to Vessmark at world prices) that chain fails, and A-6 (p = 0.3) is below the cut-off. CR-6's 1.0 clause requires the effect on the world price to be signed even if small; on A-5 it is no change. RS-16's 1.0 clause settles where the road charge goes: it is a cost on owners, so it combines with the quantity bought (C-5) and with owners' means (C-3), not with sellers' net price (Gaps found, c2).

**Complements (C-24 to C-31).** RS-12: fuel is used with combustion cars. Vehicle-km in combustion cars fall with the combustion fleet; older cars use somewhat more fuel per km, but not enough to offset (A-16): fuel sold falls (C-24). A station's costs are mostly fixed per outlet (CR-4, 0.8), so as volume falls some close (C-25; A-17), drivers of combustion cars travel further to refuel (C-31), and staff or hours fall (C-27; A-18). The value of station businesses falls from enactment, as the lower expected volume is capitalized (C-26). Charging sales rise with BEVs (C-28). Repairs: the cars missing from the combustion fleet in 2035 to 2040 are new ones, which need few repairs, while the retained cars are old ones, which need many (A-26): combustion repairs rise in that window (C-29), with A-26 dominating the (−) chain from fewer cars. After 2040 both chains have weight. The (+) chain continues because the fleet stays older than on the counterfactual path (C-48), and older cars need more repair a year; that needs A-26 extended past 2040 (A-31, p = 0.7). With it the sign is `ambiguous` (C-30). Mechanics' labor follows repair work (CR-9): `employment` and `hours_worked` as a group rise in 2035 to 2040 (C-44) and are `ambiguous` after 2040 (C-45). The value of repair businesses capitalizes both windows from enactment, a rise and then a fall with no dominance premise: `ambiguous` (C-46; CR-7c, CR-10c, CR-12b).

**Tax (C-32, C-33).** Less fuel sold means less fuel excise (C-32; TX-10). The road charge brings new revenue (C-4). Two items of `tax_revenue` move in opposite directions; the total is `ambiguous` (C-33; CR-12, 0.8). Where the total falls, the shortfall is made up by a group of payers (OC-1, Who pays); since the total is not signed, no such group is recorded.

**Drevland (C-42).** Under U1-b, Vessmark's added import demand (at most about 15,000 cars a year, limited by inspection) draws used combustion cars out of Drevland's market (RS-13, withdrawn supply): their price there rises. Under U1-a, Drevland sells about 25,000 fewer cars a year to Vessmark from 2035, and its price falls. Either change is under 1% of Drevland's 3.5 million sales a year, but CR-6's 1.0 clause and EN-6's 1.0 clause require a small effect in a wider market to be signed, with its size in the evidence: U1-a `decrease`, U1-b `increase` (C-42). The U1-b sign needs imports to rise, so it carries A-20.

**The inspection queue (C-43).** Inspection is a public supply at a set fee with fixed capacity (about 40,000 cars a year). CR-6's 1.0 clause applies the public-supply clause only where demand at the fee exceeds that capacity. Under U1-b, A-12 says capacity limits imports, so it does: the pressure goes to the margins open to importers sharing the slots, signed as a group (waiting, allocation of slots by criteria other than the fee): `at_least_one_increase`. Under U1-a combustion imports stop and today's 25,000 imports are below capacity, so there is no queue to lengthen or shorten: `no_change`.

Stopping points: spending by fuel station workers and mechanics, landowners of closed stations (depth 3, diffuse); electricity generation (depth 3; out of scope as an environmental question where it concerns emissions).

## 8. Time structure

Horizons for announcement effects are counted from June 2026; for effects of the ban, from 1 January 2035 (2038 for PHEVs).

- **June 2026 to June 2031: expectation and the review.** The whole schedule is capitalized when it becomes expected (TS-10): combustion cars, BEVs, fuel stations and repair businesses revalue at once, part of the way, since postponement is possible (C-19, C-23, C-26, C-46; CR-7c, probability clause). Lobbying on the review (C-18). Used imports rise with the capitalized premium (C-12). New combustion purchases in 2026 to 2032 and new BEV purchases in 2026 to 2034 are `ambiguous` per window (C-39, C-40).
- **2031 to 2034: models withdrawn.** As the remaining window shortens, importers launch fewer combustion models for Vessmark (C-10).
- **2033 to 2034: the rush and pre-registration.** Final buyers' purchases are ambiguous (C-9); dealers pre-register unsold stock in the last weeks (C-11), with its registration fees and carrying costs (C-34), and the stock is worth less than it would have been as new stock (C-41). The rush and pre-registration are a stock built in advance, which holds the used price down at most in part of 2035 (A-33). Under U1-a, importers also bring used combustion cars in before the date so that they are "previously registered" (part of C-12).
- **2035 to 2037: the PHEV window.** Some displaced buyers buy PHEVs (C-8), and purchases bunch toward the end of 2037 before the second date. Without the Act these purchases would have happened in this window and later, so the bunching inside the window is a path note (CR-12h); C-8 and C-2 sign the windows against the counterfactual.
- **2035 to 2040: the stock premium.** Used combustion prices are above the counterfactual (C-20), cars are repaired and kept (C-15, C-16, C-29), the fleet is older (C-48) and mechanics' labor rises (C-44).
- **After 2040: thinning and convergence.** Stations close and repair thins (C-25, C-31); used prices, repairs and mechanics' labor become ambiguous (C-22, C-30, C-45), the fleet staying older than on the counterfactual path (C-48, A-31). The share of new cars the ban binds on falls each year as the counterfactual trend moves toward BEVs (TS-3, product standards): about 110,000 cars in 2035, about 40,000 in 2045. Every consequence that depends on the size of the gap between the two paths (C-1, C-5, C-7, C-20) shrinks in the long run, without changing sign while A-1 holds.
- **Persistence, if the date were postponed or the Act repealed (CR-7, 0.9 and 1.0; CR-10b; TS-6).** The Act is not a repeal, so `persistence` is not recorded; but A-3 is not certain, and the 0.9 clause says what would follow. If the date were postponed in 2031, no stock would yet have changed except the models withdrawn (C-10): the capitalized premium on combustion cars would fall at once on whoever then holds them (SB-4), with nothing to persist. If the Act were repealed after 2035, the missing vintages (combustion cars not bought in the ban years) would not reappear: that part of the stock change persists. But the premium on used combustion cars depends less on that stock than on the absence of new combustion cars, which world supply can restore within months. So the premium would fall mostly at once, persisting only for young used cars of the missing vintages: partial at most, short-lived. CR-7's 1.0 clause on which stock now says this directly: the part of the value that rests on the legal rule follows the rule and changes on the date it changes; the part that rests on the stock of cars follows that stock. Fuel stations closed after 2040 would reopen only where the restored volume exceeds the cost of reopening (TS-6).

## 11. Report

**The policy.** From 1 January 2035 Vessmark will not register new cars with combustion engines; plug-in hybrids may be registered until the end of 2037. Cars registered before then may be driven, sold and re-registered. From 2035 battery-electric cars pay a road charge of $250 a year. The Minister may postpone the dates by up to three years after a review in 2031. Whether a used car imported after 2035 counts as "new" is unclear.

**Visible consequences.** No new combustion cars are sold from 2035 and no new plug-in hybrids from 2038 (C-1, C-2). More new electric cars are sold (C-5), at an unchanged price for a given model (C-38). Electric-car owners pay the road charge (C-3), which the budget receives (C-4).

**Invisible consequences.**
- *Buyers.* Those who would have bought a new combustion car lose that option; those who buy an electric car instead buy a dearer car than they would have chosen (A-23), which is a change of car, not of price. If "registered" means registered anywhere (U1-b), fewer new cars are bought in total (C-7), as some keep their old car or buy a used or imported one; if it means registered in Vessmark (U1-a), buyers of imported used cars also turn to new cars and the total is ambiguous. Plug-in hybrid sales rise until 2037 (C-8).
- *Before the deadline.* Importers offer fewer combustion models from 2031 (C-10); dealers register unsold stock before the date (C-11, C-34), and that stock is worth less to them than it would have been as new stock (C-41). Whether final buyers buy more or fewer new combustion cars in 2033 to 2034 is ambiguous: some rush, others find their model gone or wait for pre-registered stock (C-9).
- *The existing stock.* Combustion cars held in 2026 gain value from enactment (C-19). From 2035 to 2040 used combustion cars cost more (C-20, C-21), under either reading of the import rule; cars are repaired and kept longer (C-15, C-29), fewer are scrapped (C-16) and the combustion fleet is older (C-48). Used imports rise before 2035 (C-12).
- *Imports, per reading.* If "registered" means registered in Vessmark (U1-a), used combustion imports stop from 2035 (C-13) and Drevland's used prices fall slightly (C-42). If it means registered anywhere (U1-b), they rise, and new cars registered briefly in Drevland are imported as used (C-14); Drevland's used prices rise slightly (C-42), and importers wait longer or compete for inspection slots by other means (C-43), since limited inspection capacity keeps the rise partial.
- *Fuel and repair.* Fuel sales (C-24) and the fuel excise (C-32) fall; fewer stations operate in the long run (C-25), their value falls from enactment (C-26), their staff or hours fall (C-27) and drivers travel further to refuel (C-31). Charging sales rise (C-28). Mechanics' work rises in 2035 to 2040 (C-44).
- *Administration.* The Registry's new checks (C-35) come out of its other work (C-36). Lobbying over the 2031 review (C-18).

**Ambiguous effects.** Final buyers' purchases of new combustion cars in 2026 to 2032 and in 2033 to 2034 (C-39, C-9), and of new electric cars before 2035 (C-40); total new-car purchases under U1-a (C-7); used combustion prices, repair work and mechanics' labor after 2040, as scarcity and an older fleet meet a thinning fuel and repair network (C-22, C-30, C-45), and so the value of repair businesses (C-46); scrapping after 2040 (C-17); the value and the used price of electric cars, which displaced buyers want but which carry the new charge (C-23, C-47); total road-user tax revenue (C-33).

**Key conditions.** That the date holds at the 2031 review (A-3, p = 0.7); the forecast trend (A-1); that world supply of electric cars expands to Vessmark (A-5); that inspection capacity stays limited, which keeps imports a partial response under U1-b (A-12, p = 0.6), and that the cost per imported car does not fall as imports rise (A-32, p = 0.8); that thinning of the fuel network stays slight before 2040 (A-11, p = 0.7); that the stock built before the date is small (A-33, p = 0.75).

**What this analysis cannot say.** How large any effect is; whether displaced buyers spend more or less in total on cars and fuel; how much the price of new electric cars would move if world supply did not expand at unchanged cost; emissions, air quality and safety (out of scope for version 1); whether the policy is desirable.

## Gaps found

**(a) Calculus gaps (a step no core rule covers).** None. Every step was derived from a pattern or a core rule.

**(b) Unclear core rules.**
1. *CR-7, 0.9 (persistence follows the stock).* Written for repeals. Here it was needed twice outside a repeal: for the path of the used-car premium against a counterfactual trend that converges with the policy (C-22), and for the postponement or repeal scenarios the 2031 review makes possible (section 8). In the second use "the stock its value depends on" had to be read as the stock of substitutes, including new supply that can arrive at once. The premium on used combustion cars depends on the absence of new combustion cars, which world supply restores within months of a repeal, not on the missing vintages, which persist. Read literally, the clause would say the premium persists as long as the missing vintages do; that would be wrong here.
2. *CR-12, 0.9 (what the response argument gives).* Under U1-b the partial-response premise came from the case's figures (inspection capacity), as the clause allows. Under U1-a there is no import response at all, and the rise in used prices rests on CR-6's 0.9 split with a fixed stock, not on CR-12e. The clause does not say that where the response is closed off, the split premise does the work instead; it was applied that way.
3. *CR-1, 0.7 (dated operations).* The clause says "an option nobody currently chooses can be removed with no present effect on choices". Here the option removed in 2035 is chosen today by about 70% of buyers, and present choices do change (models withdrawn, rush, imports, lobbying). The clause covers this through CR-10a, but its example points the other way.

**(c) Missing or wrong patterns.**
1. *EN-14, applied as written, gave a conclusion that needed a dominance premise the pattern does not mention.* Its "existing units ... rise in value" omits the opposing chain that the shrinking stock brings on its own complements (RS-12: fuel stations close, repair thins). For cars and other durables that depend on a network, that chain is derived and has p ≥ 0.5 in the long run (A-10). With it, C-19 and C-20 hold only with A-11, and long-run used prices are `ambiguous` (C-22). EN-14 should say: where the exempt stock needs complements whose supply depends on the size of the stock, combine their thinning under CR-12b. Its "kept in use longer" also needs per-window signs for scrapping (C-16, C-17).
2. *RS-16, 0.9 (costs on the substitute), as written, names the wrong variable for this case.* It makes the cost an opposing chain on the substitute's net price to its sellers. With the substitute's supply elastic (A-5), the price chain fails (CR-6, 0.9), and the cost, an annual charge on owning the substitute rather than on its sale, is borne by buyers (CR-7b), so sellers' net price does not move. The charge opposed the substitute's quantity (C-5) instead, and the value of the existing stock of the substitute (C-23). The clause should say: combine the cost with whichever of the substitute's price or quantity the demand shift moves.
3. *TS-3 (product standards).* Worked; the binding share shrinks on the trend. It does not mention that the same convergence ends the premium on the exempt stock (EN-14), which is where the 0.9 persistence idea was needed (b1).
4. *Candidate "scheduled tightening faster than the trend"* (6.2e, from WE-031; CR-10c, CR-7c, CR-12h). Needed: two dated steps (2035 combustion, 2038 PHEVs) against a counterfactual trend toward BEVs (A-1). The derivation applied: the steps bind only on the gap between schedule and trend, the exempt stock is revalued at enactment, and bunching before the second step is a path note (CR-12h). The use is valid against the counterfactual: each window is signed against the no-Act trend (C-8 against C-2). Second use: promotes it.
5. *Candidate "fixed cost per item reduces variety"* (6.2e, WE-035). C-10 uses the same derivation (CR-4, 0.7: x: offer the model, y: drop it), but the policy adds no cost per item; it shortens the window over which an existing fixed cost is recovered. Possibly a second use in a wider form; not counted here, because the earlier invalid second use (WE-040) shows the experts should decide what counts.

**(d) Vocabulary.**
1. No variable for the age or scrapping rate of a durable stock; used `quantity_traded` (cars scrapped) and `maintenance_spending`. (1.0: `stock_age` now exists and is used in C-48; the scrapping rate still has none.)
2. No sign for "not signed" (the price of new BEVs), as found in the held-out test of 0.8. (Resolved under 1.0: CR-6's 1.0 clause requires a sign; on A-5 it is `no_change`, C-38.)
3. `persistence` exists only for repeals; there is no field for how a consequence fades as a counterfactual trend converges with the policy (C-20 to C-22).
4. No way to record a difference between readings that is only one of size (the used-car premium is smaller under U1-b, C-20).

**(e) Test of 0.9.**
- *EN-14 (promoted in 0.9).* Needed interpretation: a yes-or-no ban on a type read as a product standard; its value conclusion for the stock needed a dominance premise against complement thinning (c1), and its "kept longer" needed per-window signs.
