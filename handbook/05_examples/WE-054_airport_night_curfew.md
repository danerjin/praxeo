# WE-054 Airport night curfew with a daytime slot cap and slots allocated by history

Analyst: draft (Handbook 0.9 held-out test, corrected under 1.0)  |  Rule catalog version: 1.0  |  Status: awaiting expert review

A stylized policy at a fictional airport. It resembles real night curfews and slot regimes at congested airports but is not modeled on any one of them. It was written as a held-out test of catalog 0.9, whose changes were drawn from WE-036 to WE-045. It probes: CR-12's 0.9 rule for a Tier C chain against a Tier B chain, where night noise reaches neighbors through a `proximity` relation while travelers' outcomes run through exchange (Tier B); EN-12 (allocation of a right by history) in its branch where the base seasons run after the announcement, so that airlines race to build history; EN-13 (consolidation of tradable slots); EN-6 with its 0.9 Inputs clause (movements are an input to passenger trips sold locally and to parcel delivery sold nationally); CR-7c for home values near two airports and for the new slots; RS-8 for flights moved from the night into the shoulder hours, per window and per reading; RS-16 for the other airport; and CR-12's 0.9 clause on what the response argument gives. Noise, sleep and health are outside the scope of version 1 and have no variable; they enter only where buyers of homes capitalize them. The probabilities are illustrative; the evidence column says what a real analysis would cite. After its core-only comparison (`05_examples_core/WE-054_airport_night_curfew_core.md`) it was corrected under catalog 1.0; the changes are listed before "Gaps found", and part (e) of "Gaps found" keeps the 0.9 verdicts as first recorded.

## 1. Policy record

**Wexcombe Airport Night Curfew and Slot Order.** Wexcombe International Airport (WXI) is run by the Wexcombe Airport Authority (WAA), a public authority owned by the City of Wexcombe and two counties, which sets the airport's operating rules and charges. In the summer 2025 season WXI handled about 420 movements (a takeoff or a landing) a day: about 380 between 06:00 and 23:00, and about 40 between 23:00 and 06:00. Of the night movements, about 22 are an express parcel carrier's night sort (the "integrator"), about 6 are two other cargo carriers, and about 12 are passenger flights (8 late arrivals scheduled 23:00 to 00:30 and 4 early departures scheduled 05:15 to 05:55). On top of these, about 6 movements a night are daytime flights scheduled before 23:00 that run late and operate between 23:00 and 00:00; about 1 a night operates after midnight. The runway's declared capacity is 34 movements an hour (an existing rule, set by the WAA from air traffic control's assessment); the peaks 07:00 to 09:00 and 17:00 to 20:00 run at 30 to 34, the hour 06:00 to 07:00 at about 10 and the hour 22:00 to 23:00 at about 12. Daytime movements have grown about 3% a year and night cargo about 5%. Fourteen airlines operate in the daytime; the three largest operate about 60% of daytime movements. Aircraft average about 150 seats; airlines can move to larger aircraft only as fleets turn over (two to four years), and three of WXI's gates take wide-bodied aircraft.

WXI employs about 11,000 people on site, including about 900 (mostly part-time night shifts) in the integrator's sort; about 30% of airport workers live within 8 km. About 9,000 homes lie inside the WAA's night-noise contour (the area where night noise exceeds the WAA's threshold), with a median value of about $310,000. The WAA's charges (about $2,000 per movement on average, about $13 per seat) are set each year to recover its costs, which are mostly fixed. Its noise office, which handles residents' complaints, costs about $0.5 million a year; about 60% of its cases concern night movements. The WAA estimates the slot coordination unit and curfew monitoring at about $2.8 million a year.

Pellham Regional Airport, 70 km away by motorway (about 55 minutes), is run by a separate authority, has no curfew, and uses about 45% of its runway capacity. About 1,200 homes lie in the area that would be inside a night-noise contour at Pellham if night cargo moved there; Pellham's commuting area holds about 40,000 homes. Overnight parcel delivery is sold in a national market; Wexcombe's region is about 4% of it.

The WAA board published the draft order, including the base seasons, on 2 March 2026, after a residents' campaign for a curfew that had run since 2023. It adopted the order on 15 September 2026. Slots are issued on 1 September 2027; the curfew and the cap take effect with the winter season on 31 October 2027. No expiry; the WAA will review the cap in 2032.

Existing rules: the hourly limit of 34 movements; the WAA's cost-recovery charge method. Both stay in the counterfactual.

Unclear provision:
- U1, late-running flights. K1 says: "No aircraft shall take off or land between 23:00 and 06:00, save that a flight scheduled to operate before 23:00 and delayed by circumstances beyond the operator's control may be permitted to operate by the duty manager." The WAA's explanatory note says late-running flights "may operate until midnight"; the order itself names no time and no criteria. Reading U1-a: a flight scheduled before 23:00 and delayed may land or take off until 00:00, as of right. Reading U1-b: there is no dispensation for ordinary delays; after 23:00 only emergencies operate, and a late flight diverts (in practice to Pellham) or is cancelled.

Readings get no probability; where a sign depends on one, each reading's sign is reported.

**Figures.** Without the order, daytime movements would be about 409 a day by winter 2027/28 and about 440 by 2030 (3% a year; A-1); the cap holds them at 380. The curfew removes about 40 scheduled night movements a night and, under U1-b, about 6 late-running ones as well. At an illustrative price of $2 million for a slot (one daily movement for every day of the year), the 380 slots are worth about $760 million. A charge of $2,000 per movement spread over about 13% fewer movements than the counterfactual by 2030 rises by about $300 per movement, about $2 per seat. The WAA's slot unit and monitoring ($2.8 million a year) are more than five times its whole noise office ($0.5 million).

The counterfactual is WXI without the order: no curfew, no cap, no slots, daytime and night movements growing as before, and the residents' campaign continuing.

The curfew's purpose, quieter nights near the airport, reaches neighbors through no exchange: a movement changes the situation of those who live under it. That is a `proximity` relation (02_ontology.md, 2.8), so every chain through it is Tier C pending Q20 (CR-9, 0.7). Travelers, shippers, airlines, workers and the airports deal with each other by exchange, so their chains are Tier B.

C-1 is the curfew's purpose; C-2 is the cap's. The cap is fixed at the summer 2025 number while demand grows, so it binds from the first season and more each year (TS-3, a parameter fixed in physical units). C-1 holds under both readings of U1: under U1-a, late-running flights scheduled before 23:00 still operate until midnight, but the 40 scheduled night movements stop; under U1-b, the late-running ones stop too. The difference is one of size.

C-3 is the created asset. A slot is a claim on part of a capped quantity that airlines would pay to use (A-1), so it has a price (CR-6), and its value goes to whoever receives it at creation (CR-7c). Because the claim is earned by operating in the base seasons, it was valued from the draft of March 2026, rising toward adoption (CR-7c, probability clause). Unlike a history fixed before the announcement, this history could still be built: section 5 (C-5, C-6) records the race, and by CR-7c's 0.7 clarification part of the slots' value is used up in it. C-3 is the value of the slots, not airlines' net position (C-36).

**What rations the slots (C-4).** The cap fixes the quantity of daytime movements and leaves the price of a slot free (CR-6, a fixed quantity with a free price). Those who value a movement more than the lease price buy or lease; those who value it less lease theirs out. Against the counterfactual, where a daytime movement needed no right at all, the lease price is an `increase` (CR-12, 0.9). By CR-6's 0.8 clarification, the slot price satisfies the "at least one margin" conclusion on its own; no queue or non-price margin is signed.

**The race to build history (C-5, C-6).** The draft of March 2026 named the base seasons, summer 2026 and winter 2026/27, both after the announcement. Each airline's share of 380 slots is its share of base-season movements, so an airline that adds movements in those seasons gains slots at its rivals' expense, and one that does not loses share (EN-12, second branch; CR-10a). With runway room off-peak (about 10 movements an hour at the shoulders and spare midday capacity), airlines add movements, some of them thinly loaded, in the base seasons (C-5; A-5). The cap is fixed at the summer 2025 number, so the race raises no one's total: it changes shares, and the crew, fuel and aircraft time spent on it are used up (C-6; CR-8c; CR-7c, 0.7: part of the value is used up in competing to qualify). C-5 is signed on a flow, movements in the window, against a counterfactual of the same seasons without the order (CR-12, 0.9). In the gap between the base seasons and the cap (summer 2027) nothing is earned, and movements return toward their counterfactual path; no sign is derived for that window. That night operators moved night flights into the daytime during the base seasons, to earn daytime history, would need A-28 (p = 0.35); not reported. By RS-8's 1.0 clause on races, the race adds movements in its window without taking them from another, so no matching dip is recorded.

**Entry to qualify (C-43).** K3 issues slots to any airline with base-season movements, not only to incumbents. An airline not at WXI could start flying in summer 2026 and receive slots worth more than the losses on a year of thin flights (CR-10a; CR-4: x: stay out, y: enter now). EN-12's newcomer sentence points to this move. With A-29 (p = 0.55), entry in the base seasons is an `increase` in that window against the counterfactual (C-43). It is a different window from the lower entry under the cap (C-13), so the two are not combined (CR-12g).

**Night flights move in time and place (C-7 to C-10, RS-8).** A night operator has three options against the curfew: buy or lease a daytime slot and fly in the nearest daytime hour (the shoulders, 06:00 to 07:00 and 22:00 to 23:00), move the flight to Pellham, or stop. The integrator's night sort needs the night: a sort that ends at 05:00 cannot be done in daytime slots. So most of its operation goes to Pellham (C-7; RS-4; A-6). Passenger night flights and some cargo flights move into the shoulders, using slots bought or leased (C-10) and retimed under K4 (C-8, C-9; A-7). The same flights happen without the order, at night, so by 02_ontology.md, 2.5 the shift is signed per window against the counterfactual: night movements at WXI decrease (C-1) and shoulder movements increase.

The 22:00 to 23:00 window has a second timing chain under one reading (RS-8, 0.9: an opposing chain from a second incentive). Under U1-b, a flight scheduled to arrive at 22:40 that runs 25 minutes late cannot land at WXI; airlines move some late-evening flights earlier to keep a buffer (A-23), which lowers movements in that hour against the counterfactual. Under U1-a, a late flight may still operate until midnight, so no buffer is needed, and the hour is if anything more attractive (C-15). So the sign in the hour is `increase` under U1-a and `ambiguous` under U1-b (CR-12b; no premise ranks the night flights moving in against the late flights moving out). The 06:00 to 07:00 hour has no such chain: early arrivals before 06:00 already hold or slow down en route.

**Larger aircraft (C-11).** The constraint is counted in movements, not seats. By CR-4c with the unit of the constraint, an airline that values a slot at the margin gets more from it with more seats on it: where fleets allow (A-8), routes move to larger aircraft. This is RS-21 (high-grading under a cap counted in physical units), promoted in 1.0 with this case as one of its uses.

**Consolidation (C-12, EN-13).** Slots are tradable and airlines value them differently (A-10): network carriers with connecting traffic and large aircraft value a WXI slot more than a small airline flying a thin route. Slots move toward the first (CR-5, CR-7a). EN-13 as written in 0.9 signed fewer users against the allocation before trading; its 1.0 baseline clause asks for the comparison with no order. Against no order, the number of airlines at WXI falls through two moves. Airlines that would have started at WXI stay out, since they must now buy or lease slots (C-13; CR-4: x: stay out, y: enter). And some small airlines that value their slots below the lease price lease out or sell their whole holding and leave, some for Pellham (CR-4: x: operate, y: lease out, an option that did not exist before; A-10). Night operators who buy slots were already operating at WXI, so they add no operator. Against these, airlines that entered in the base seasons to earn slots (C-43) and keep operating would add operators; that needs A-30 (p = 0.4), below the cut-off, so C-12 is `decrease` and the chain is recorded in section 10 (CR-12, 0.8: kind 2). The 0.9 claim that the quantity per remaining user increases is not made: every holder is capped below its counterfactual growth. Lessors receive the lease price (C-34).

**The priority for freed slots (K5).** Night operators rank first for slots returned to the WAA. But with slots tradable, a holder that no longer wants a slot sells or leases it rather than let it return free (CR-4: x: let it lapse, y: sell it); a holder below 80% use leases out the unused days. So slots return mainly when a carrier fails, or when a holder ceases to operate. The priority is a free claim on a small and uncertain flow. The method signs direction, not size (CR-6, 1.0: small effects are signed): a free claim on a flow that has some chance of being positive has a positive value, captured by the night operators when it was created (CR-1, operation 4; CR-7c, CR-10c), and by CR-3b it cannot make them worse off. With A-31 (p = 0.75: over a decade, some of fourteen airlines cease or under-use), the claim's value is an `increase` (C-44). Its size is small for the reason just given. Against it, night operators lose the night (and the integrator its hall, C-18), so the value of their WXI businesses as a whole is `ambiguous` (C-45; CR-12b, with each item recorded, CR-12 0.8). EN-12's clause on a share reserved for newcomers does not fit a priority on a residual flow (Gaps found, c).

**Entry, breach, scheduling (C-13 to C-15).** Would-be entrants must buy slots, where before they needed only gates and staff (C-13). The curfew penalty is $50,000; an operator whose aircraft would otherwise be stranded at the wrong airport, or whose cargo cannot wait, sometimes pays it (C-14; A-14). Both readings give C-14: under U1-a breach is a movement after midnight or an unscheduled one after 23:00, under U1-b any movement after 23:00 without an emergency. Under U1-a, a flight scheduled at 22:55 that usually operates at 23:20 is protected while a flight scheduled at 23:20 is banned: airlines reshape late flights into the protected form (C-15; RS-5; A-24). Under U1-b the nominal schedule protects nothing, so CR-4 gives no reason for it: `no_change`.

**Lobbying (C-16, C-17, C-46).** Slot holders now gain from scarcity and have a stake in keeping the cap at 380 (EN-5's move, applied to holders of a tradable right); would-be entrants press for a larger new-entrant share (A-15). Neither lobbied on slots without the order, since slots do not exist there: C-16 is `increase`. The residents' associations did lobby on the question: their campaign for a curfew had run since 2023 and, on the counterfactual path, would have continued. By RS-7's Unless clause (which 1.0 extends to impositions, CR-4 1.0) their lobbying is compared with that campaign: under the order they defend it and press for U1-b; without it they would press for a curfew. No premise says which is more, so C-17 is `ambiguous`. The same clause reaches the other side of that contest. Night operators have a concentrated stake in exemptions and in reading U1-a, but without the order they would have opposed the residents' campaign (A-32, p = 0.75). Their lobbying is therefore compared with that opposition, and no premise says which is more: C-46 is `ambiguous`. The 0.9 version signed them `increase` with the slot holders (C-16), which compared with no lobbying rather than with the counterfactual contest.

**The integrator's facility (C-18).** The sort facility at WXI has little use other than a night sort (A-16); from the draft its expected yield falls, and the loss falls on the integrator as holder when the change became expected (CR-7c, CR-10c).

**Fares and trips: what the response argument gives (C-19, C-20).** WXI's passenger trips are sold locally; Pellham is a partial substitute 70 km away. So EN-6's main clause applies to them: with fewer movements than demand would call for, fares rise (CR-6). Two responses push back: larger aircraft (C-11) and travelers moving to Pellham (C-23). Both are responses to the scarcity the cap creates; by CR-12e's response argument they cannot take fares back below their counterfactual level, since they stop once the scarcity premium they respond to is gone. CR-12's 1.0 clause treats a second response to the same change as a second chain to combine; here both respond to the same premium and both stop when it is gone, so the argument is applied to the two together. The total it bounds is the seats that serve the counterfactual demand for trips from Wexcombe: seats at WXI (on larger aircraft) and seats at Pellham taken by WXI's travelers together, not WXI seats alone. That gives "increase or no change". CR-12's 0.9 clause asks for more to report `increase`: the response must be partial. A-9 supplies it: fleets change over two to four years, WXI has three wide-body gates, and Pellham is 55 minutes away, so the responses cannot carry all of the counterfactual growth at the old cost. CR-12's 1.0 clause asks for one more premise: that the cost per seat at WXI does not fall as movements are held below their path, since otherwise equal yields could return at a fare below the old one. A-35 supplies it: the WAA's charge per movement rises (C-32), and larger aircraft lower cost per seat only as far as the premium pays for fleet changes and lost frequency. With A-9 and A-35, C-19 is `increase`; the same argument makes passenger trips through WXI a `decrease` (C-20): larger aircraft add seats per movement but cannot take trips past their counterfactual number. Travelers who keep flying from WXI pay the higher fares on the trips they keep, so their means for other uses fall (C-49; CR-3 note, CR-8b); those who fly less or switch are worse off by their own ranking, with no money sign.

**Parcel delivery and EN-6's Inputs clause.** Movements are also an input to overnight parcel delivery, which is sold nationally. EN-6's 0.9 Inputs clause fits this output: the right's price rations the input (here the curfew prohibits the night input outright, and the day input is rationed by C-4), and the output's price moves in the wider market only by as much as Wexcombe matters there. Wexcombe is 4% of the national market and the integrator keeps serving it from Pellham, so the national price effect is small. The 0.9 version noted it as negligible; under CR-6's 1.0 clause and EN-6's 1.0 clause a small effect is signed, with its size in the evidence column. The integrator chose WXI's night when Pellham was open to it (CR-2), and serving Wexcombe from Pellham adds trucking and a later sort window, so the supply of overnight delivery at the old price is lower (A-18, restated, p = 0.7): the national price rises by a small amount (C-51). The local effect is a change in service, not price: with the sort 70 km away, collection cut-offs in Wexcombe move earlier (C-31; A-17), and parcels are trucked between Wexcombe and Pellham (C-48; CR-9, haulage an input to the moved sort).

**Airlines' net price per seat (C-21).** EN-2 gives holders of the scarce permission a higher net price. Against it, the WAA's charges per movement rise (C-32): its costs are mostly fixed and are spread over fewer movements than without the cap (PC-15; A-13). EN-2's 1.0 clause on cost recovery now names this opposing chain, and CR-9's 1.0 clause puts it at Tier B: the charge is a price, so the link between users runs through exchange, not through a `shared_facility` relation. The two chains reach the same key with opposite signs. The figures give a dominance premise (CR-12e, arithmetic with an empirical comparison): the rise in charges is about $2 per seat by 2030, against a scarcity premium per seat that at capped airports is a multiple of that (A-26, p = 0.75). So C-21 is `increase`. Without A-26 it would be `ambiguous`.

**Travelers' outcomes are Tier B.** Fares, trips, travel time and the move to Pellham (C-19 to C-23) run through exchange: airlines sell seats, travelers buy them. No `proximity` chain reaches these keys. A traveler who also lives under the flight path is a `neighbors.wxi` member for C-27 and C-28, a different key. So CR-12's 0.9 rule for a Tier C chain against a Tier B chain is not engaged for travelers, and their consequences carry no "not counting" qualifier. A flight moved into the shoulders is a change of time, not a longer trip, so C-22 counts only night travelers who switch to Pellham (A-6, A-11) and, under U1-b, late flights diverted there; it is `increase` under both readings, but under U1-a it rests on the switches to Pellham alone.

**Home values near WXI (C-27, C-28): Tier C against Tier B.** Two kinds of chain reach the value of homes in WXI's night contour.
- *Tier B, through exchange.* Fewer jobs at WXI than without the order (C-25: the night sort leaves, growth is capped) means fewer airport workers seeking homes nearby, linked by a `complement` relation noted `location` (02_ontology.md, 2.8; A-12). A home's price falls with the demand for it (CR-6), and the change is capitalized when it becomes expected, from March 2026 (CR-7c, CR-10c). Sign: decrease.
- *Tier C, through proximity.* Buyers of homes under the flight path value quieter nights (A-22), so the end of about 40 night movements raises what they will pay (+). But the same order moves flights into the shoulder hours over the same homes (C-8, C-9), which buyers value negatively (−). The proximity chain is therefore itself a chain whose parts push in opposite directions: by CR-12's 0.9 clause on kind 3, it can push either way.

Under CR-12's 0.9 rule, two consequences are recorded: the Tier B sign, `decrease`, qualified "not counting effects through proximity" (C-27), and the combined sign at Tier C (C-28). The combined sign is `ambiguous` twice over: the proximity chain is kind 3, and even if it were a plain increase no premise in the case says it outweighs the job chain. The Tier C chain does not lower C-27's tier, and C-28 stays at Tier C.

**Home values near Pellham (C-29, C-30).** The mirror case. The sort and its 900 jobs arrive at Pellham (C-26), raising demand for homes in its commuting area (Tier B, increase, C-29; A-12). Night cargo begins over 1,200 homes that had none (Tier C, decrease; A-22); here the proximity chain has one direction only, since nothing leaves Pellham's nights. The record supplies a dominance premise for the homes in the contour: hub workers spread their housing demand over about 40,000 homes in a 30 km commuting area, while the night-noise discount falls on the 1,200 under the new path (A-20, p = 0.6). So the combined sign at Tier C is `decrease` (C-30), and the Tier B sign, `increase`, is recorded beside it, qualified (C-29).

**Workers (C-25, C-26).** At WXI the night sort's part-time night shifts go and growth is capped, so employment or hours fall (RS-6, as a group). At Pellham the sort is an activity new to the place, so OC-5's 0.9 clause gives `employment` increase alone there.

**Pellham (C-23, C-24; RS-16).** Travelers and cargo move to Pellham, so its movements and trips rise. Signing the quantity of trips alone, rather than price and quantity as a group, needs a premise about the supply side (CR-6, 0.9: splitting a shift): A-34 (p = 0.85) states that flights at Pellham can be added in the period, since its runway is 45% used and aircraft can be moved. RS-16 asks whether Pellham's fares rise: only where flights there cannot be added at unchanged cost. Airlines can move aircraft to Pellham, whose runway is 45% used; A-19 (p = 0.35) is below the cut-off, so Pellham's fares are not reported. Pellham's own charges are set by its authority; more movements spread its fixed costs further (PC-15's mirror), but night operations need night staff and air traffic control, which raise its costs. No sign is recorded.

**Money between airlines (C-34, C-35).** Slot leases and sales are receipts to their sellers and outlays to their buyers (CR-8b). Night operators and entrants who buy or lease slots keep paying for something that cost them nothing before, so their means for other uses fall (CR-3 note).

**Off-peak fares in the base seasons (C-47).** The race flights (C-5) add seats to WXI's off-peak market in summer 2026 and winter 2026/27. Added supply at an unchanged price leaves sellers with empty seats, so off-peak fares fall in that window (RS-13, CR-9, CR-6; A-5). The aircraft and crews come partly from other routes, whose output falls (RS-13, 1.0); that is a different market and is not combined with WXI's off-peak fares.

**Airlines' WXI operations as a whole (C-36).** Against an airport without the order, a holder has free slots of value (C-3) and a scarcity premium (C-21), and against these the loss of the option to grow at WXI, the race costs (C-6) and higher charges (C-32). No premise ranks them: `ambiguous`. C-3 is recorded as its own item (CR-12, 0.8).

**Concessions (C-33).** Parking, shops and car hire are complements in use of trips through WXI; with fewer trips (C-20), they sell less (RS-12; A-21).

Stopping points: Pellham-area shops and services, hub workers' other spending, aircraft lessors and manufacturers (larger aircraft), routes that lose aircraft to the race: depth 3 or 4, diffuse; stopped. Noise, sleep and health: out of scope.

## 8. Time structure

- **March 2026: the draft.** Expected changes are capitalized from the draft, rising toward adoption in September 2026 (CR-7c, probability clause): the claim to slots (C-3), night operators' claim to freed slots (C-44), the integrator's facility (C-18), and homes near both airports (C-27 to C-30), at Tier B through jobs and Tier C through noise.
- **Summer 2026 and winter 2026/27: the race.** Base-season history sets each airline's share, so daytime movements rise in these seasons against the counterfactual (C-5), some airlines start at WXI to qualify (C-43), off-peak fares fall (C-47), and the resources spent on thin flights are used up (C-6). No matching dip follows within the policy's own windows: after March 2027 the incentive ends, and from October 2027 the cap governs (C-2). This is a rise in the window and a separate fall under the cap, each signed against the counterfactual (CR-12g), not a shift of the same movements from one window to another.
- **From 31 October 2027: curfew and cap.** Night movements stop (C-1); night flights move to the shoulders (C-8, C-9) and the sort to Pellham (C-7), whose new contour begins to hear it. Slot leasing and trading begin (C-4, C-10). The cap binds by about 7% in the first season against the counterfactual and more each year (TS-3, C-2), so fares, trips and the slot price move further apart from their counterfactual paths over time (C-19, C-20, C-4).
- **Two to four years: fleets.** Larger aircraft arrive as fleets turn over (C-11); A-9 says this is too slow and too limited to carry the counterfactual growth. Small airlines sell or lease out and leave (C-12).
- **The 2032 review.** Holders expect slots to last (A-3); as a change in the cap becomes more or less likely, slot values move on whoever holds them then (CR-7c).
- **Per reading of U1.** Under U1-a, late-running flights keep using 23:00 to 24:00, airlines schedule into the protected hour (C-15), and the shoulder 22:00 to 23:00 rises (C-9). Under U1-b, late flights divert to Pellham, and the hour before 23:00 has two opposing timing chains (C-9).

## 11. Report

**The policy.** From 31 October 2027 no aircraft may take off or land at Wexcombe International between 23:00 and 06:00, and daytime movements are capped at 380 a day, the 2025 number. The 380 daytime slots are given free to airlines in proportion to the flights they operated in summer 2026 and winter 2026/27, seasons that began after the plan was published; slots can be sold, leased and moved to other daytime hours; slots returned to the authority go first to night operators. Whether a late-running flight scheduled before 23:00 may still operate until midnight is unclear.

**Visible consequences.** No more scheduled night flights at WXI (C-1); fewer daytime flights than the airport would otherwise have had (C-2); slots of substantial value for incumbent airlines (C-3).

**Invisible consequences.**
- *Airlines and slots.* A price for slots appears (C-4). Because the base seasons came after the announcement, airlines raced to build history, flying extra, thinly loaded flights in 2026 and 2027 (C-5) and using up crew, fuel and aircraft time (C-6); some airlines started at WXI just to earn slots (C-43), and off-peak fares were lower in those seasons (C-47). Slots then consolidate in the airlines that value them most. Fewer airlines operate at WXI than without the order, because new airlines start less often (C-13) and some small airlines lease out or sell all their slots and leave (C-12); lessors receive lease income (C-34). Airlines move to larger aircraft (C-11). The slots' value is not airlines' net gain: counting lost growth, race costs and higher charges, their position may be better or worse (C-36). The priority for night operators is a claim on a small residual flow of slots; it has a positive value (C-44), but whether night operators' WXI businesses gain or lose overall is open (C-45).
- *Night operations.* The integrator moves its night sort to Pellham (C-7), its WXI facility loses value (C-18), collection times in Wexcombe move earlier (C-31), parcels are trucked between Wexcombe and Pellham (C-48), and the national price of overnight delivery rises by a small amount (C-51). Other night flights buy or lease daytime slots (C-10, C-35) and fly in the shoulder hours: more flights 06:00 to 07:00 (C-8), and 22:00 to 23:00 under the "until midnight" reading (C-9). Some services stop (C-41). Some operators break the curfew and pay penalties (C-14, C-42, C-50).
- *Travelers.* Fares at WXI are higher (C-19) and trips fewer (C-20) than without the order; travelers who keep flying have less for other things (C-49); airlines' net price per seat rises (C-21). Night travelers who switch to Pellham, and under the "no dispensation" reading late flights diverted there, travel longer (C-22); more travelers use Pellham (C-23, C-24). Airport concessions sell less (C-33), and the authority's charge per movement rises (C-32).
- *Workers and homes.* Fewer jobs or hours at WXI (C-25), more at Pellham (C-26). Not counting noise, homes near WXI lose value with the jobs (C-27), and homes near Pellham gain (C-29). Counting noise (Tier C), home values near WXI may rise or fall (C-28); near Pellham's new night path they fall (C-30).
- *Costs.* The authority's slot unit and monitoring (C-37, C-39), net of fewer night complaints (C-38, Tier C), only slightly offset by penalties (C-52); airlines' paperwork (C-40).
- *Lobbying.* Slot holders and would-be entrants lobby more (C-16). Residents and night operators were already contesting a curfew without the order, so whether each lobbies more or less is open (C-17, C-46).

**Per reading of U1.** If late flights may operate until midnight (U1-a), airlines schedule into the protected hour (C-15) and the hour before 23:00 gets busier (C-9). If not (U1-b), late flights divert to Pellham, airlines move some late flights earlier, and the hour before 23:00 may get busier or quieter (C-9).

**Ambiguous effects.** Home values near WXI counting noise (C-28): quieter nights against busier shoulders and fewer airport workers. Residents' and night operators' lobbying (C-17, C-46), against the counterfactual contest over a curfew. Airlines' overall position (C-36). Night operators' overall position (C-45). The 22:00 to 23:00 hour under U1-b (C-9).

**Key conditions.** That demand would have grown (A-1, p = 0.85); that the responses to the cap are partial (A-9, p = 0.8) and cost per seat does not fall as movements are held down (A-35, p = 0.75), which together turn "fares do not fall" into "fares rise"; that the integrator moves to Pellham (A-6, p = 0.7); that the slot market works (A-4, p = 0.75); that airport jobs matter for nearby housing (A-12, p = 0.6); and, for every claim through noise, that home buyers price quiet nights (A-22, p = 0.85).

**What this analysis cannot say.** How large any effect is; whether the curfew's gain to sleepers exceeds its cost to travelers and shippers; noise, sleep and health as such (out of scope for version 1); how much of the slots' value was used up in the race; whether the policy is desirable.

## Gaps found

**(a) Calculus gaps (a step no core rule covers).** None new. Every chain through night noise rests on the `proximity` relation, which is GAP-026-1 (closed into CR-9 0.7 as a Tier C relation; Q20 open). Every other step was derived from CR-1 to CR-12.

**(b) Unclear core rules.**
1. *CR-12 0.9, Tier C against Tier B, when the Tier C chain is itself kind 3.* At WXI the proximity chain has two parts (quieter nights, busier shoulders). The 0.9 rule says "the combined sign at Tier C (`ambiguous` unless a dominance premise covers the Tier C chain)". It does not say what a dominance premise would have to cover when the Tier C chain is itself mixed; read with CR-12's 0.9 kind 3 clause, it would have to cover the Tier B chain against both directions of the Tier C chain. Applied that way (C-28); the rule could say so. *1.0:* settled by CR-12's 1.0 clause on tiers and dominance.
2. *CR-12 0.9, the response argument with two responses.* Fares face two responses (larger aircraft, Pellham). The 0.9 clause asks that "the response" be partial; read as the responses together, since either alone might be partial while both together are not. A-9 is stated for both together. *1.0:* CR-12's 1.0 clause treats a second response as a second chain to combine and asks for the total and a constant-cost premise (A-35).
3. *CR-7c and the race.* CR-7c 0.7 says part of a right's value is used up where actors compete to qualify. Here the cap was fixed before the race, so the race raised no one's total and was pure competition for shares. The clause does not say that the whole of what is spent is then used up (CR-8c) with nothing created; read so (C-6).
4. *CR-9 with a location complement for homes.* The Tier B chain on home values runs from jobs to housing demand through a `complement` relation noted `location`. 02_ontology.md, 2.8 defines that note for "holding a good tied to a place (a controlled tenancy)" linking housing to labor markets; used here in the other direction (jobs at a place link to housing near it). It seemed to fit, but the note's definition is narrower.

**(c) Missing or wrong patterns.**
1. *EN-12, a priority on a residual flow.* EN-12's last sentence covers "a share reserved for newcomers by lottery or open eligibility". K5 reserves not a share but a priority on slots that are returned. With tradable slots, holders sell rather than return (CR-4), so the flow is small. No pattern prompts this; derived from CR-3b and CR-4. Not a sign the pattern gave wrongly, but a case it does not reach. *1.0:* the claim is now signed (C-44) with A-31; the flow's small size bears on size only (CR-6, 1.0).
2. *EN-2 under a cap with a cost-recovery charge.* EN-2 gives incumbents `net_price_received` increase. Here an existing rule (cost-recovery charges, PC-15) pushes the other way. EN-2 applied as written would have given `increase` without the opposing chain; the sign held only with a dominance premise (A-26). EN-2 could note that a charge spread over the capped quantity is an opposing chain. *1.0:* EN-2's cost-recovery clause now does.
3. *RS-8 and the race.* RS-8 says every timing move "has two sides: the action rises in the favored window and falls in the window it was taken from".
