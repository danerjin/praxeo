# WE-064 Auction of licenses in an unused radio band, with a build-out obligation and a per-bidder cap

Analyst: draft (Handbook 1.0 held-out test, corrected under 1.1)  |  Rule catalog version: 1.1  |  Status: awaiting expert review

A stylized policy in a fictional country. Several countries auction spectrum licenses with coverage obligations and caps; this one is not modeled on any of them. It is a held-out test of catalog 1.0 on a kind of policy none of the 1.0 changes were drawn from: a right created by the policy and allocated by auction rather than by history. It probes EN-12 (which should not apply, since nothing is allocated by history), CR-7c on who captures the value of a created right when the recipient pays for it, EN-13's 1.0 baseline note where the resource has no users at all without the policy, PC-17 for a mandated term attached to a right the state creates (the build-out obligation), RS-17 for a cap on holdings per bidder, OC-1 and CR-8's 1.0 notes on receipts into the budget that also pays the administration, CR-6's 1.0 note that small effects are signed (the world price of radio equipment; the interest rate), and CR-12's 1.0 notes on what the response argument needs and on the baseline of patterns. The unclear provision is whether a license may be resold before the build-out deadline. The counterfactual throughout is **Tarnesse without the Award Regulations**: the band stays unused, as the Radio Act requires for spectrum that is not licensed, and the question of whether and how to release it stays open. Health effects of radio emissions and the visual effect of towers are outside the scope of version 1. The probabilities are illustrative; the evidence column says what a real analysis would cite.

## 1. Policy record

**Tarnesse 2.9 GHz Award Regulations.** Tarnesse is a country of about 12 million people with its own currency, the mark (M). Three mobile network operators serve it: Ardel Mobile (about 42% of subscriptions), Nevra Telecom (36%) and Kostel (22%). Each holds national licenses in three existing bands (about 110, 100 and 70 MHz in total respectively), all expiring in 2035. Mobile data traffic grows about 30% a year, and busy-hour capacity in the cities is tight; without new spectrum, operators add capacity by adding cell sites in their existing bands ("densification"). Fixed broadband: Linmark Fibre passes about 25% of homes with fibre; Pellow Networks' copper (DSL) lines serve about 1.6 million households, including about 700,000 outside fibre and cable areas. There are about 11,000 tower and rooftop sites, most owned by two tower companies (Selwyn Towers and Haldmoor Sites); new towers need municipal permits that take 4 to 9 months. About 1,400 certified tower riggers do about 3,000 site installations a year. All radio equipment is imported; Tarnesse buys about 0.4% of the world's mobile radio equipment.

The 2,880 to 3,000 MHz band ("the 2.9 GHz band", 120 MHz) has been unused since a defence radar left it in 2024. Under the Radio Act, no one may transmit on spectrum without a license. The Tarnesse Communications Authority (TCA) consulted on releasing the band in March 2026; the Award Regulations were made on 1 March 2027. The auction ran in September and October 2027; licenses run from 1 January 2028 to 31 December 2042. Five bidders qualified: the three operators, Linmark Fibre (which plans fixed wireless access outside its fibre footprint) and Ombra Group (a foreign operator seeking entry). A financial investor registered interest but did not qualify by the deposit date under the TCA's provisional reading of regulation 9(3).

**Figures.** Six blocks of 20 MHz at a reserve price of M40 million each (M240 million in all). The TCA's consultant valued the blocks at M100 million to M200 million each; projected receipts are M600 million to M1.2 billion, against a general budget of about M58 billion a year and annual borrowing of about M2 billion. Covering 90% of the population with this band takes about 6,500 sites per network; covering the cities and large towns (about 65% of the population), which operators said in consultation they would cover commercially within five years, takes about 3,000. Three to four networks building to 6,500 sites each is 20,000 to 26,000 installations in five years, 4,000 to 5,000 a year, beside the existing 3,000. About 2,000 new towers are projected. The TCA's added costs: M9 million for the auction and M2 million a year for monitoring coverage, funded by an increase in its annual appropriation from the general budget.

Existing rules that stay in the counterfactual: the Radio Act (no transmission without a license); the operators' existing licenses, expiring 2035; tower permits; no regulation of mobile or broadband prices.

Unclear provision:
- **U1, resale before the coverage deadline.** Reg. 14: "A licence may be transferred, in whole or in part, with the consent of the Authority, and the obligations in regulation 9 pass with it." Reg. 9(3): "Until the coverage obligation is met, the licence is held by the licensee for the purpose of meeting it." Reading U1-a: transfers are allowed at any time with consent (reg. 14), and reg. 9(3) only states the purpose of the obligation. Reading U1-b: reg. 9(3) bars any transfer until the obligation is met or the deadline passes. The TCA has issued no guidance; its provisional reading for the deposit date is not a decision on the Regulations.

Readings get no probability; where a sign depends on U1, one sign per reading is given.

**The counterfactual law.** Without the Regulations, nobody may use the band (Radio Act). So the auction removes no one's option: every operation in K1 is an addition (CR-1, recording). Against that baseline, the losing bidders are not worse off by the band itself; they are reached only through what winners do with it (CR-9).

**K2 against PC-17's baseline.** PC-17's conclusion ("the bargaining range moves, so the other terms adjust") compares contracts with the term and the same contracts without it. Here the contract is a license that does not exist without the policy, so that comparison is with another policy (an auction without the obligation), not with the counterfactual. By CR-12's 1.0 note on the baseline of patterns, and now by PC-17's 1.1 clause ("compare with no term, not with the same contract minus the term"), PC-17's "other terms adjust" is re-signed: against no band, the obligation's cost lowers what bidders will pay, but receipts still rise from zero (C-1); its consequences against the counterfactual are what licensees build (C-3, C-5), the money that building takes from other uses (C-41), its breach (C-11), and its pull on inputs (C-25 to C-30, C-43). The obligation is a burden attached to an option that exists only under the policy, so by CR-3's 1.1 note it is no loss to licensees against the counterfactual, and it is not an opposing chain on any licensee's value (C-2, C-21). See section 7.

**K3 against RS-17's baseline.** RS-17 gives, for a threshold, staying below it, splitting into separately counted units, and a lower value of growing past it. The first is the law's own content. The second is valid against the counterfactual (C-8). The third compares with an auction without the cap: against no band, there is nothing to grow into, so it gives no consequence (CR-12, 1.0). RS-17's 1.1 baseline clause now says this directly: a line above which holding more is forbidden (a cap per bidder) is a prohibition that removes the option of holding more (CR-1), not a burden, and the responses are signed against no policy.

C-1 to C-5 are what the Regulations' supporters expect: the state is paid, operators build, customers get more capacity, and coverage reaches the towns. Against the counterfactual every one is an increase from nothing in the band, except that what city customers gain is a group (C-4): more capacity at each price leaves sellers offering better terms (CR-6), but whether the gain comes as a lower price, faster busy-hour service or more data needs a premise splitting the response (CR-6, 0.9), and the case has none. Busy-hour speed is therefore not signed alone.

**Who captures the band's value (CR-7c with CR-5 and CR-8b).** CR-7c says that the value of an asset the policy creates "is captured by whoever receives it at creation". With an auction, the recipient pays for it. CR-7's 1.1 note (a right sold at creation) and SB-4's 1.1 clause now settle the point: the price goes to the state and only the value above the price to the winner; the capture clause applies to rights given away. CR-5c places the price within the range both sides accept: no winner pays more than the value it expects (else it would have stopped bidding), and the clock rises until no more blocks are demanded than offered, so the price is set by the bids of those who drop out (CR-6). CR-8b: what the winner pays, the state receives. So the band's value is split: the price goes to the general budget (C-1), and each winner keeps the excess of its own expected value over the price (C-2 net of C-36). With five bidders each allowed two blocks, demand at the reserve is ten blocks for six, so the price rises above the reserve; the cap holds each operator's demand to two, so the price is set by the third-highest bidder's value for its blocks rather than by an operator's value for a third or fourth block. How large each share is the method cannot say. Two points follow without magnitudes: no part of the value goes to anyone by history (EN-12 does not apply), and the excess kept by winners is non-negative by their own ranking at the time of bidding (CR-5), not necessarily in money afterwards. There is no variable for the split ((d) 1).

**Prices to customers.** The auction price and the build-out cost are paid once per license, whatever the traffic carried: a cost fixed per firm, which bears on whether a firm takes and keeps a license, not on the price of each unit sold (CR-4, 0.8). The common claim that auction payments are passed on in phone bills is therefore not derived; a price effect would need a premise about entry and exit, which C-19 handles through added capacity instead.

**Entry into the auction (C-6, C-7, C-14).** Bidding is an option the counterfactual lacks; those who expect a license to be worth more than the reserve after the build-out cost bid (CR-4; A-1). Preparing to bid uses resources that winners and losers alike spend (C-7; CR-8c). Under U1-a an investor without a network can win, resell to a builder before the deadline, and keep the margin; under U1-b it must build or lose the license, which it cannot do, so it does not bid (C-14; CR-4, CR-10a; A-12). There is no race to qualify: eligibility depends on a deposit, not on any history (EN-12 does not apply; TS-5 1.0 is not needed).

**The cap (C-8, C-9, C-16).** The cap removes the option of holding a third block for any person "together with its affiliates", defined by control. Investors who want more can take minority stakes in a second bidder, or form joint ventures below the control line (C-8; RS-17, RS-5; A-5). Licensees can also pool their blocks by network-sharing agreements, which the cap does not count (C-9; A-6). Against the counterfactual, where there is no band, both are increases from none. The cap's purpose is to leave room for a bidder other than the three operators; with A-14 an entrant wins and builds, raising the number of networks selling wireless broadband (C-16). That an entrant wins depends on the cap, but the sign is against no band, not against an uncapped auction.

**Substitution between capacity sources (C-10).** Without the band, traffic growth is met by adding sites in the existing bands. With it, part of that capacity comes from the band instead, which is cheaper per unit of capacity in the cities (A-7). So densification in the existing bands is lower than it would have been (CR-4; CR-9, LW-10: spectrum and sites are substitutes as inputs to capacity). The same substitution starts before the band can be used: once the award is expected (A-28), operators hold back densification they would have done in 2026 and 2027, since a site added now would duplicate capacity the band will give (C-40; CR-10a, x: densify now, y: wait for the band). This is not a timing shift: the densification held back is replaced by the band, not done later. C-10 and C-40 are the opposing chain that every market used by densification must combine: sites (C-25 to C-27), riggers (C-28) and equipment (C-32, C-33) (section 7).

**The obligation and its breach (C-3, C-5, C-11).** K2 binds where licensees would not otherwise cover the towns with this band within five years (A-8: operators told the consultation they would cover about 65%). Against the counterfactual, all building in the band is an increase (C-3), and the towns get service in the band (C-5) because K2 requires it (A-3). Coverage is checked by drive tests and maps, so some licensees will meet it by the cheapest sites that pass the test, or overstate it, or miss it (C-11; RS-3, RS-5; A-9). That the building is bunched toward the deadline is a path note, not a consequence (section 8): without the policy no building in the band would happen at all (02_ontology.md, 2.5).

**Transfers (U1; C-12, C-13).** Under U1-a, a licensee who finds it cannot finance the build-out, or that another values the license more, sells it with the obligation (C-12; CR-5a; A-10). Under U1-b, no license changes hands before the deadline, as in the counterfactual, where there are no licenses (C-12, `no_change`); the same pressure goes to forms the regulation may not catch: selling the company that holds the license, or leasing its capacity long-term to another network (C-13; RS-5; A-11). Under U1-a no such reshaping is needed. After 31 December 2032 both readings allow transfers.

**EN-13's baseline (C-17).** EN-13 gives: rights move to those who value them most, so the number of users of the capped resource falls. Its 1.0 baseline note says that against no policy the number falls "through blocked entry and holders who sell their whole holding". Here the resource has no users without the policy, so no entry is blocked and no user is lost: the number of users of the band rises from none, under both readings (C-17). EN-13's 1.1 clause (no users without the policy) now gives this directly. Transfers under U1-a can bring it below the number of first winners; that is a comparison with the allocation, not with the counterfactual ((e)). The cap also bounds consolidation: an operator already holding two blocks cannot buy more, so EN-13's premise that the highest-value users are among the buyers may fail (EN-13, 1.0).

**Lobbying (C-15).** Whether and how to release the band was debated before the Regulations and would have stayed contested on the counterfactual path (the 2026 consultation drew submissions from all five bidders). By CR-4's 1.0 note and RS-7 (1.0), lobbying on release as such is signed against that contest, and the case gives no premise, so it is not signed. CR-4's 1.1 note (the same question) confirms the split: the contested question is the release of the band, and lobbying over provisions that exist only under the policy is an increase. The questions in C-15 exist only under the Regulations: an extension of the deadline, waivers of revocation, the reading of U1, the cap when the band is re-awarded in 2042. Lobbying on them is an increase against none (CR-12, 0.9; A-13). Town councils have a concentrated stake in the deadline being enforced; licensees in its extension.

**Price of mobile capacity (C-19).** The band adds spectrum, an input to capacity, and with A-4 it is used where busy-hour capacity is tight. Added supply at the old price leaves sellers wishing to sell more capacity than buyers take, so the price per GB at a given speed falls (RS-13, CR-6), provided the licensees compete rather than hold prices (A-15). RS-16's 1.0 note applies to how it is signed: per unit of a given quality (a GB at a given speed), so that a shift toward faster plans is not counted as a price change. Two possible opposing chains were checked. (1) The auction price and the build-out cost are costs fixed per firm and give no unit-price chain (CR-4, 0.8; section 4). (2) A smaller operator that wins little of the band may exit or merge, and fewer networks could raise the price. That chain is derivable but needs a premise the case does not support (A-16, p = 0.3), so by CR-12's 0.8 note (kind 2) the derived sign is reported and the chain is recorded in section 10. CR-12e's response argument was not used to bound the price: the incumbents' responses (less densification, C-10; a possible merger) are responses to the new capacity, but mobile networks have large costs fixed per network, so by CR-12's 1.0 note a contraction can restore equal yields at a higher price; the argument would need a constant-cost premise that does not hold here. CR-12's 1.1 note (the mirror case, an expansion bounded by the old price) does not change this: C-19 is not signed by a bound but by the added supply directly. C-19 is close to the price and quality members of the group in C-4 taken together: buyers take more data only on better terms (LW-3), so the quantity member cannot carry the whole response, and the price per GB at a given speed falls. C-19 does not use any member of C-4 as a chain (CR-12, 1.1).

**Incumbents' assets (C-20, C-21).** The existing licenses are worth what the capacity they provide is expected to earn until 2035 and at renewal. Added capacity in a substitute band lowers that yield (RS-13, CR-7c), from when the award became expected (CR-10c; A-28: partly at the March 2026 consultation, the rest when the Regulations were made). The operators' shares combine that fall and the fall from possible entry (C-16) with the gain on blocks they win at a price below their own value (A-17: the cap holds the clearing price below some operators' values for their two blocks). No premise ranks them: `ambiguous` (C-21; CR-12b). An operator that wins no block bears only the losses.

**Fixed broadband (C-22 to C-24).** Linmark's fixed wireless offers outside its fibre footprint, and the operators' "home internet" plans in the band, are substitutes for DSL (A-18). DSL lines fall (C-22; RS-16 mirror case), and since the copper network is sunk and cannot contract at once (A-19), its price per line of a given speed falls (C-23; CR-6, 0.9). Pellow's network loses value from when the award became expected (C-24).

**Sites, towers and riggers (C-25 to C-30, C-42, C-43).** The build-out raises demand for sites and installation work, more so because K2 requires building beyond the cities (A-8). The same markets also lose demand: the densification that the band replaces (C-10, and C-40 from the announcement) would have used city sites, riggers and equipment (A-7). Every sign in these markets therefore combines two chains (CR-12b), and each needs a dominance premise or is `ambiguous`.

*Riggers (C-28).* Arithmetic settles it (CR-12e). Building to the obligation takes 4,000 to 5,000 installations a year (A-3), more than the 3,000 a year done in total without the policy, and densification cannot fall below zero; so, counting the installation as the unit of rigger work, demand for riggers at the old wage rises even if densification stopped altogether. Certification takes 6 to 12 months (A-22), so the split goes to price while supply cannot expand (CR-6, 0.9): riggers' pay rises. Other buyers of installation work pay more (C-43; CR-9, CR-7a: riggers move to the higher-paying use, and others must match it to keep them), and so install less in 2028 to 2032 (C-30; CR-4c, CR-8a, RS-13 1.0). Riggers' number rises in the medium run as more are trained (C-29; A-23); by CR-6's 1.1 note labor demand moves as a group of employment and hours, and A-23 (new riggers trained or moving from other work) is the premise that signs employment.

*Sites (C-25, C-26).* Here no figure in the case compares the two chains: a band installation in the cities is often co-location on an existing site, while a densification installation needs a new site. The sign rests on a dominance premise (CR-12e, an empirical premise with a probability): the site capacity taken by the band build-out, including about 2,000 new towers and the tenancies on them, exceeds the site capacity that the densification not done would have taken (A-31, p = 0.65). Given A-31, site demand at the old lease rate rises. Sites cannot be added at an unchanged cost per site within the period: permits take 4 to 9 months and the riggers are already busy (A-20); so lease rates rise (C-25) and tower portfolios gain value when the award becomes expected (C-26; SB-4 once the yield sign is settled). New towers drawn by higher lease rates are a response within the total the response argument bounds (CR-12, 1.1: responses within the named total are part of the response): they cannot push rates back below their old level. The total bounded is all sites available to networks (existing and new together); the response is an expansion, and by CR-12's 1.1 note (the response argument in every direction) the cost premise is its mirror, that cost per site does not fall as building expands in the period (A-20: permits and riggers are scarcer, not cheaper, as more is built). The response is partial because towers take months to permit and build. With those premises the sign is `increase` (CR-12, 0.9, 1.0 and 1.1). If A-31 fails, C-25 and C-26 are `ambiguous`.

*New towers (C-27).* The obligation draws new towers outside the cities (about 2,000 projected); the densification not done would have needed new city sites, some of them new towers. Installations are not towers, so the rigger arithmetic does not carry over, and no count in the case compares the two. The two chains oppose, and no premise ranks them: `ambiguous` (CR-12b). Site tenancies and new towers are kept apart: the tenancy chain is signed through C-25 with A-31.

*After the build-out (C-42).* After 2032 the band's build-out ends, and the riggers trained for it stay in the trade (CR-10b; A-23), which pushes the wage down against the counterfactual; projects delayed by the shortage are done then (A-24), which pushes it up. No premise ranks them: `ambiguous`.

**Other projects after the deadline (C-31).** Projects delayed by the shortage of riggers are done after 2032 (A-24): a timing shift of work that happens with and without the policy, so the later window is signed against the counterfactual (02_ontology.md, 2.5), with an increase from catch-up. But fixed wireless competes with fibre outside the cities, which lowers the return on some fibre builds that would otherwise have gone ahead (A-18; CR-9). The two chains oppose: `ambiguous` (CR-12b). In the earlier window both push down (C-30).

**Equipment (C-32, C-33, C-44).** Equipment is imported; Tarnesse's orders in this band rise from none. But they are not all added orders. In the short run, operators hold back densification from the announcement (C-40) and do less of it once the band is in use (C-10), and installations are limited by the riggers available (A-22), so band orders partly replace densification orders. Tarnesse's total orders in the short run have two opposing chains, and no figure ranks them; so the world price in the short run is `ambiguous` (C-33; CR-12b). The densification chain rests on A-7 and A-28, both above the cut-off, so it is a chain of kind 2 that cannot be set aside (CR-12, 0.8). In the medium run, the number of riggers rises (C-29; A-23), so total installations, and the equipment ordered for them, exceed the counterfactual (C-32; CR-9, CR-7a). Tarnesse buys about 0.4% of the world's radio equipment, so its added orders raise the world price by a small amount, where vendors' supply cannot expand at an unchanged cost in the period (A-25). By CR-6's 1.0 note the sign is recorded even though the effect may be small (C-44); its size goes in the evidence column.

**Loans (C-34).** Licensees borrow part of what they pay and build (A-27): more demand for loans in Tarnesse's market at the old rate pushes the rate up for other borrowers (CR-8a, CR-6). The receipts let the Treasury borrow less than its M2 billion a year (A-26), which pushes the rate down (OC-8 in reverse). Each effect may be small; each is signed (CR-6, 1.0). They oppose, and no premise ranks them: `ambiguous` (C-34; CR-12b).

**The general budget (C-35).** The receipts go to the Consolidated Fund (K4). CR-8b gives that someone receives them; CR-8d and CR-8's 0.7 note on payers from a general budget, read in reverse, give the recipients as a group: taxpayers (taxes lower than otherwise), recipients of other spending (more spending), or future taxpayers (less borrowing). The TCA's added costs come out of the same budget, so OC-1 and CR-8's 1.0 note apply: the receipts (C-1) and the outlay (C-37) are two items of the budget, and the net (C-38) is a separate consequence, signed by arithmetic (M600 million or more against M9 million plus M2 million a year). The group's sign follows the net: `at_least_one_increase` (C-35). The TCA's appropriation and the Consolidated Fund are treated as one budget for netting (CR-8, 1.1: a dedicated fund): the TCA has no fund of its own, and both the receipts and its added appropriation pass through the general budget.

Stopping points. The currency link (CR-9): more equipment imports give foreigners more marks to buy Tarnesse's exports, while licensees' payments reduce their other spending, some of it on imports; no premise ranks these, and the chain is diffuse at depth 3, so it is stopped. The spending of riggers and tower owners, and of the group in C-35: diffuse, depth 3 to 4; stopped. What licensees give up to pay (C-36) and to build to the obligation (C-41) is not followed to named uses: which uses fall needs a premise (CR-4, a change in means alone). Proximity effects of new towers on neighbors (CR-9, 0.7, Tier C) and health effects: out of scope.

## 8. Time structure

- **March 2026 to March 2027: the release becomes likely.** Values move part of the way at the consultation and the rest when the Regulations are made (CR-7c, "becomes expected is a matter of probability"; A-28): incumbents' existing licenses and Pellow's copper fall (C-20, C-24), tower portfolios rise (C-26, given A-31). Operators hold back densification they would have done in the window, since the band will replace it (C-40; CR-10a); this is a decrease, not a timing shift, because the capacity comes from the band instead.
- **March to October 2027: the auction.** Bidders spend on preparation (C-7); receipts are paid (C-1). The financial investor's participation depends on U1 (C-14).
- **2028 to 2032: build-out.** Licensees build in the cities first, where the band earns most, and in the towns as the deadline nears: building is bunched toward the end of 2032. This is a path note: without the policy no building in the band would take place at all, so no window is signed as a decrease (02_ontology.md, 2.5; CR-12h). Riggers' pay and tower lease rates are highest in this window (C-25, C-28); other buyers of installation work pay more (C-43) and their projects are delayed (C-30). Equipment orders rise above the counterfactual only as riggers are added (C-32, C-44); before that, band orders partly replace densification orders (C-33). Under U1-a, licenses change hands; under U1-b, companies or capacity do (C-12, C-13). Lobbying on an extension peaks before the deadline (C-15).
- **End of 2032: the deadline.** A licensee that has not met the obligation loses its license without refund (C-11). The band then reverts to the TCA, which may re-auction it: further receipts are contingent on a revocation (A-30, p = 0.3, below the cut-off; CR-8 0.8, contingent payers, read for receipts). Transfers become possible under both readings.
- **2033 onward.** Delayed projects resume (C-31); riggers trained for the build-out stay in the trade, so their wage may be higher or lower than without the policy (C-42; CR-10b). Mobile prices per GB stay lower while the band is in use (C-19). In 2035 the operators' existing licenses expire; their renewal value is lower than it would have been (C-20).
- **Toward 2042.** With no right of renewal, licensees' investment in the band falls in the last years of the term. Against the counterfactual there is no investment in the band at all, so this is a path note, not a decrease (CR-10a).

## 11. Report

**The policy.** Tarnesse auctions six national licenses of 20 MHz each in a radio band nobody may use today. Licenses run for 15 years from 2028. Each licensee must cover 90% of the population with the band by the end of 2032, or lose the license without refund. No one may hold more than two blocks. The payments go to the general budget. It is unclear whether a license may be sold before the coverage deadline.

**Visible consequences.** The state is paid (C-1), net of the regulator's costs (C-38), and the general budget's beneficiaries gain as a group (C-35). Licensees hold valuable licenses (C-2) and build networks in the band (C-3). City customers gain from more capacity, as a lower price, faster busy-hour service or more data; which of these the method does not say (C-4). Towns get service in the band because the obligation requires it (C-5).

**Invisible consequences.**
- *Who keeps the band's value.* The price goes to the state; each winner keeps any excess of its own value over the price. No one receives any of it by history. The auction payment is a cost fixed per firm and is not passed on in the price per GB by any chain the method derives.
- *Licensees.* They pay (C-36), spend on the sites the obligation requires (C-41) and on proving coverage (C-39), hold back densification from the announcement (C-40) and use the band in place of some densification afterwards (C-10), use stakes below the affiliate line (C-8) and network sharing (C-9), and some fall short of or game the obligation (C-11).
- *Customers and rivals.* The price per GB falls (C-19). Fixed wireless takes DSL customers (C-18, C-22), and DSL prices per line fall (C-23). An entrant adds a network (C-16), and the number of users of the band rises from none (C-17).
- *Asset owners.* Incumbents' existing licenses lose value (C-20); their shares may rise or fall (C-21). Pellow's copper loses value (C-24). Tower owners gain (C-26) and charge more for sites (C-25), if the band build-out takes more site capacity than the densification it replaces (A-31, p = 0.65); whether more towers are built than without the policy is ambiguous (C-27).
- *Inputs.* Riggers are paid more (C-28) and more are employed (C-29); after 2032 their pay may be higher or lower (C-42). Other projects pay more for installation work (C-43) and install less until 2032 (C-30), and after it may install more or less (C-31). Equipment vendors sell more once riggers are added (C-32), and the world price of radio equipment then rises by a small amount (C-44); in the short run, while band orders partly replace densification orders, its direction is open (C-33). The interest rate for other borrowers may rise or fall (C-34).
- *Process.* Bidders spend on preparing (C-7); the regulator's costs rise (C-37); lobbying grows on the deadline, waivers, U1 and the cap (C-15).

**Per reading of U1.** If licenses can be sold before the deadline (U1-a), some are (C-12), and investors without networks bid to resell (C-14). If they cannot (U1-b), no license changes hands before 2033, and the same pressure goes to sales of the companies that hold them and to long leases of capacity (C-13). Against the counterfactual of no band, every other sign is the same under both readings.

**Ambiguous effects.** Incumbents' share values (C-21); new towers (C-27); other projects after 2032 (C-31); the world equipment price in the short run (C-33); other borrowers' interest rate (C-34); riggers' pay after 2032 (C-42).

**Key conditions.** That the obligation is enforced (A-3, p = 0.75); that the band adds capacity where it is needed and licensees compete (A-4, A-15); that an entrant wins and builds (A-14, p = 0.6); that sites and riggers cannot be added at once (A-20, A-22); that the band build-out takes more site capacity than the densification it replaces (A-31, p = 0.65).

**What this analysis cannot say.** How much the state receives and how much licensees keep; whether a different design (no cap, no obligation, an auction with a resale rule) would have raised more or covered more, since those are comparisons between policies; how large any price change is; whether the policy is desirable; health and visual effects (out of scope).

## Gaps found

**(a) Calculus gaps (a step no core rule covers).** None. Every step was derived from CR-1 to CR-12. Proximity effects of new towers would be the open GAP-026-1 (Tier C, Q20); they were not followed.

**(b) Unclear core rules.**
1. *CR-7c, an asset created and sold by the state.* CR-7c says the value of a created asset "is captured by whoever receives it at creation". With an auction the recipient pays, and by CR-5c and CR-8b the state captures the price and the recipient only the excess of its own value over the price. The rule is right for free allocation (EN-12) and literally wrong for a sale. It needed CR-5 and CR-8b to give the split. A sentence is needed: "where the right is sold at creation, the seller captures the price and the buyer the excess of its value over the price; an auction sets the price at the value of the bidders who drop out."
2. *CR-1, recording against a counterfactual law that forbids the act.* Because the Radio Act forbids use of the band without a license, the Regulations remove no option from anyone. CR-1's recording note ("state the counterfactual law") settled this, but a consequence of it was not obvious: losing bidders are not worse off by the band itself (CR-3b), only through what winners do with it (CR-9).
3. *CR-8 0.8, contingent payers, read for receipts.* Re-auction receipts after a revocation are contingent on a breach. The clause speaks of payers; it was read for a contingent receipt, which seems right but is not stated.
4. *CR-12 1.0, what the response argument needs, in an input market.* For site lease rates (C-25) the 1.0 note was written for a contraction ("cost per unit does not fall as the activity contracts"). Here the response is an expansion (new towers), and the needed premise is the mirror: cost per site does not fall as building expands. Worked with that reading; the note could state both directions.

**(c) Missing or wrong patterns.**
1. *PC-17, a term in a right the state creates.* Its preconditions are met (the term is required in every license; it is absent from what licensees would choose for coverage beyond 65%). Its conclusion that "the other terms adjust" compares the license with the term against the same license without it: another policy. Against no policy there is no license, so the conclusion had to be re-signed: the term's consequences are what is built, its breach and its pull on inputs, and the lower bids show only as a comparison between designs (section 7). PC-17 needs a clause: "where the contract exists only under the policy, the adjustment of other terms is a comparison between policies; against the counterfactual, sign what the term causes to be done."
2. *RS-17, a cap on holdings.* Applied as written, its third conclusion (the value of growing past the threshold falls, CR-7c) would have given a decrease in operators' value from the cap. That compares with an auction without the cap. Against no band, the cap takes nothing away. I overrode it (no consequence). Its splitting conclusion worked (C-8). RS-17 is written for a burden above a line; a cap is a prohibition above the line, which its notch and kink note does not name.
3. *EN-13 on a resource with no users without the policy.* Its main conclusion ("number of sellers using the right decreases") and its 1.0 baseline note both presume users in the counterfactual. Here the number rises from none (C-17); transfers reduce it only against the first allocation. Overrode the main conclusion as written.
4. *EN-12 has no counterpart for allocation by auction.* EN-12 covers allocation by history and, in one sentence, by lottery. A pattern for allocation by sale would say: no windfall and no race; the state captures the price; a cap or set-aside on bidders changes who wins and the clearing price; costs paid for the right are fixed per firm (CR-4, 0.8) and not passed into unit prices without a premise on entry and exit.
5. No other pattern gave a sign I had to override.

**(d) Vocabulary.**
1. No variable for the share of a created right's value captured by the state against its recipients. `subsidy_share_received` and `tax_share_borne` are for subsidies and taxes. A `rent_share_captured` (signed for one party relative to the other) would let the brief's question be scored.
2. No variable for the coverage of a network (population reached); `quality_per_unit` with a qualifier was used (C-5).
3. No actor class for the state as a receiver of non-tax payments; `treasury` was coined.
4. The instrument list has `rights_allocation` "by history, auction or lottery" but the rule families listed for it (SB-4, CR-7c, EN-12) cover only history; no pattern covers the auction case ((c) 4).
