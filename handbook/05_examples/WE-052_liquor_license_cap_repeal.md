# WE-052 Repeal of a cap on retail liquor licenses

Analyst: draft (Handbook 0.9 held-out test, corrected under 1.0)  |  Rule catalog version: 1.0  |  Status: awaiting expert review

A stylized repeal in a fictional state. Several real states cap retail liquor licenses by population, and some have debated ending the cap; this one is not modeled on any of them. It is a held-out test of catalog 0.9 on a case the 0.9 changes were not drawn from. No worked example of the original cap exists, so, as the 0.9 workflow note on repeals asks, the cap's own effects are derived first from the core rules (section 7, metamorphic check) and the repeal is checked against that table. The case probes CR-7's 0.9 clause on the persistence of an asset's value (a license's value depends on a legal count that ends at once, while the stock of stores that the count held down grows only gradually), EN-6 and EN-13 in reverse (a cap on the number of sellers, with tradable and leased licenses), CR-4's and RS-7's 0.9 clauses on lobbying in a repeal, CR-12's 0.9 clause on what the response argument gives, RS-8's timing chains in an 18-month window, CR-8 on a privilege voided without compensation, and proximity effects of more stores at Tier C under CR-12's 0.9 rule for a Tier C chain against a Tier B chain. The unclear provision is whether licenses already in force keep the right to sell on Sunday that the new licenses lack. The counterfactual throughout is **the cap stays in force**; every sign is relative to that path. `persistence` compares each consequence with the path on which the cap was never imposed (TS-6). Effects on health and safety (drinking, underage sales, drunk driving) are out of scope for version 1. The probabilities are illustrative; the evidence column says what a real analysis would cite. The example was corrected under catalog 1.0 after its core-only comparison; the changes are listed before "Gaps found".

## 1. Policy record

**Merrowind Retail Liquor Licensing Reform Act.** Merrowind is a state of about 6.0 million people in 290 towns. Since the Retail Liquor Act of 1949, section 12 has allowed one off-premises retail liquor license (a "package store" license, for wine and spirits sold for consumption elsewhere) per 2,500 residents of each town, rounded down, with at least one per town. Licenses are recounted at each census. A license may be sold or leased to another qualified person in the same town, with routine approval by the Alcohol Control Board (about 90 transfers a year). The figures:

| Item | Figure |
|---|---|
| Off-premises licenses in force | 2,380 |
| Average sale price of a license, last three years | about $400,000 (from $150,000 in small towns to $750,000 in cities) |
| Licenses leased to an operator by a holder who does not operate | about 560, at $26,000 to $34,000 a year |
| Licenses held by supermarket chains (bought at market prices) | about 140; Merrowind has 1,150 supermarkets and 2,600 convenience stores |
| Licenses pledged as collateral | about 830 (35%); banks lend up to 60% of appraised value; about $210 million outstanding with three regional banks |
| Annual renewal fee | $1,000 |
| Average sales of a package store | about $1.6 million a year; stores hold about six weeks of stock |
| Employment in package stores | about 13,500 |
| Sunday sales | Allowed since 2012 by section 12(4): "A licensee under this section may sell on Sunday between noon and 6 p.m." About 9% of package-store revenue. |
| Liquor excise | per gallon; about $310 million a year. The 6% general sales tax also applies. |

Ashcombe, the neighboring state, issues off-premises licenses to any qualified applicant for $3,000 a year, lets supermarkets sell wine and spirits, and allows Sunday sales. About 1.1 million Merrowind residents live within 30 minutes of the Ashcombe line. A basket of 50 common wine and spirits items costs on average 11% more in Merrowind than in Ashcombe, after allowing for the difference in excise. The state revenue department estimates that 7% of the wine and spirits bought by Merrowind residents is bought in Ashcombe.

Small retail units (1,500 to 4,000 sq ft) in commercial zones rent for about $26 a sq ft a year, with 5% vacancy; adding retail space outside existing commercial zones needs a rezoning that takes 12 to 24 months in most towns. The Board's licensing staff of 18 is funded by an annual appropriation of $8.5 million; license fees go to the general fund. Merrowind's supreme court held in 1987 that a liquor license is a privilege and not property, so its holder has no claim against the state when the legislature changes the licensing rules.

The grocers' association has campaigned against the cap for twenty years; ballot initiatives to end it failed in 2019 (48% yes) and 2023 (46% yes). The license holders' association opposed both.

The bill was introduced on 2 February 2027, passed on 20 May 2027 (Senate 41 to 9, House 112 to 38) and signed on 1 June 2027. It takes effect on 1 December 2028, 18 months after signing. No referendum petition was filed. It has no expiry.

Existing rules, unchanged and in force on both paths: the three-tier system (retailers buy only from 12 licensed wholesalers), the excise and sales tax, the 500-foot rule, zoning, and the rules on underage sales, which the Board enforces with compliance checks.

Unclear provisions:
- **U1, Sunday sales of licenses already in force.** Section 12, including the Sunday clause in 12(4), is repealed; K4 says old licenses "continue in force" and "are renewed under section 12A", whose hours are Monday to Saturday. Reading U1-a: licenses in force on the effective date keep the Sunday privilege granted to them under section 12(4), for as long as they are renewed; only new licenses lack it. Reading U1-b: from their first renewal under 12A (each license renews on 1 December), old licenses carry 12A's hours, so no off-premises license may sell on Sunday. The Board has not said how it will read K4, and no court has ruled. Readings get no probability; where a sign depends on one, each reading's sign is reported.

| ID | Actor class | Variable | Sign | Horizon | Rules | Assumptions |

**C-1: does the license value fall at once?** Yes, for the license as a permission. From 1 December 2028 anyone qualified can get a license for $5,000 a year, so an old license is then worth only what it conveys that a new one does not: under U1-a the Sunday privilege, under U1-b nothing (CR-7c, CR-10c). Nor can it then be sold or leased: K4 renews old licenses "under section 12A", whose licenses are "not transferable". So the U1-a remnant is a value by the holder's own ranking, the Sunday trade of the store it is tied to, not a sale value (CR-7, 0.9 on assets without a market price, and 1.0 on which stock; EN-2, 1.0). Before that date a license still carries the cap's full scarcity rent, but only for the months left. So its price falls, when the repeal becomes expected, to the value of the rent until December 2028 plus, under U1-a, the value of the Sunday privilege to a buyer who will operate the store. CR-7c's probability clause times it: part of the way from the bill's introduction in February 2027, as passage became likely, and most of it at signing. The loss falls on whoever holds the license then, including those who bought in recent years at $400,000 (SB-4). It is a decrease under both readings, smaller under U1-a.

What does **not** fall at once is the scarcity of stores. The cap held down the number of outlets; the stock of outlets grows only as entrants find premises, fit them out and hire (CR-10b, C-2). Until it does, existing stores keep earning more than a store would on the never-imposed path, but that rent no longer goes to the license, which anyone can now get. It goes to the store itself: its premises, location, fittings and customers (C-31, C-32). Section 7 takes this up for persistence (CR-7, 0.9).

**C-2, C-3.** From the effective date, supermarkets, convenience stores and new standalone stores enter (CR-4, x: stay out, y: enter; A-3, with Ashcombe's experience and the Board's projection of 2,000 to 3,000 applications in the first year). Supermarkets add a department in existing floor space within months; standalone stores need premises and take longer (TS-1, upward clause). The price falls as entry proceeds (CR-6: at the capped number of outlets, buyers wished to buy more than the capped stores sold at a competitive margin). That the money price, rather than only travel and convenience, carried part of the cap's rent is a premise (A-4: the 11% basket gap after excise). The license value itself shows a rent exists, but a rent can come from more customers per store as well as from a higher price, so C-3 needs A-4; EN-6's 1.0 clause now says the same of any cap on the number of sellers. An opposing chain also reaches the price: with costs fixed per outlet, more outlets each selling less raise the cost per bottle, and CR-4's 0.8 note signs a unit price only with a premise about entry and exit. The response argument does not settle this, since under CR-12's 1.0 clause it needs a constant-cost premise that fixed costs per outlet deny. A-4 is the dominance premise (CR-12e, empirical): Ashcombe, which has free entry, sells the same basket 11% cheaper after excise, so the price with free entry is lower net of that cost.

**Buyers (C-4 to C-6, C-16).** Supermarkets and convenience stores are spread more widely than the 2,380 package stores, so some buyers find an outlet nearer home or on a trip they already make (C-4; A-5). With a lower price and a shorter trip, buyers buy more (C-5; CR-4c). Each chain is independent, so C-5 holds if either premise does (CR-12d). Some border residents who crossed to Ashcombe for price or for grocery-store sales now buy at home (RS-4; C-6; A-6). Saturday sales rise with the general increase under both readings, and under U1-b also from Sunday purchases moved to Saturday (C-16).

Under U1-b the Sunday clause cuts the other way. No Merrowind store may sell off-premises on Sunday. Buyers who bought on Sunday on the counterfactual path move the purchase to Saturday (wine and spirits keep), buy on Sunday in Ashcombe (A-25), drink in a bar, or go without. Purchases moved to Ashcombe or lost oppose C-5 and C-6, and no premise ranks them against the gains from lower prices and nearer outlets, so both are `ambiguous` under U1-b. Under U1-a the only Sunday loss is from old-license stores that close (C-7), whose Sunday buyers go to the remaining old-license stores, move to Saturday or go without; A-18 states that this is small against the main chains, so C-5 is an increase and C-6 a decrease under U1-a.

**Incumbents (C-7, C-8, C-19 to C-20).** CR-4 (x: keep the store open, y: close or sell the premises): some package stores, with lower margins (C-35), fewer customers and a fee five times higher, no longer cover their costs (A-7) and close over the medium run (C-7; RS-6). K2 is a cost fixed per outlet: by CR-4's 0.8 clarification it bears on whether marginal outlets operate, not on the price of each bottle, so no price effect is drawn from it. Their staff lose jobs or hours (C-8, a group under RS-6). Investment by incumbents who stay is pushed both ways (C-19): lower expected margins lower the yield of expanding (−), while competition makes service and selection the margins on which they can keep customers (+; A-14). Kind (2) chains with p of at least 0.5: `ambiguous`.

**Leases (C-10, C-11) and EN-13 in reverse.** Under the cap, licenses moved to those who valued them most: holders who did not want to operate leased them to operators (EN-13; about 560 leases at about $30,000). From the effective date an operator needs no lease: a new license costs $5,000 a year. Leases for periods after that date are worth nothing under either reading: licenses renewed under section 12A are not transferable, so they cannot be leased, and the U1-a Sunday privilege stays with the holder and premises (C-10; CR-7c; CR-7, 1.0). Lessors' receipts fall (C-34), and lessees no longer pay them: the two sides of one transfer (CR-8b). Lessees' means (C-11) are not the lease alone. CR-3's note says that an actor who loses receipts as well as an outlay combines the two under CR-12b. (+) The lease of about $30,000 a year is replaced by a $5,000 fee, which the holder used to pay at $1,000 out of the lease. (−) The lessee's store loses the margin and some of the sales that the cap's rent gave it (C-35; A-4), and the lease price was what that rent was worth to operators. No premise ranks the two, so C-11 is `ambiguous`. Leases for months before the effective date carry the cap's full rent, which the repeal does not change until entry is possible; they are not signed. In reverse, EN-13's other conclusion is re-based in 1.0 on the no-policy path: the cap held users down by blocking entry and by holders who leased out, so its reverse is entry by many (C-2) and the end of leasing, not deconsolidation; the locations to which licenses moved under the cap keep their stores (section 7).

**Lobbying (C-12 to C-14).** CR-4's 0.9 clarification and RS-7's repeal clause apply: lobbying is signed against the counterfactual path, on which the cap was contested. That it would have stayed contested is a premise (A-9: twenty years of campaigns and two ballot initiatives that came close). Holders lobbied to keep the cap and grocers to end it; after the repeal holders lobby to restore it (A-8) and grocers, if holders press, to keep the repeal (A-27). The two classes are recorded separately (CR-1, recording). For holders (C-13), lobbying to restore the cap (+) replaces defending it against initiatives (−); for grocers (C-47), defending the repeal (+) replaces the campaign against the cap (−). Each lobbied in both worlds, so each is `ambiguous` (CR-4, 1.0; RS-7, 1.0). Two questions exist only under the repeal and are increases against none (CR-12, 0.9): compensation, which K5 refuses and holders now seek (C-12; A-8); and Sunday sales (C-14). Under U1-a, new licensees lobby for Sunday sales and old-license holders to keep their exclusive Sunday market; under U1-b, every licensee lobbies to restore Sunday sales. On the counterfactual path every licensed store sells on Sunday and nobody needs to lobby over it (A-26).

**Sunday sales (C-15) and bars (C-17).** Under U1-b, legal off-premises Sunday sales end (CR-1, operation 1): a decrease. Under U1-a, Sunday sales are reserved to old-license stores. (−) Some of those stores close (C-7), and some buyers move Sunday purchases to cheaper new stores on Saturday. (+) Old-license stores cut prices to meet new competitors, and if they price the same on every day of the week, Sunday prices fall too (A-21). No premise ranks these: `ambiguous`. Bars and restaurants (C-17): cheaper, nearer off-premises drinks draw some drinkers from bars (RS-16, mirror case; A-19). Under U1-b, Sunday drinkers who cannot buy off-premises go to bars instead, an opposing chain with no dominance premise: `ambiguous`. Under U1-a that Sunday chain is small (A-18): decrease.

**Timing in the window (C-18, C-20 to C-22).** The 18 months between signing and the effective date reward acting at different times (RS-8). Entrants apply from June 2028, lease premises and fit out departments in the window so as to open on the first day (C-18; an activity that exists only under the repeal, an increase against none; signed on the flow of investment in the window). Three chains reach purchases near the effective date. Buyers who expect lower prices from December delay storable purchases (−, A-15). Incumbents expect stock carried past the effective date to sell at a lower margin, and those expecting to close sell off stock; they cut what they carry (C-20; CR-7a across time, x: hold stock into December, y: sell it before; A-16) and sell it at lower prices in the last months (+). Both the clearance and buyers' delay lower the price in those months (C-42; CR-6, more offered and less sought at each price; A-16, with A-15 a second chain in the same direction). Sellers and buyers move in opposite directions in time, which is RS-8's 0.9 case: in September to November 2028, C-21 is `ambiguous`. In the first months after the effective date, the first new outlets and delayed purchases push up, and purchases pulled into the clearance sales push down; the stock of outlets is still small, so no premise ranks them (C-22, `ambiguous`). The medium-run sign (C-5) is a separate horizon (CR-12g). These are transition effects: `persistence` `n/a` (CR-10b).

**The Board (C-23).** The Board's work rises from about 90 transfers a year to 2,000 to 3,000 applications, on an appropriation the Act does not raise (A-17). Under OC-3's 0.9 fixed-budget clause the resources come from some other use of its time: license decisions take longer, or fewer compliance checks are made on existing licensees, or both. Recorded as a mixed group. Fees go to the general fund, so they do not fund the Board (OC-3).

**Credibility (TS-5).** Supermarkets add departments in existing space with little sunk cost, and enter whatever they expect. Standalone entrants sign multi-year leases; they would hold back if they feared the cap's return, but with the Act's wide margins and no referendum petition filed, that premise is A-22 (p = 0.25), below the cut-off, so it is not reported.

**Trips (C-24 to C-26).** Buyers who bought wine and spirits at a package store on a separate trip, and mixers, snacks and ice with them, now buy them in the supermarket on the grocery trip (RS-9; A-20): the package stores lose those other sales (C-24), and supermarkets gain them. Supermarkets and convenience stores gain those trip sales (C-45). Border residents who now buy liquor at home also buy at home the groceries they bought on those trips (C-26). Under U1-b, Sunday trips to Ashcombe carry groceries the other way, so C-25 and C-26 are `ambiguous` under U1-b. Ashcombe's stores face fewer Merrowind buyers under U1-a; by CR-6's 0.9 clarification, how that divides between fewer sales and lower prices needs a premise on their supply, so the two are a group (C-25).

**Wholesalers (C-27, C-44).** Retail sales in the state are wholesalers' sales (CR-9 with LW-10), so C-27 follows C-5. In the last months of the window, deliveries are retailers' sales plus the change in their stock. Incumbents run their stock down (C-20), and the clearance sales that oppose buyers' delay in C-21 come out of that stock, not out of new deliveries (A-16); buyers' delay lowers sales as well (A-15, a second chain in the same direction). Entrants' opening stock would push the other way, but it cannot be delivered before their licenses take effect on 1 December 2028 (A-28), so it falls after the window. Deliveries fall (C-44).

**Retail premises (C-28, C-29): the response argument.** Standalone entrants and convenience stores fitting out a liquor section bid for small retail units in commercial zones, where vacancy is 5%. Rents rise (CR-9, then CR-6). Landlords respond by converting other space and by seeking rezonings. CR-12e's response argument says this cannot push rents below their old level: the conversions stop once a converted unit earns no more than other uses. The total it bounds is the rent of small retail units in commercial zones, paid by all tenants, liquor outlets and others alike (CR-12, 1.0: name the total). That gives "an increase or no change" only with a premise that the cost of adding such units does not fall as more are added, and CR-12's 0.9 clarification asks for a partial response to report `increase`. A-13 supplies both: within a year or two, units can be added only by conversions and rezonings at a cost above the old one (rising cost), so supply cannot fully expand at the old cost (5% vacancy, rezonings of 12 to 24 months). So C-28 is an increase in the short run. At the higher rent, some other tenants at the margin rent no unit, or a smaller one (C-43; CR-4c; CR-8a: units taken by liquor outlets are withdrawn from other uses; RS-13's 1.0 clause on added supply that draws inputs from other uses). In the medium run a second change the repeal causes adds supply: package stores that close vacate their units (C-7; RS-13). That is not a response to the rent rise, so the response argument does not cover it (CR-12e, 0.8); with no dominance premise, C-29 is `ambiguous`.

**Lenders (C-30, C-46).** About $210 million of loans are secured by licenses at up to 60% of their value. When the value falls (C-1), some loans exceed it, and some borrowers cannot cover the shortfall from other assets (A-10): the banks' expected recoveries fall (CR-9, `contract`; CR-7c). Loans to buy licenses also fall: before the effective date licenses sell for much less, and after it they cannot be sold at all (C-46; CR-9 with LW-10, loans `input_to` license purchases). Stopped: the banks' other borrowers, depth 3.

**Stores and licenses (C-31, C-32).** For an owner-operator the license and the store are one asset. Its value falls at signing by the license's fall (C-1), less the transitional rent the store keeps while new outlets are opened (CR-7c, CR-10c): a decrease, which then shrinks toward the never-imposed path as the stock of outlets grows (section 7). For an operator who leases the license, the store without the license is reached by opposing chains (C-32). (+) From December 2028 the lease ends, while customers stay scarce until entry catches up, so the store keeps a declining transitional rent. (−) On the counterfactual path the lease took the license's rent and the holder paid the $1,000 fee; after the repeal the operator pays $5,000 a year for good and loses margin and sales as entrants arrive (C-35; A-4); lessees whose leases run past the date keep paying for a right that has lost its value. A transitional gain against a lasting cost, with no premise to rank them (CR-12b): `ambiguous`.

**Proximity (C-33, C-38).** A new outlet changes the situation of households nearby in two ways. Through exchange, it shortens their trips to buy wine and spirits (C-4), and a location tied to shorter trips is worth more to buyers who make them (RS-9, 0.9, location complements; CR-7c): homes near new outlets gain value if some home buyers value that (A-11). That is a Tier B chain. Without exchange, the outlet brings traffic, parking, litter and late-hour activity next door (CR-9, 0.7, `proximity`): a Tier C chain, which also needs a premise on what neighbors value (A-12). CR-12's 0.9 rule for a Tier C chain against a Tier B chain gives two consequences: C-33, the Tier B sign, qualified "not counting effects through `proximity`"; and C-38, the combined sign at Tier C, `ambiguous`, since no premise ranks the convenience against the disamenity. The 500-foot rule from schools and places of worship stays on both paths; it limits where the proximity chain runs but does not remove it.

**Stopping points.** Employees of closing stores look for other work (C-8), depth 3: stopped. Suppliers of fixtures and refrigeration to new outlets, depth 3, diffuse: stopped. Customers of the banks in C-30: stopped.

## 8. Time structure

- **Introduction to signing (February to June 2027).** License prices fall part of the way as passage becomes likely, and most of the rest at signing (C-1; CR-7c). Lenders mark down license-secured loans (C-30). Owner-operators' stores lose value (C-31); the value of stores run on leased licenses is `ambiguous` (C-32). Leases written now for periods after December 2028 are priced low (C-10), and loans to buy licenses fall (C-46).
- **Window (June 2027 to November 2028).** The cap still binds: no new outlet may open, so prices, travel and leases for these months are not changed by the repeal. Entrants apply, lease premises and fit out (C-18), bidding up rents of small retail units (C-28), so that some other tenants rent fewer units (C-43). Holders lobby for compensation and, depending on U1, both sides lobby over Sunday sales (C-12, C-14). In the last months, incumbents carry less stock and sell some off, while some buyers wait for lower prices (C-20, C-21); prices and wholesalers' deliveries fall (C-42, C-44).
- **From 1 December 2028.** Supermarkets open liquor departments at once; standalone stores follow over one to three years (C-2, TS-1). The Board's queue is longest in the first year (C-23). Prices and travel fall as outlets open (C-3, C-4). Under U1-b, Sunday off-premises sales stop on each license's first renewal under 12A, which is 1 December 2028 for all of them (C-15). Under U1-a, the old licenses keep a Sunday market, which shrinks as some old-license stores close (C-7).
- **Medium to long run.** Some package stores close (C-7, C-8) and vacate units (C-29). The stock of outlets approaches what it would have been without the cap, and the transitional rent of existing stores fades (C-31, C-32). From the effective date no license can be bought or leased (C-10, C-41, C-46). Under U1-a, the old licenses' Sunday privilege lasts as long as the licenses are renewed: a fixed stock that can only shrink.
- **Costs already incurred.** Licenses bought at $400,000 before the bill was introduced, and stores sited where licenses could be had, are the same with and without the repeal; they are not consequences of it (CR-10b). They enter through what they make cheaper or dearer next: buyers of licenses with loans outstanding still owe them (C-30).

On bottles that would have been bought anyway, what holders no longer receive buyers no longer pay (C-35 and C-3, two sides of one transfer; C-34 and the lease item of C-11 for leases). Holders who would have sold their license lose the sale price, which is part of C-1; the other side is the roughly 90 would-be buyers a year, who keep the means (C-41). In the year each would have bought, the $400,000 outlay is far larger than the higher fee plus one year's rent forgone (arithmetic, CR-12e), so C-41 is an increase. Over the whole period it is not signed: the license would have earned its rent, whose present value is what they would have paid (CR-7c), and CR-3's note combines lost receipts with saved outlays. On bottles bought only because the price falls (C-5), there was no payment to transfer: these are new exchanges that each party expects to gain from (OC-4 in reverse, CR-5). The two sides differ in timing: holders lose the capitalized value at once (C-1), while buyers receive the transfer year by year as outlets open; and during the transition part of it goes to existing stores (C-32), not to buyers. Under U1-b, the voided Sunday right passes to no one: it is an option removed from holders and buyers alike (CR-1, operation 1), whose prevented exchanges are a loss to both (CR-5a), not a transfer under CR-8b. Under U1-a, it becomes a privilege of the old licenses alone.

**The fee (C-36, C-37).** Both sides recorded (CR-8b). C-36 is the fee rise for holders of existing licenses. For entrants it is not a burden against the counterfactual: those who would have bought or leased a license pay far less for the right to sell (C-41, C-11), and those who would not have entered gain an option (CR-3b). The fees go to the general fund, which also funds the Board; the Board's appropriation does not change (A-17), so the fees are the net change in that budget's receipts from the Act's licensing (CR-8, 1.0). The fund spends more or taxes or borrows less; which, the record does not say, so no class of taxpayers or recipients is signed. `resources_available_other_uses` of licensees is not signed: a licensee who closes stops paying (CR-3, note).

**Excise and sales tax (C-39, C-40).** The excise is per gallon, so it follows quantity. The sales tax is a percentage of a price that falls, on a quantity that rises (under U1-a), so its sign is open.

**Unseen uses.** Fit-out, applications and the lobbying in C-12 to C-14 are means used up (CR-8c); the Board's extra work comes from its other tasks (C-23). Entrants' spending on fit-out (C-18) is drawn from other uses (CR-8a), which are not named without a premise.

## 11. Report

**The policy.** From 1 December 2028, Merrowind issues an off-premises liquor license to any qualified applicant for $5,000 a year, ending a 1949 cap of one license per 2,500 residents per town under which licenses traded for about $400,000. Existing licenses continue, pay the same fee, and get no compensation. New licenses may sell Monday to Saturday; whether old licenses keep their Sunday hours is unclear (U1).

**Visible consequences.** License holders lose most of the value of their licenses, at once (C-1). Supermarkets, convenience stores and new stores start selling wine and spirits (C-2), and prices fall as they do (C-3).

**Invisible consequences.**
- *Buyers.* Shorter trips (C-4); more bought in Merrowind if old licenses keep Sunday sales, an open question if they do not (C-5); fewer trips to Ashcombe under U1-a (C-6, C-25, C-26); more Saturday buying (C-16).
- *Incumbents.* Some package stores close and their staff lose jobs or hours (C-7, C-8); package stores sell fewer mixers and snacks (C-24); margins fall (C-35); the fee rises fivefold (C-36). Operators who lease a license stop paying for it but lose margin and pay the higher fee, so whether they have more to spend, and whether their stores gain value, is open (C-11, C-32); lessors lose the income, under either reading (C-10, C-34). Owner-operators' stores lose value (C-31). Banks with license-secured loans lose part of their collateral (C-30), and lend less to buy licenses (C-46). Would-be buyers of licenses keep the price they would have paid (C-41). Supermarkets and convenience stores sell more mixers and groceries on liquor trips (C-45).
- *The 18 months before the effective date.* Entrants invest to open on the first day (C-18) and bid up rents of small shops (C-28), crowding out some other tenants (C-43). In the last months, stores carry less stock (C-20), prices fall (C-42) and wholesalers deliver less (C-44); whether purchases rise or fall around the date is open (C-21, C-22). The Board's applications back up or its other checks thin (C-23).
- *Politics.* Holders seek compensation (C-12) and both sides lobby over Sunday sales (C-14); whether lobbying over the cap itself rises is open, for holders and for grocers (C-13, C-47).
- *State receipts.* License fees rise (C-37); excise rises if old licenses keep Sunday sales (C-39).
- *Homes near new outlets* gain at Tier B from shorter trips (C-33); counting traffic and litter next door, the sign is open, at Tier C (C-38).

**Ambiguous effects.** Lessees' means and the value of their stores (C-11, C-32); incumbents' investment (C-19); purchases around the effective date (C-21, C-22); retail rents in the medium run (C-29); lobbying over the cap (C-13, C-47); sales tax revenue (C-40); and, under U1-b, in-state purchases, cross-border trips, bars' sales, wholesalers' sales and excise (C-5, C-6, C-17, C-25, C-26, C-27, C-39); under U1-a, Sunday sales (C-15).

**What does not reverse.** None of the licenses' scarcity value survives, except, if old licenses keep Sunday hours, a new privilege on a stock that can only shrink, worth something only to the holder who uses it, since no license can be sold or leased after the effective date. The shortage of stores does survive for a while: existing stores keep part of their advantage until entrants catch up, which takes years for standalone stores. Licenses bought on credit leave debts that the repeal does not cancel.

**Key conditions.** The cap binds (A-1, p = 0.95); entry pays at the new fee (A-3, p = 0.85); part of the cap's rent is in the price (A-4, p = 0.7). Under U1-a, the Sunday losses are small (A-18, p = 0.7).

**What this analysis cannot say.** How far prices fall or how many stores open or close; whether buyers' gains outweigh holders' losses; whether the repeal is fair to holders who bought recently; effects on drinking, health and safety, which are out of scope for version 1; and, until Q20 is decided, whether proximity effects belong to the analysis.

## Gaps found

**(a) Calculus gaps (a step no core rule covers).** None new. The proximity chain (C-38) is the open GAP-026-1, reported at Tier C under CR-9's 0.7 clarification pending Q20.

**(b) Unclear core rules.**
1. *CR-7's 0.9 clause on persistence: which stock?* "An asset price persists only as far as the stock its value depends on persists" needed interpretation, because the license's value depended on two things at once: a legal count of permissions, which ends on one date, and the physical stock of stores the count held down, which grows over years. Read literally with "the stock of stores", the license's value would persist partly; it does not, because after the effective date the scarcity rent goes to the stores, not to the permission (C-1 against C-31, C-32). The clause should say that the stock is the one whose scarcity the asset's holder can capture; where a repeal separates a permission from the capital it allowed, the two persist differently. (Settled in 1.0: CR-7, "Which stock".)
2. *CR-8's voiding clause and a statutory privilege.* CR-8 0.7 covers a contract right that passes from its holder to the other party. The cap's privilege is not a contract right and passes to no single party: on continuing sales it is a transfer to buyers, received year by year as outlets open, while holders lose it at once; on new sales it is not a transfer at all; during the transition part of it goes to existing stores. Under U1-b, the voided Sunday right passes to no one (CR-5a, not CR-8b). Derivable from CR-8b, CR-5a and CR-7c, but the clause invites reading the whole license value as a transfer to "the other party". (Settled in 1.0: CR-8, "Statutory rights".)
3. *CR-12e's response argument with a second change.* For retail rents (C-28, C-29), the 0.9 clarification on a partial response worked in the short run; in the medium run the opposing chain (units vacated by closing stores) is a second change the repeal causes, which the 0.8 clause already excludes. Worked, but the two clauses must be read together and the horizon split (CR-12g) to get a signed short-run consequence.

**(c) Missing or wrong patterns.**
1. *EN-6 on a cap on the number of sellers.* EN-6 is written for a quota on quantity. Applied as written to a cap on outlets, it gives `quantity_traded` decrease and `price_paid` increase as if quantity were capped; but buyers can buy as much as they like at each capped store, so the pressure goes to price and to travel, and the rent can come from more customers per store rather than a higher price. Its conclusion for `price_paid` needed a premise (A-4) that EN-6 does not ask for. A clause for caps on sellers (pressure split among price, travel and volume per store, as CR-6 gives) would help. No sign had to be overridden, but EN-6's unconditional `price_paid` would have overclaimed. (Settled in 1.0: EN-6, "Caps on sellers".)
2. *EN-13 in reverse.* EN-13 covers consolidation under tradable rights. In reverse, consolidation does not unwind: stores sited where licenses moved stay there, and what ends is the lease market (C-10). The pattern could say that its reverse is entry by many plus the end of leasing, not deconsolidation. (Settled in 1.0: EN-13, "Baseline".)
3. *RS-16 mirror case for a good whose availability rises through more sellers rather than a lower set price* (bars, C-17): worked as written.
4. No pattern gave a sign I had to override.

**(d) Vocabulary.**
1. *Hours and days of sale.* There is no variable for the hours or days on which a good may be sold; Sunday sales were recorded as `quantity_traded` with a qualifier (C-15, C-16).
2. *A license that is not property.* The ontology has `rights_allocation` for creating a right but no instrument for ending one; K1 was recorded as the removal of a `quota` on sellers.
3. *Transitional rent of existing capital* (C-32): recorded as `asset_value`; a note in 2.4 that a stock's scarcity rent during a transition is `asset_value` of the stock would help.
