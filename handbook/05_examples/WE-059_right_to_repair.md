# WE-059 Right to repair for farm equipment and phones

Analyst: draft (Handbook 1.0 held-out test, corrected under 1.1)  |  Rule catalog version: 1.1  |  Status: awaiting expert review

A stylized policy in a fictional state. Several jurisdictions have passed "right to repair" laws; this one is not modeled on any of them. It is a held-out test of catalog 1.0 on a kind of policy none of the 1.0 changes were drawn from. It probes PC-17 (a mandated term: makers must supply parts, tools and software to independent repairers and owners on terms no worse than they give their authorized repairers; who bears it), PC-18 (contract terms that restrict repair are made void, including in existing contracts), RS-16 and its 1.0 clause on prices per unit of quality (authorized repair as the substitute whose demand falls), CR-5c (the terms between makers, dealers, independents and buyers), CR-7c (the values of makers, dealers, independent shops and devices), EN-14 read for a design rule on new devices with older units exempt, CR-12's 1.0 note on the baseline of patterns, RS-4 at the state line, and CR-11 (diagnostic information and the information that parts locks give buyers of used phones). The unclear provision is whether software locks that pair parts to a device must be removed or only made unlockable by the owner. The counterfactual throughout is **Kestrava without the Act**: makers free to choose whom they supply, contract terms restricting repair enforceable, and parts pairing lawful. Health, safety and environmental effects (including emissions tampering and electronic waste) are outside the scope of version 1. The probabilities are illustrative; the evidence column says what a real analysis would cite.

## 1. Policy record

**Kestrava Fair Repair Act.** Kestrava is a state of about 6.2 million. To its west lies Darnholt, which has no such law; about 3,000 Kestrava farms lie within 30 km of the state line, and six farm equipment dealers operate on the Darnholt side within 30 km of it. Similar bills were introduced in Kestrava in 2023 and 2025 and failed after campaigns on both sides.

**Phones.** About 5.4 million phones are in use in Kestrava and about 1.9 million are sold a year (about 60% online, delivered to Kestrava addresses); three makers sell about 90% of them, at the same prices in every state. About 1.1 million phone repairs are made a year: about 60% by makers' stores and authorized providers (a screen repair with a genuine part costs about $280), about 40% by some 900 independent shops using non-genuine or salvaged parts (about $160). On recent models makers pair screens, batteries and cameras to the device: a replacement part not paired by the maker's server works with warnings (battery health hidden, "unknown part" notices) or with some features disabled (face unlock). One maker already sells genuine parts and rents tools to owners in all states at the prices its authorized providers pay, and pairs the part through its server after the repair. About 40,000 phones are reported stolen in Kestrava each year; activation locks and parts pairing now keep most parts of reported phones from working in other devices.

**Farm equipment.** About 38,000 farms run about 95,000 tractors and 12,000 combines with electronic controls; about 30% of the tractors were sold on or after 1 January 2023. Four makers sell through 140 authorized dealer locations, which are independent businesses under dealer agreements; about 260 independent shops repair farm equipment. Dealers' service revenue is about $520 million a year ($330 million parts, $190 million labor at about $165 an hour; independents charge about $95). Dealers subscribe to each maker's diagnostic software (about $8,000 a year per location) and buy parts at 25% below list. Independents can buy many mechanical parts at list from dealers but cannot buy the software, without which many electronic parts cannot be calibrated or paired. Farm surveys put the average wait for a dealer technician at harvest at 2.5 days. New equipment sales in Kestrava are about $1.1 billion a year; 14 specialty models sell fewer than 20 units a year each in the state.

**Timing.** The bill passed the legislature on 18 February 2027 and was signed on 1 March 2027; it takes effect on 1 September 2028 (18 months). No expiry.

The counterfactual law, which K1 to K3 change (CR-1, recording): a maker may refuse to sell parts, tools and software to anyone but its authorized repairers; contract and license terms restricting repair are enforceable; parts pairing is lawful.

Unclear provisions:
- **U1, parts pairing.** §5(c) forbids "a software lock to prevent the functioning of a part supplied, repaired or replaced in accordance with this Act". §2(f): "'Software lock' means any technical measure that restricts the functioning of a part unless the manufacturer authorizes it." §3(a)(iv) lists among what makers must supply "the means to pair a part to the equipment". Reading U1-a: pairing that needs the maker's authorization is itself the lock §5(c) forbids; any part installed, genuine, salvaged or non-genuine, must work fully without contacting the maker, and makers must remove pairing from covered devices and leave it out of new ones. Reading U1-b: §3(a)(iv) presupposes that pairing continues; a lock is lawful if the owner (or a repairer acting for the owner) can pair a genuine part using a tool the maker supplies on fair and reasonable terms. Makers may then refuse to pair parts reported stolen and need not pair non-genuine parts. The Attorney General has issued no guidance.

Readings get no probability; where a sign depends on U1, one sign per reading is given.

**Figures.** If independents' share of phone repairs and their prices stayed as they are, and dealers' farm service revenue fell by a fifth, about $100 million a year of dealer service would move to independents and owners. The maker's existing national phone program already matches K1 on price and meets U1-b's pairing condition. Phones in use are replaced about every three years, so by 2030 nearly all phones in use will be covered; about 70% of tractors in use were sold before 2023 and are not covered, and tractors last 15 to 25 years.

**Who is bound, and whose options change.** K1 is a term in the sale of the device, but the party who most often invokes it is not the buyer: it is an independent repairer whom the buyer hires, now or years later. So the "contract" PC-17 speaks of is the sale of covered equipment, and the mandated term is a promise to third parties. PC-17's 1.1 clause covers this: the terms that adjust are those of the sale (its price, warranty and design), and the term also moves the bargaining range of the third exchange, the maker's sales of parts and software to independents (C-1, C-31); both are compared with no term. A buyer values it as far as it lowers the expected cost of repair over the device's life (CR-7, gross and net), whether or not the buyer ever uses an independent.

These are what the Act's supporters expect: independents and owners can buy what dealers buy, on the same terms; more repairs are done outside the makers' networks; repairs of a given kind cost less; anti-repair terms cannot be enforced. C-4 is signed per repair of a given kind with parts of a given quality (RS-16, 1.0): an independent screen repair with a genuine part may cost more than one with a non-genuine part did, but less than a genuine-part repair did before, when independents could not obtain the part or paid list. For self repair the owner's price is that of the inputs (C-1). For independent repair, C-4 holds only once independents' capacity has grown: before entry (A-5), demand for independents rises (C-2) against capacity that is short (their returns rise, C-24), which pushes their price per job up against the cheaper inputs, so the short-run sign is `ambiguous` (C-47; CR-12b). After entry, yields in independent repair are equalized (C-25), and with capacity added at constant cost (A-13) the price per job settles at the lower cost the cheaper inputs allow (CR-12e and its 1.1 note on every direction: entry, a response to the lower cost, cannot push the price back above the counterfactual; A-13 keeps the fall from being undone by rising cost).

**Authorized repair loses demand (C-6, C-7; RS-16 mirror).** K1 makes independent and self repair of covered equipment better (genuine parts, software, calibration). By RS-16's mirror case, a policy that makes a good more available pulls buyers from its substitutes: authorized repair loses jobs (C-6), and where its capacity cannot contract at once (A-4: dealers' technicians and bays are fixed in the short run) the pressure goes to its price or to its wait (C-7). The dealer market was rationed partly by waiting (2.5 days at harvest), so by RS-16's 1.1 clause on waiting and CR-6 the price and the wait are a group, `at_least_one_decrease`; neither is signed alone without a premise on how the shift splits (CR-6, 0.9). RS-16's 1.0 clause says to sign the price per unit of a given quality: the simple jobs (filters, sensors, screens) leave first, so the average dealer ticket may rise as the mix shifts to complex jobs. That is a change in mix, not a price change, and is not signed. Per job of a given kind, the dealer price or the dealer wait falls (C-7).

**Independents (C-2, C-8).** The software subscription and tools are costs fixed per shop, not per repair: by RS-16 (1.0) and CR-4 (0.8) they bear on whether a shop operates and at what size, not on the price per job. Entry rises (C-8; RS-6 in reverse; A-5) as the yield on independent repair rises (C-24).

**The mandated term and who bears it (C-9 to C-11; PC-17, PC-18).** For every sale of covered equipment, the maker must now provide a term it did not provide (A-2), so its cost of selling in Kestrava rises: the margin it earned on repair inputs sold only through its network, the cost of supplying many small buyers, support. By CR-5c the bargaining range of the sale moves, and the pressure goes to the other terms of the sale, which PC-17's 1.1 clause names for a product: price, warranty and the models offered, as a mixed group (C-9, `at_least_one_as_stated`; PC-17). For farm equipment one member has its own premise: farm makers raise list prices in Kestrava (A-18, p = 0.55; regional dealer pricing), so the farm price is signed alone (C-46), and the specialty models withdrawn are signed alone on A-11 (C-21, RS-24). For phones, sold at one national price, A-6 names no member, so C-9 stays a group and no member of it is used as a chain elsewhere (CR-12, 1.1: group members are not chains). The buyer values the term (A-22), so part of the cost is recovered in what buyers will pay (CR-7, gross and net): the burden C-9 names is gross. K2 removes the option of protecting the aftermarket by contract; makers move to substitute terms that remain lawful (warranty exclusions for damage caused by a repair, service bundles, software sold as expiring subscriptions, which K2 voids only if they restrict repair) and to price (C-10; PC-18). Who bears the net burden (C-11): makers sell phones and equipment in many states at prices set nationally, so their alternatives are wider than those of Kestrava's dealers, whose buildings, staff and franchise are tied to their territory (A-19). By CR-7b, the side with clearly worse alternatives bears more: dealers bear more of the farm side's burden than makers do. DM-1 is not cited: its If clause asks for a tax or subsidy (TX-2 or SB-2), and CR-7b carries the comparison for a mandate. On the phone side the authorized providers are mostly makers' own stores; no share is signed.

**Pairing (C-12 to C-14; U1).** Under U1-a makers must remove pairing that needs their authorization, from covered devices and from new ones (C-12, decrease). Under U1-b pairing may stay if owners can pair genuine parts themselves. That makes pairing costlier to keep (the maker must supply a pairing tool on dealer terms; CR-4: some makers drop it) but leaves it as the one lawful means of controlling which parts work, which favors adding it to more parts (A-24). The chains oppose: `ambiguous`. Non-genuine parts (C-13): under U1-a they work fully, which raises their value to buyers (+); under both readings genuine parts are now available to independents at dealer terms, a close substitute (−, A-21); and more repairs move to independents, some with non-genuine parts (+). The chains oppose under both readings: `ambiguous`. Parts from stolen phones (C-14): now, pairing and activation locks keep most of them from working (A-17). Under U1-a any part works, so parts from stolen phones gain value and their trade rises (RS-3, CR-4). Under U1-b makers may refuse to pair parts reported stolen, as they do now: `no_change`. Theft itself, and any effect on personal safety, is out of scope.

**Timing (C-15, C-16).** The Act was signed 18 months before it takes effect. Owners whose repair can wait (a cracked screen, a worn part outside the season) defer it until independents can do it with genuine parts and software (RS-8; A-7): authorized repairs in the window fall, and independent and self repairs in the first months after the date rise, each against the counterfactual. Purchases of new equipment are not shifted: equipment sold since 1 January 2023 is covered whenever bought, so the Act gives no reason to buy before or after a date.

**Across the state line (C-17, C-18; RS-4).** Coverage attaches to equipment sold or leased to a purchaser in Kestrava. A Kestrava farmer near the line who buys from a Darnholt dealer gets no coverage, so buying in Kestrava gains value (−); if farm makers raise list prices in Kestrava to recover the mandate's cost (A-18, p = 0.55), buying in Darnholt gains (+). The second chain needs a premise the case supplies at p ≥ 0.5, so by CR-12's 0.8 note (kind 2) the sign is `ambiguous` (C-17). That chain is the farm price signed alone on its own premise (C-46), not a member of the group C-9, so it may be combined (CR-12, 1.1: group members are not chains). RS-4's 1.1 clause says that where the policy favors one side on some terms and the other side on others, the net is signed under CR-12b, which is what is done here (Gaps found, c4). Phones are sold nationally at one price. A phone maker can supply parts and tools only to Kestrava buyers (x) or to all (y); segmenting by state costs more than the margin it protects outside Kestrava for some makers (A-8, p = 0.6), as the one existing national program shows. Where makers extend their programs, owners in Darnholt pay less for genuine-part repairs too (C-18): a consequence beyond the boundary through the makers' choice (CR-9).

**Reclassification (C-19).** K's definition covers equipment "designed primarily for agricultural use". Compact tractors used on farms and for grounds care can be designed and marketed for the latter (RS-5; A-9).

**Compliance and breach (C-20).** The Attorney General acts on complaint. By RS-20's 1.0 baseline clause, against no Act breach of a new rule increases from none wherever enforcement is less than certain: makers' offers that fall short (prices above dealer terms net of discounts, slow fulfillment, software missing functions) occur (A-10). Where they fall depends on who complains: farmers and organized independents more than phone owners (RS-20).

**Specialty models (C-21).** Supplying documentation, tools and software for each model to anyone in Kestrava is a cost fixed per model offered there. By CR-4's 0.7 clause and RS-24 it bears on whether the model is offered (x: offer it, y: drop it), not on the price of each unit. For specialty models selling fewer than 20 units a year in the state, the cost can exceed the margin (A-11, p = 0.5): some are withdrawn from Kestrava. Against the counterfactual, where those 14 models are offered, the count can fall.

**Lobbying (C-22).** The question of right to repair was contested on the counterfactual path: bills failed in 2023 and 2025 after campaigns on both sides, and would likely have returned. By CR-4's 1.0 and 1.1 notes (the same question is the policy field, right to repair) and RS-7 (1.0), lobbying on right to repair as such is signed against that contest and needs a premise the case lacks; it is not signed. The questions C-22 names (the reading of U1, what is "fair and reasonable", what counts as agricultural equipment) exist only under the Act: `increase` against none (CR-12, 0.9; A-23).

**Dealers' parts counter, complaints, genuine phone parts and farm prices (C-43 to C-46).** Owners and independents who bought parts at list from dealers can now buy them from the maker on dealer terms (K1; CR-4: the dealer counter at list is made worse relative to the maker's offer), so dealers' retail parts sales fall (C-43; A-1). Complaints under the Act are an activity that exists only under it: owners and independents who receive offers short of it complain (C-44; CR-4, x: accept, y: complain; CR-12, 0.9; A-10). Genuine phone parts (C-45): more repairs raise demand for parts (CR-9, LW-10). Under U1-b non-genuine parts still give warnings while genuine parts are now sold to independents and pair fully, so independents switch toward genuine parts and no one switches away on account of the change (CR-4a): `increase`. **Values (C-23 to C-27; CR-7c).** Each value moves when the Act becomes expected, in February 2027 at passage (A-20), not on the effective date. Dealers lose service income on covered equipment (C-6, C-7) on capital tied to their territory, so their dealerships lose value (C-23). Independent shops earn more while capacity is short (C-24). Entry follows (C-8): there is no permission to hold here, so the gain follows the stock of repair capacity, which grows (CR-7a, CR-10b; CR-7's 1.0 clause on which stock is about a permission and does not arise). By CR-12's response argument, entry stops once yields in independent repair equal those elsewhere; with constant cost of setting up a shop (A-13) yields return to the counterfactual level (C-25). CR-12's 1.0 note: the argument gives equal yields, not a return of the old price; the price of independent repair per job (C-4) stays below the counterfactual, because the inputs are now cheaper (C-1). Makers lose the expected margin on repair inputs for the covered stock, which is already sold (the price of those units cannot be renegotiated) and, by K2, the right to enforce anti-repair terms in existing contracts (CR-8, 0.7: voiding transfers the right's value from its holder to the other party). For future units the Act only removes options, so by CR-3a makers cannot be better off by their own ranking. A firm's money value is another matter: the Act binds every rival at once and changes each maker's situation through the others (CR-9), and buyers value the term (A-22), so a fall in share values needs the premise that the lost repair-input margins and costs outweigh any gain from all rivals being bound and from buyers' higher valuation (A-25, an empirical dominance premise under CR-12e). With it, their share values fall (C-26). Kestrava is a small part of their sales, so the effect may be small; by CR-6's 1.0 note it is signed (A-12). Owners of covered units receive what makers lose: lower expected repair costs and terms they may now ignore raise the value of covered used equipment and phones (C-27; RS-12: repair is a complement to holding the device).

**EN-14 and the exempt stock (C-28).** K1 to K3 apply to equipment sold since 2023; older equipment is exempt. Read as a design rule on new units with the stock exempt, EN-14's conclusion is that new units cost more, so exempt units face less competition and rise in value, and are kept longer. Applied as written, it gives `increase` for exempt tractors. But EN-14 assumes the standard is a cost to buyers; here it is also something buyers value (A-22). Against the counterfactual, covered units became cheaper to keep running (C-27), which draws buyers from exempt units (RS-16: the substitute's price, −). And independent shops that enter to serve covered units also serve older ones (RS-12, +). EN-14's 1.1 Unless clause now states the first of these: where buyers value the standard, covered units draw demand from the exempt stock, which opposes the rise in its value. EN-14's own (+) chain rests on new farm units being dearer, which is signed alone (C-46, A-18), not on a member of the group C-9. The chains oppose: `ambiguous` (CR-12b; Gaps found, c2).

**Longer lives, fewer new units? (C-29, C-30).** Cheaper repair lowers the cost of keeping a device running against replacing it (CR-4): devices are kept longer. New sales: repair substitutes for replacement (−; RS-16 mirror); where new farm prices rise (C-46), fewer are bought (−); but each new covered unit is worth more to its buyer, who gets the term (+; A-22). No premise ranks them: `ambiguous` (C-30). The phone price is a member of the group C-9 and is not used as a chain here (CR-12, 1.1). The average age of covered devices in use (C-29) rises with longer lives but falls with any rise in new sales, a chain that is itself `ambiguous` (C-30; CR-12, 0.8, kind 3): `ambiguous`. That devices are kept longer is the (+) chain, not a consequence of its own (the ontology has no variable for a unit's life).

**Dealers' parts prices (C-31; CR-5c).** §2(d) ties independents' terms to the "most favorable terms" given dealers, net of discounts. A discount to dealers must now be given to everyone, so each discount costs the maker more (CR-4): the 25% dealer discount is narrowed or replaced by payments that are not tied to parts (+ for dealers' net parts price). Genuine parts now also compete with salvaged and non-genuine parts installed by independents (A-21), which presses makers' prices down (−). The chains oppose: `ambiguous`. The relation between makers and dealers is a `contract` (the dealer agreement), so its terms adjust only as agreements are renewed (CR-6, 0.7).

**Farmers' downtime (C-32).** More repairers and self-repair with the software shorten the wait for a repair at harvest (CR-6: where capacity per job rises, the queue margin falls; A-14). The dealer wait is a member of the group C-7 and is not used as a chain here (CR-12, 1.1).

**Technicians (C-33 to C-35).** Dealers employ fewer technicians or fewer hours (C-33, group: RS-6; CR-6, 1.1: labor demand); independents employ more (C-34): their added labor is largely in new shops, an activity that starts from none, where hours cannot substitute for jobs (OC-5, new activities), and nothing lowers employment in existing shops. The wage of farm technicians is pushed down by dealers' demand and up by independents': `ambiguous` (C-35).

**What pairing tells used-phone buyers (C-36; CR-11).** Now, a used phone's "unknown part" notices tell a buyer that a part was replaced with one the maker did not pair (A-15: some buyers check this; that is the prior belief CR-11's 0.7 note asks for, and they pay less for such phones, which is the direction). Under U1-a the lock is removed and the device no longer checks parts, so that information is lost: `decrease`. Under U1-b pairing stays and a part paired by the owner is recorded as paired: `no_change`. The Act changes what buyers can learn only through what the lock conveyed (CR-11c).

**Diagnosis (C-37; EN-15).** K1 requires makers to supply documentation and diagnostic software. As a disclosure mandate, it changes choices only where owners did not already know (A-16: owners could not read the codes and documentation before) and where they act on it (they diagnose and shop for repairs, rather than accept the dealer's diagnosis). With both premises, owners' information about faults increases.

**New designs (C-38).** Making parts separately replaceable now helps rivals in the aftermarket (−: some makers integrate components to cut exposure; A-24); buyers value repairable designs more now that repair is open (+; A-22). No premise ranks them, and the counterfactual trend (more integration) is not changed in a known direction: `ambiguous`. TS-3's product-standard clause applies to K3 under U1-a: it binds on the share of designs that would use pairing, which rises with the counterfactual trend.

**Owners' means (C-39).** Owners who keep buying the same repairs now pay less for them once independents' capacity has grown (C-4), or less for the parts they fit themselves (C-1), and so have more for other uses (CR-3, CR-8b). Before entry the independent price is `ambiguous` (C-47) and the dealer side is a group (C-7), so no short-run sign is given; owners who buy more repairs at the lower price may spend more on repair, and are not included.

**Independents' price before entry (C-47).** In the short run demand for independent repair rises (C-2) while independents' capacity is still short (A-5: shops open or expand only with time), which pushes their price per job up (CR-6); their inputs are cheaper (C-1), which pushes it down (CR-5c). No premise ranks them: `ambiguous`.

Stopping points. Farm output and crop losses from shorter downtime, phone resale abroad, the suppliers of independent shops, the spending of dealers' former employees: depth 3 to 4, diffuse; stopped. Emissions tampering by owners with diagnostic software, electronic waste, and theft's effect on personal safety: out of scope.

## 8. Time structure

- **February to March 2027: passage.** Values move: dealerships and makers' shares fall, covered used equipment rises, independent shops' expected returns rise (C-23, C-26, C-27). The bill's passage was likely from its committee vote, so part of the move came earlier (CR-7c, probability).
- **March 2027 to August 2028: the window.** Makers build parts portals and owner tools, renegotiate dealer agreements (C-31), consider withdrawing specialty models (C-21) and redesigning (C-12, C-38). Owners defer repairs that can wait (C-15). Independent shops prepare to buy software and tools.
- **September 2028 onward.** Anti-repair terms become void in existing contracts (C-5). Deferred repairs arrive (C-16). Independents and owners take jobs from dealers (C-2, C-3, C-6), and buy parts from makers rather than at dealers' counters (C-43); dealer prices per job or dealer waits fall while their capacity is fixed (C-7); independents' prices may rise or fall while their capacity is short (C-47). Complaints under the Act rise from none (C-44), and the Division's other work waits (C-20, C-41).
- **Medium run.** Independent shops enter (C-8) and their price per job falls below the counterfactual (C-4); dealers shed technicians or hours (C-33) as agreements and leases end. Under U1-a parts from stolen phones gain a market (C-14).
- **Long run.** Independents' returns are back to what capital earns elsewhere (C-25). Devices are kept longer, though the average age of those in use may rise or fall (C-29). The exempt stock of older tractors shrinks as it wears out, so the share of the farm fleet covered rises over 15 to 25 years; for phones nearly all units are covered by 2030.

## 11. Report

**The policy.** From 1 September 2028, makers of farm equipment and phones sold in Kestrava since 2023 must sell parts, tools, documentation and diagnostic software to owners and independent repairers on terms no worse than those they give their own repairers; contract terms that restrict repair are void; and software locks may not stop parts from working. It is unclear whether locks that pair parts to a device must be removed or only made unlockable by the owner.

**Visible consequences.** Independents and owners pay less for genuine parts, tools and software (C-1), do more repairs (C-2, C-3) and pay less per repair of a given kind (C-4). Anti-repair terms cannot be enforced (C-5).

**Invisible consequences.**
- *Authorized repair.* Dealers and authorized providers do fewer repairs and charge less per job of a given kind (C-6, C-7), though their average ticket may rise as simple jobs leave. Dealerships lose value (C-23) and bear more of the Act's burden than makers on the farm side (C-11); they employ fewer technicians or hours (C-33). Their net price for parts may rise or fall as discounts are reworked (C-31).
- *Independents.* More shops open (C-8), earning more at first and, as they enter, what capital earns elsewhere (C-24, C-25); they employ more technicians (C-34) and pay for software and tools (C-42).
- *Makers.* They pay to comply (C-40) and lose value (C-26). They recover part of the cost through price, warranty or the models they offer (C-9), shift to substitute terms (C-10), withdraw some specialty models from Kestrava (C-21), market some compact tractors outside the definition (C-19), and some offers fall short of the law (C-20). Their designs may become more or less repairable (C-38). New sales may rise or fall (C-30).
- *Owners.* Covered equipment and phones gain value (C-27) and are kept longer (C-29); owners who repair anyway have more to spend (C-39); farmers wait less at harvest (C-32) and know more about faults (C-37). Owners of older, exempt tractors may gain or lose (C-28). Repairs were deferred into the effective date (C-15, C-16).
- *Across the line.* Farmers near Darnholt may buy more or less there (C-17); Darnholt phone owners pay less for genuine repairs where makers extend their programs (C-18).
- *The state.* Other consumer complaints wait longer (C-41). Lobbying on the reading of the Act rises (C-22).

**Per reading of U1.** If pairing must go (U1-a), pairing disappears from covered phones (C-12), parts from stolen phones gain a market (C-14), and used-phone buyers lose the notices that told them a part was replaced (C-36). If owners need only be able to pair genuine parts (U1-b), pairing may spread or shrink (C-12), and stolen parts and used-phone information are unchanged (C-14, C-36). Non-genuine parts may sell more or less under both (C-13).

**Ambiguous effects.** Pairing under U1-b (C-12); non-genuine parts (C-13); cross-border farm purchases (C-17); exempt tractors' values (C-28); new sales (C-30); dealers' parts prices (C-31); technicians' wages (C-35); new designs (C-38).

**Key conditions.** That makers comply (A-1, p = 0.7); that some owners prefer independent or self repair (A-3, p = 0.9); that dealers' capital is more tied to place than makers' (A-19, p = 0.75); that buyers value the term (A-22, p = 0.7).

**What this analysis cannot say.** How large any effect is; whether owners as a whole gain more than makers and dealers lose; whether repairs by independents are better or worse done; emissions, safety, theft's harm to persons and electronic waste (out of scope for version 1); whether the Act is desirable.

## Gaps found

**(a) Calculus gaps (a step no core rule covers).** None. Every step was derived from CR-1 to CR-12. Whether the diagnostic knowledge the Act spreads raises total output is not signed: it is a non-rival means (CR-8, 0.7; Q21), and no consequence here depends on it.

**(b) Unclear core rules.**
1. *CR-1 for a term owed to third parties.* K1 is a term of each sale, but it is invoked by independent repairers the buyer may never deal with at the time of sale. CR-1's recording note covers operations on each actor class; it does not say how to record a term that adds an option for a class (independents) that is not party to the contract carrying it. Recorded as operation 3 on the maker's option of selling and operation 2 for independents and owners (section 2).
2. *CR-3a for a firm.* The sign of makers' asset values (C-26) rests partly on CR-3a: removing options cannot make an actor better off by its own ranking. For a firm the ranking that matters is investors' expected yield, and a rule binding all rivals at once can raise each one's yield (it ends a rivalry no one could end alone). That is a premise, not a rule; I used CR-3a with A-12 and note that CR-3 does not say when an option's removal can raise a firm's money value.
3. *CR-12's 0.8 kind 2 with a group member as the premise.* C-17's opposing chain rests on one member of PC-17's group (Kestrava prices rise). CR-6 and PC-17 say a member is signed alone only with its own premise; I gave it one (A-18, p = 0.55) and then treated it as a kind 2 chain. The rules allow this, but nothing says that a member premised at 0.55 for one key can be used as a chain while the group stays a group for another (C-9).

**(c) Missing or wrong patterns.**
1. *PC-17's main clause, for a term owed to third parties.* PC-17's If clause ("every contract of a kind to include a term") fits the sale of covered equipment, and its Then (cost to the provider rises, other terms adjust as a mixed group, gross and net) worked (C-9). Its examples and its group are written for employment (wages, hours); the device group (price, warranty, models) had to be built from CR-5c and CR-6. "Who bears it" needed DM-1 and CR-7b across three parties (makers, dealers, buyers), which PC-17 does not prompt (C-11). The 1.0 clause (a charge refunded on the act) did not arise.
2. *EN-14, applied as written, gave a sign I had to override.* Read for K3 (and K1) as a rule on new units with older units exempt, its preconditions are met: it gives `increase` for the exempt stock's value (new units dearer, so older ones face less competition). The conclusion assumes the standard is only a cost to buyers. Here buyers value what it requires (A-22): covered units are cheaper to keep running, which draws demand away from exempt units, and repair capacity built for covered units serves exempt ones too. Against the counterfactual the sign is `ambiguous` (C-28). EN-14 needs an Unless clause: where buyers value the standard, a chain through substitution toward covered units opposes its sign.
3. *RS-16's mirror case for a substitute input to the same end.* Worked as written: making independent repair available pulls demand from authorized repair (C-6, C-7), and its 1.0 clause on prices per unit of quality kept a mix shift (dealers keep complex jobs) from being read as a price rise. Its 1.0 clause on costs fixed per facility also applied, to independents' subscriptions (C-8).
4. *RS-4, where the favored side depends on two chains.* RS-4's Then says activity moves "to whichever side the policy favors". For farm purchases near the line, the policy favors Kestrava through coverage and may favor Darnholt through price (A-18); RS-4 gave no way to say which side is favored, and the sign came from CR-12 (C-17). A clause saying that where the policy changes both a benefit and a price on one side, the move is combined under CR-12b, would help.
5. *No pattern for a duty to deal at terms tied to another buyer's terms.* §2(d) ties independents' terms to dealers' "most favorable terms". The consequence that each discount to dealers now costs the maker more, so discounts shrink (C-31), was derived from CR-4 and CR-5c; no pattern prompts it. It resembles a most-favored-customer clause imposed by law.
6. *PC-18* worked as written: void terms, substitute terms as a mixed group (C-10), and voiding in existing contracts as a transfer (C-26, C-27).

**(d) Vocabulary.**
1. No variable for a repair as a service bought in place of a replacement; recorded as `quantity_traded` with qualifiers.
2. `product_attribute` was used for parts pairing (a software feature) and for the number of separately replaceable parts; neither is a "composition" in the definition's sense.
3. No actor class for a third party to a contract who is given an option under it (independents under K1).
4. `tax_share_borne` covers a mandate's burden by its definition ("a tax's (or a mandate's) burden"), which fit C-11; no change needed.

**(e) Test of 1.0.**
- *PC-17 1.0 (mandated term; who bears it).* The 1.0 clause (a charge refunded on the act) was not exercised. The main clause needed interpretation ((c) 1, (b) 1): the term is owed to third parties, the group had to be built for a product, and who bears it needed DM-1 and CR-7b (C-9, C-11).
- *PC-18 (prohibited term).* Worked as written (C-5, C-10, C-26, C-27).
- *RS-16 1.0 (per unit of quality; fixed costs per facility).* Worked as written, in the mirror case: dealer prices fall per job of a given kind though the average ticket may rise (C-7); independents' subscriptions bear on the number of shops, not on their price per job (C-8).
- *CR-5c.* Worked as written for three bargaining ranges: makers and buyers (C-9), makers and independents (C-1), makers and dealers (C-31).
- *CR-7c (makers' and repairers' values).* Worked as written: values move at passage, not at the effective date (C-23, C-26, C-27); CR-8's voiding clause gave both sides of the voided terms. CR-7's 1.0 "which stock" applied to independents (no permission: the gain follows the stock of repair capacity, C-25).
- *CR-12 1.0, what the response argument needs.* Worked as written for independents' returns (C-24, C-25): yields equalize with constant cost of entry (A-13), while the price per job stays below the counterfactual because inputs are cheaper.
- *EN-14 (effects on new-device design, exempt stock).* Failed ((c) 2): its conclusion for the exempt stock's value is wrong where buyers value the standard; overridden to `ambiguous` (C-28). Its "kept in use longer" conclusion applied to covered units, not exempt ones (C-29), for the same reason.

- *RS-7 1.0 and CR-4 1.0 (lobbying against a contested counterfactual).* Worked as written: right to repair was contested before (2023, 2025), so lobbying on it as such is not signed; questions only under the Act are (C-22).
