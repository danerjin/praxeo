# WE-066 Ban on student e-bikes at a private school, with staff exempt

Analyst: draft (Handbook 1.0, held-out test)  |  Rule catalog version: 1.0  |  Status: awaiting expert review

A stylized policy in a fictional town. Many schools restrict e-bikes; this one is not modeled on any of them. It is a held-out test of catalog 1.0 on a kind of rule none of the 1.0 changes were drawn from: a rule made by a **private** institution over its own land, binding students through the enrollment contract, rather than a rule made by the state. It probes whether the instrument typology (01_foundations.md, the Rothbard table; 02_ontology.md, 2.3) fits such a rule; CR-1's removal of an option over one's own property (an e-bike) in one place; substitution among commute modes (CR-4); relocation to the boundary (RS-4: parking off school land) and reclassification (RS-5: to pedal-assist under one reading, to e-scooters under both); timing between announcement and effect (RS-8: selling e-bikes early) and the resale price and value of e-bikes (CR-7c, CR-6 1.0 on small effects); car drop-offs and congestion at the gates as a `shared_facility` chain at Tier C against Tier B chains (CR-12 0.9 and 1.0, including what the response argument needs); bikes parked on neighbours' streets (`proximity`, Tier C, Q20); the public cycle stands as a fixed quantity at a zero price (CR-6 1.0, scope of the public-supply clause); enforcement cost against the right baseline (OC-3); lobbying on a question that was already contested (RS-7 1.0, CR-4 1.0); and local shops (bike shops, a newsagent that sells bus passes). The rule's stated aim, safety, is outside the scope of version 1: chains through collision risk are recorded as out-of-scope notes, never as signed consequences. The counterfactual throughout is **St Aldric's College without the e-bike rule**: the same campus, coaches, streets and Behaviour Code, with e-bikes allowed on school grounds as now. The probabilities are illustrative; the evidence column says what a real analysis would cite.

## 1. Policy record

**St Aldric's College Student Travel Rule 2027.** St Aldric's College is a private (independent, fee-paying) secondary school in Wendlemere, a town of about 46,000. It is not part of any public school system; the rule is made by its board of governors and enforced by its own staff. Fees are $18,600 a year, paid by term. The enrollment contract says that "the College may amend its rules from time to time, and parents agree that their child will abide by the rules in force", and that a child may be withdrawn on one full term's written notice (or a term's fees in lieu).

**The school.** 1,240 students aged 11 to 18 and 140 staff, on a 9-hectare campus at the north edge of town. The Main Gate opens on Quarry Lane; the East Gate on Kestle Road. Bike shelters inside the Main Gate hold 420 cycles; about 398 are used on a school day (270 student e-bikes, 110 student pedal bicycles, 18 staff e-bikes): about 95% of capacity.

**How students travel** (the school's travel survey, October 2026):

| Mode | Students | Share |
|---|---|---|
| E-bike | 270 (190 pedal-assist, with no throttle; 80 with a throttle that can propel the bike without pedalling) | 22% |
| Pedal bicycle | 110 | 9% |
| School coach (contracted) | 370 | 30% |
| Public bus (route 14) | 125 | 10% |
| Walking | 150 | 12% |
| Driven by car | 215 (in about 150 cars) | 17% |

E-bike riders live 3 to 12 km away (median 6 km); about 120 of them live on the Hollin Rise estate, 6 to 8 km north. For a 6 km trip, door to door: e-bike about 16 minutes; pedal bicycle about 24; school coach about 35 (routes 3 and 4 serve Hollin Rise); route 14 about 40, with a 20-minute headway; car about 15 plus the drop-off queue (about 8 minutes at 8:30 now). In a follow-up poll of e-bike riders (February 2027) asking what they would do if all e-bikes were banned, 30% said a pedal bicycle, 25% the school coach, 10% route 14, 20% being driven, 5% walking and 10% an e-scooter or "other".

**Coaches and buses.** Marrick Coaches runs nine school routes under a contract renewed each September: 57 seats a coach, 513 seats, 370 riders (about 72%). Routes 3 and 4 have 114 seats and 106 riders. Passes cost $420 a term, a price fixed in the contract for the school year; when a route is full the school allocates seats by a published priority (distance, then year group) and keeps a waiting list. Wendlemere Buses runs route 14 past the Main Gate at about 60% of peak capacity; a student term pass costs $165, sold at the bus station and at Dunnock News on the Quarry Lane parade (3% commission).

**The gates and the streets.** Cars drop off in a loop inside the Main Gate that holds about 20 cars; between 8:15 and 8:45 about 150 cars use it and the queue backs onto Quarry Lane, which the nine coaches also use to reach the coach bay (arriving 8:20 to 8:40). Between the fence and the Quarry Lane footway is a grass verge (the Verge) owned by the school. Opposite the Main Gate are two residential cul-de-sacs, Linnet Close and Orchard Row (64 homes, about half with a driveway or garage). The Quarry Lane parade, 250 m east, has six shops (Dunnock News, a bakery, a café, a pharmacy, a chip shop and a convenience store) and 24 free council cycle stands, of which about 8 are used in school hours. A council by-law lets the council remove cycles locked to street furniture other than stands, after notice; it is enforced on complaint.

**Bike shops and the used market.** Spokewright Cycles (Mardle Street, 600 m from the Main Gate) sells e-bikes, pedal bicycles and e-scooters and services them; about 30% of its service jobs are students' e-bikes. Corrow Cycle Works is 3 km away in the town centre. Students bought about 60 e-bikes in 2025-26 (35 new, 25 used), about 40 of them from the two shops. New pedal-assist e-bikes cost $1,500 to $2,400, throttle e-bikes $900 to $1,700, pedal bicycles $350 to $700. On many throttle models the throttle unplugs; Spokewright removes one for $40. Wendlemere's online marketplace lists about 25 used e-bikes a month in term time; a two-year-old pedal-assist sells for about $850, a throttle e-bike for about $550. About 40 student e-bikes a year are sold by leavers and upgraders in July to September. On 2 February 2027 Spokewright advertised a secure yard for 40 e-bikes at $4 a week.

**Why the rule.** In 2025-26 there were 7 collisions on campus involving student e-bikes (3 with pedestrians, 2 with cars in the staff car park, 2 between cycles; 5 involved throttle e-bikes); one student went to hospital. In October 2026 a parents' group, Safe Lanes, petitioned the board for a ban (310 signatures); the student council petitioned against (540 signatures). The board minuted in November 2026 that "the e-bike question will be revisited".

**The rule.** Announced by letter to families on 21 January 2027 (the Spring term began on 6 January). Effective on 19 April 2027, the first day of the Summer term (about 12.5 weeks later). The Summer term ends on 16 July; the next school year begins on 6 September 2027. The board will review the rule in June 2028.
- §1: "From the first day of the Summer term 2027, no student may ride or park an electric bicycle on school grounds."
- §2: "'Electric bicycle' means a bicycle fitted with a motor capable of propelling it. Ordinary bicycles may continue to use the shelters."
- §3: "'School grounds' means all land within the College's title, including the verge along Quarry Lane and the coach bay."
- §4: "This rule does not apply to members of staff."
- §5 (enforcement): staff on gate duty check arrivals at both gates; first breach, a one-hour after-school detention; second, the e-bike is confiscated and held in the site office until a parent collects it; third, a Saturday detention and a meeting with parents. Parents may appeal to the deputy head.

Existing rules that stay in the counterfactual: the Behaviour Code (e-bikes must be walked from the gate to the shelter and carry a free registration sticker; breach, a detention); Behaviour Code §14, "Scooters, including electric scooters, must be carried folded on school grounds and stored in lockers"; the coach contract and pass price; the council's cycle stands and by-law. Existing supervision: site staff spend about 150 hours a year managing the shelters and stickers and about 110 hours on e-bike incidents and walking-rule detentions (about 260 hours).

**Figures for the arithmetic.** Gate duty: two staff at each gate for 30 minutes in the morning and 20 in the afternoon on 190 days: about 630 staff-hours a year, plus about 120 hours of detentions and confiscations projected for the first year (about 750 hours). The ban makes unnecessary, under U1-a, about 90 of the 150 shelter hours (the shelters would hold about 130 cycles) and about 90 of the 110 incident and walking-rule hours (about 180 hours); under U1-b, less (about 30 and 35 hours), since pedal-assist e-bikes stay. Net: about +570 hours under U1-a and about +685 under U1-b. Car drop-offs: 20% of 270 riders is about 54 students, or about 40 more cars at 8:30 under U1-a (siblings share cars), against about 150 now; under U1-b, about 12 more cars. Coach demand on routes 3 and 4: about 120 riders on Hollin Rise, of whom 25% is about 30, against 8 free seats. Outlays: a coach pass is $1,260 a year and a route 14 pass $495; a car school run of about 12 km a day is about $500 a year in fuel; an e-bike costs about $150 a year to run (charging, tyres, servicing). Parking: about 60 to 80% of the riders who said "pedal bicycle", "coach" or "other" would in fact keep riding an e-bike to a spot outside the grounds (staff estimate); even 50 such bikes exceed the 16 free council stands.

Unclear provision:
- **U1, pedal-assist e-bikes.** §2 defines an electric bicycle as "a bicycle fitted with a motor capable of propelling it". Reading U1-a: every bicycle with a motor, including pedal-assist e-bikes, whose motor propels the bike while the rider pedals. Reading U1-b: only bicycles whose motor can propel them without pedalling (throttle e-bikes); pedal-assist e-bikes are "ordinary bicycles", as the national road law classes them (pedal cycles with assistance up to 25 km/h) and as §2's second sentence might suggest. The letter refers to "e-bikes such as those involved in last year's collisions" (five of seven were throttle e-bikes). The school has issued no guidance and has no practice yet. Under U1-a, 270 riders are bound; under U1-b, 80.

The Verge and the coach bay are school land and §3 names them, so parking just outside the gate on school land is covered under both readings; the boundary that matters is the College's title line. Whether an e-bike with its battery removed is still "fitted with a motor capable of propelling it" is a further question the text leaves open; it is noted, not analyzed.

Readings get no probability; where a sign depends on U1, one sign per reading is given.

## 2. Classification

| Component | Rothbard type | Binding? (assumption ID) | Rule families |
|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | None of the three. The rule is not an intervention by the state: it is made by the owner of the land, who is also one party to a continuing contract with each family that reserves to it the right to set rules. For each student who rides an e-bike, it removes an option over the student's own property in one place (CR-1, operation 1: riding or parking one's own e-bike on the College's land), and, where enforcement is imperfect, attaches a risk (detention, confiscation) to the option instead (CR-1, operation 3). It forbids no exchange, so it is not triangular; it takes or gives nothing, so it is not binary; it does not restrict the use of the student's property on the student's own land, so it is not autistic in the sense of `own_production_ban`. The relation through which it binds is a `contract` (02_ontology.md, 2.8: the enrollment contract). | Yes: 270 riders under U1-a, 80 under U1-b (A-1). | No pattern family. CR-1, CR-3, CR-4, CR-5c, CR-6, CR-7c, CR-9, CR-10, CR-12; RS-1, RS-3, RS-4, RS-5, RS-7 (1.0), RS-8, RS-9 (0.9), RS-12, RS-13, RS-16 (1.0), OC-4 |
| K2 | Administration by the rule-maker from its own budget for the year. | Yes | OC-3 (right baseline; 0.9 fixed budget) |

**The counterfactual law (CR-1, Recording).** Which operation K1 performs depends on the rule it replaces: the Behaviour Code, under which e-bikes may be ridden to the gate and walked to the shelter. The counterfactual is that Code, not "no rules". EN-7 (prohibition of a good moves trade underground) does not apply: no trade in e-bikes is forbidden, and e-bikes may be bought, sold and ridden everywhere except on the College's land.

**Shared facilities and proximity.** Quarry Lane at drop-off time, the coaches, the bike shelters and the footways are shared by their users: one more car in the queue slows every other vehicle in it, one more rider on a coach below capacity crowds the others. These are `shared_facility` relations, and bikes locked on neighbours' railings reach residents through `proximity` (02_ontology.md, 2.8). Under CR-9's 0.7 clarification every chain through them is Tier C, pending Q20; where such a chain opposes a Tier B chain on the same key, CR-12's 0.9 rule applies (two records; the Tier C chain never lowers the Tier B tier, and a dominance premise does not raise the Tier C one, CR-12 1.0). Congestion and crowding below capacity are not CR-6 rationing (CR-6, 1.0). The 24 free council stands are different: a fixed quantity at a zero price, where demand will exceed the quantity, so CR-6's public-supply clause does apply there.

**The parents who drive.** A parent who drives a child to school gives the child a ride; parent and child are linked by a `gift` relation (CR-5, 0.7; CR-9). The family chooses the mode together; costs fall on the parent.

C-1 is what the board intends: fewer student e-bikes on its land. It is signed on trips onto the grounds, not on e-bike use: the rule says nothing about riding elsewhere, and C-10 shows that many trips end just outside instead. Under U1-b, throttle riders who convert or swap to pedal-assist add pedal-assist trips (C-2, U1-b: increase; A-11), but each converted trip replaces one throttle trip at most, so the total of e-bike trips onto the grounds still falls (C-1, U1-b: decrease): a conversion cannot add more trips than it removes. Pedal-assist riders under U1-b face no operation at all (CR-1), so their travel time is `no_change` (C-3); under U1-a they fall back to a lower-ranked option (CR-3a), which for most is slower (A-25).

**Staff (§4).** The rule performs no operation on staff (CR-1). That is not a consequence by itself: no Tier B chain is derived for staff commuting that would establish no change, and the only Tier B chain that reaches them (cheaper used e-bikes, C-32, C-33) needs a premise below the cut-off (A-38). The chains that do reach them run through the shelters and Quarry Lane, at Tier C (C-49).

**Substitution among modes (C-4 to C-8).** Each e-bike rider now compares the options left (CR-4, x: the e-bike to the gate, removed; y: each other mode). Which mode each takes follows their own ranking, which the rules do not assume; that some take each mode is a premise per mode (A-2 to A-6), resting on the poll and the distances. Under U1-b only the 80 throttle riders are bound, so every flow is smaller but has the same sign. RS-1 gives the direction of each switch; for the coaches, whose seats and pass price are fixed for the year, the quantity on full routes is bounded by the seats (C-24). E-scooters are not "electric bicycles", and Behaviour Code §14 lets them be carried folded and kept in lockers, so a switch to an e-scooter is a move to an uncovered form that serves nearly the same end (C-8; RS-5). The car switch (C-7) is signed at Tier B "not counting effects through Quarry Lane"; its Tier C record is C-30.

**Converting to pedal-assist (C-2, C-9, C-18).** Under U1-b, a throttle rider can unplug the throttle ($40 on many models) or swap the bike for a pedal-assist one, and keep riding in (RS-5; A-11). Under U1-a, pedal-assist bikes are covered too, so conversion buys nothing: `no_change`. Throttle purchases by students fall under both readings (C-17); pedal-assist purchases fall under U1-a and rise under U1-b (C-18).

**Parking at the boundary (C-10).** The rule reaches only the College's land (RS-4, 0.9: the boundary that matters is the limit of enforcement, here the title line). A rider who values the e-bike trip enough keeps riding to a spot just outside and walks the last few hundred metres (RS-4; CR-4, x: park on the grounds, removed; y: park outside). §3 puts the Verge inside the boundary, so the spots are the council stands, Spokewright's yard, residents' driveways and street furniture (C-40 to C-44). Under U1-b, pedal-assist riders keep using the shelters; only throttle riders who do not convert park outside.

**Evasion and penalties (C-11, C-12).** Some riders take the risk: riding in through the East Gate after duty ends, leaving bikes behind the coach bay (RS-3; A-10). Against the counterfactual there was no such rule to breach, so breach rises from none wherever enforcement is less than certain. Those caught serve detentions; confiscated bikes are held until a parent collects them; time is used up (CR-8c). A confiscation that is returned on collection transfers nothing (CR-8e covers confiscations that are kept or destroyed); it is a penalty attached to the option of riding in (CR-1, operation 3).

**Time (C-13, C-14).** Riders who switch to a coach, route 14, a pedal bicycle or walking take longer door to door (A-25: 35 to 40 minutes against 16 by e-bike for a 6 km trip; 24 by pedal bicycle); so do those who park outside and walk in. For car switchers the trip may take no longer, so they are not counted in C-13. A parent who now drives spends time on the run: a gift of time to the child, chosen by the family (C-14).

**Selling and buying e-bikes (C-15 to C-18).** An e-bike whose main use was the school commute is worth less to its holder from the day the rule is announced (C-35, C-36). Some holders sell it; the sale is a stock adjustment that, for many, would have happened anyway when they left school or upgraded. So it is a timing shift (02_ontology.md, 2.5): sales by students rise from the announcement through the Summer term (C-15; RS-8, CR-10a; A-12). In July to September, two chains meet: leavers and upgraders who would have sold then have already sold (−), and riders who tried other modes through the Summer term and now give up the bike sell (+; TS-8, habits change over weeks). No premise ranks them (RS-8 0.9, more timing chains; CR-12b): `ambiguous` (C-16). Students who would have bought an e-bike, mostly Year 7 entrants and those upgrading, do not: those exchanges with shops and used sellers do not happen, and both sides lose the gain they expected (C-17, C-18; CR-5a, OC-4; A-14).

**Lobbying (C-19, C-20).** RS-7's If clause speaks of gains that "depend on political decisions"; here the decision-maker is the board of a private school. The move is the same (CR-4, x: accept the board's rule, y: spend means to change it), and `lobbying_effort` is defined as resources spent seeking to change, extend or keep the policy, so the pattern is used as written with that reading. By CR-4's 1.0 note and RS-7 (1.0), lobbying is signed against what would have been contested on the counterfactual path. The question of a ban was contested before the rule (two petitions; the board's minute), and would have stayed contested without it (A-19). So lobbying on the ban as such, by either side, is `ambiguous` (C-20): Safe Lanes no longer needs to press for a ban but may press to keep it; the student council and Ride to School press for repeal where they would have pressed against adoption. The questions C-19 names exist only under the rule (the reading of U1, a distance exemption, appeals against confiscation, the fairness of the staff exemption), so lobbying on them is an `increase` against none (CR-12, 0.9).

**Choosing the school (C-21, C-22).** The school sells a bundle: teaching, a campus and its rules (CR-5c: the terms include everything either party gives or receives). For families who live far away and relied on e-bikes, the bundle is worth less (A-22); for families who rank a campus without student e-bikes higher, it is worth more (A-21). That second chain is a choice made on the parents' own ranking, which may rest on what they believe about safety; it is signed as a choice (CR-2), not as a safety outcome. Both chains are at p ≥ 0.5, so applications for September 2028 and staying on into September 2027 are `ambiguous` (CR-12b; CR-12 0.8, kind 2). Withdrawal for September 2027 needs a full term's notice, so a family deciding to leave must give notice by 19 April, inside the announcement window.

**Coaches (C-23 to C-26, C-29, C-31).** Coach passes are sold at a price fixed in the contract for the year, so the price cannot ration (CR-6, 0.7: terms fixed by existing contracts count as regulated while they last). On most routes there are free seats: riders drawn from e-bikes are carried, and they crowd those already aboard through the shared vehicle (C-26, Tier C; CR-6 1.0: crowding below capacity is not rationing). On routes 3 and 4, about 30 new applicants meet 8 free seats (A-8), and the contractor cannot add a coach within the Summer term (A-9). Demand at the fixed price exceeds the seats, so the pressure goes to the margins open: the school's published priority (distance, then year group) and its waiting list. The record says that rule is used, so `allocation_by_non_price_criteria` is signed on its own (C-24; CR-6). Those refused take another mode. From September the contract is renegotiated: the contractor can add a coach on those routes (C-25; A-34). Whether the pass price for 2027-28 rises needs a premise on the contractor's cost of an added coach (CR-6, 0.9: splitting a shift), which the case does not supply (A-32, p = 0.45, below the cut-off).

**Quarry Lane at drop-off (C-28 to C-31).** About 40 more cars at 8:30 under U1-a (12 under U1-b) join a queue of about 150 (C-7, Tier B). Each car in the queue slows the others: that is the `shared_facility` relation, not an exchange, so the longer queue (C-28) and the delayed coaches (C-29) are Tier C (A-23, A-36). The longer queue makes driving worse for the families who chose it, which opposes C-7 through the shared facility, so CR-12's 0.9 rule applies: C-7 stands at Tier B, qualified, and the combined sign is recorded at Tier C (C-30). The combined sign is decided by CR-12e's response argument, with what CR-12's 1.0 note says it needs:
1. *The total it bounds.* School drop-off cars at 8:15 to 8:45. If their number fell back to its old level, the queue would be no longer than before, and every family that switched to the car at the old queue time would face the same choice again with the e-bike still removed; so the number would rise again. The deterrence responds to a queue that exists only because cars were added, and cannot reverse its cause.
2. *The analogue of constant cost.* At a given number of school cars the queue is no longer than without the rule: the school does not change the loop or the times in the Summer term, and nothing in the rule adds other traffic to Quarry Lane (A-24). If through traffic diverts away from the longer queue, the old number of school cars gives a shorter queue than before, which only strengthens the argument.
3. *Partial response.* By CR-12's 0.9 note the argument alone gives "increase or no change"; A-5 makes the response partial: at today's queue time, some families choose the car.
4. *Second responses.* Families who drop off earlier to beat the queue change the timing, not the number, of drop-offs; staff marshals, if the school adds them later, would be a second response to the same change and a second chain (excluded in the Summer term by A-24).

So C-30 is `increase` at Tier C. The dominance comes from the response argument; it settles the sign but does not raise the tier (CR-12, 1.0). The queue itself (C-28) is reached by the same added cars (+) and by any through traffic that diverts away (−), a response to the longer queue that cannot reverse it for the same reason; A-23 states that the response is partial. For coach rides (C-31), the Tier C chain through Quarry Lane pushes both ways: delayed coaches make the coach worse (−), but the car, the coach's main rival for the longer trips, sits in the same queue (+). The Tier C chain is kind 3, so the combined Tier C sign is `ambiguous` (CR-12, 0.8 and 0.9) while C-5 stands at Tier B, qualified. For riders who switch to pedal bicycles or walk, the queue only makes the car and coach worse, so the Tier C chain pushes C-4 the same way and no second record is needed; the same holds for switchers' travel time (C-13), which the queue lengthens further.

**The used e-bike market (C-32 to C-36).** Wendlemere's used market lists about 25 e-bikes a month; students' early sales add to the supply in the window (C-15), and students who would have bought used e-bikes withdraw (C-17, C-18). Most sellers sell locally (A-13). At the old price sellers are unsatisfied, so the price falls (RS-13; CR-6). The town market is part of a regional one reached online, so the effect may be small; CR-6's 1.0 clause says to sign it anyway: decrease for throttle e-bikes under both readings (C-32). For pedal-assist e-bikes, under U1-a the same two chains push the price down; under U1-b, throttle riders who swap for pedal-assist add demand and no pedal-assist holder is bound, so the price rises (C-33, U1-b; A-11). Prices are signed per model and age (RS-16, 1.0: per unit of quality), so that students selling older bikes is not read as a price change. Buyers who are not students buy more at the lower price (C-34). The holders' side: the value of an e-bike held on 21 January 2027 moves when the rule became expected (CR-7c, CR-10c), in the market by the price change, and, for a holder whose main use was the commute, by more by the holder's own ranking (CR-7, 0.9) (C-35, C-36; A-27). "Becomes expected" is a matter of probability (CR-7c): part of the move came in October and November 2026, as the petition and the board's minute raised the chance of a ban. Buyers' gain and sellers' loss on each sale are the two sides of one price (CR-8b).

**Bike shops (C-37 to C-41, C-51).** Students who switch to pedal bicycles buy them, partly locally (C-37; A-29). E-bike servicing for students falls with the e-bikes in use near the school (C-38; RS-12 in reverse, LW-10); under U1-b throttle removals add jobs while throttle servicing falls: `ambiguous`. Bikes parked on the street need better locks (C-39; RS-12). Spokewright's yard and residents' driveways sell a service that did not exist (C-40; CR-12 0.9: an increase against none). E-scooter sales rise (A-6). Against these, sales of e-bikes to students fall (C-17, and C-18 under U1-a). No premise ranks the chains for the shops' total (C-41, `ambiguous`). The extra demand for pedal bicycles and e-scooters raises their price per model by an amount that is likely very small, since the shops restock from a national distributor; CR-6's 1.0 clause signs it, with the premise that supply is not perfectly elastic within the term (C-51; A-37).

**Council stands and the streets (C-42 to C-46, C-50).** The 24 council stands are free and fixed in number, with about 16 free in school hours; riders parking off site exceed them (A-15). Here demand at the set price exceeds a fixed quantity, so CR-6's public-supply clause applies (CR-6, 1.0, scope): the pressure goes to the margins open to users, as a group: waiting for a stand to free up, searching, walking from farther away (C-42). Shoppers who used the stands share the loss. Some riders lock to railings and lamp posts against the by-law (C-43; A-17). Bikes on the footway narrow it for pedestrians (C-44, Tier C, the footway as a `shared_facility`). For neighbours, two chains reach their homes' value: those with a driveway or garage can rent space (C-40), a lasting yield while the rule lasts, which raises the value by a small amount (C-45, Tier B, qualified "not counting effects through proximity"; CR-6 1.0); and bikes on railings and students gathering in the cul-de-sacs reach every home through `proximity`, which may lower it (Tier C). No premise compares the two, so the combined Tier C sign is `ambiguous` (C-46; CR-12 0.9). Complaints bring council notices and removals (C-50); the complaints come from neighbours affected through proximity, so the chain is Tier C. The council's budget for this work is not changed; its funding is not signed further (depth 4).

**The parade (C-47).** Students who park at the parade walk past six shops twice a day; some buy on the way (RS-9, 0.9: purchases tied to a location; A-26). Whether the school canteen loses breakfast and snack sales to the parade is a premise below the cut-off (A-30, p = 0.4); RS-9's clause on new occupants does not apply, since the rule does not change who occupies the parade.

**Shelters and staff (C-48, C-49).** Under U1-a the shelters, now about 95% full, would hold about 130 cycles; under U1-b about 318. Space in them is shared by its users below capacity: more space for pedal cyclists and staff is a Tier C chain (C-48). Staff who drive now sit in a longer queue (C-28) and find the shelters emptier; some switch to e-bikes (C-49, Tier C; A-33). The cheaper used e-bikes (C-32, C-33) push the same way through an exchange relation, but that a staff member is at the margin for a few dollars is below the cut-off (A-38).

**Stopping points.** Fuel sellers, the coach contractor's drivers and suppliers, the families' other spending, and the council's other work: depth 3 to 4, diffuse; stopped. The parade shops' suppliers: stopped at depth 3.

**Out of scope: safety.** Version 1 has no variables for health or safety (02_ontology.md, 2.4). The rule's stated aim is that fewer student e-bikes on campus means fewer collisions on campus. Opposing chains exist and are recorded here only as notes, not as signed consequences: about 40 more cars at the gate (C-7, C-30); riders still ride e-bikes on the public roads to within a few hundred metres of the gate and then cross Quarry Lane on foot from the stands, the parade and the cul-de-sacs (C-10); e-scooters (C-8); converted pedal-assist bikes under U1-b (C-9); slower pedal bicycles (C-4); students walking a longer way at the end of the trip. Economic variables that would follow from a change in collisions (the school's liability insurance premium, time lost to injuries, repairs) are not signed either: no rule in the catalog derives a change in a price from a change in accident risk without a safety premise, so each is a Tier C note (CR-12, 0.7). What the analysis can sign is the choices people make on their beliefs about safety (C-21, C-22), not whether those beliefs are borne out.

## 8. Time structure

- **October 2026 to 20 January 2027: a rising chance of a ban.** The petition and the board's minute raise the probability of a ban; e-bike values move part of the way (CR-7c, probability clause). Few other actions: a trip cannot be stored.
- **21 January to 18 April 2027: the announcement window.** E-bike values move at once (C-35, C-36). - **19 April to 16 July 2027: the first term.** E-bike trips onto the grounds fall (C-1); car drop-offs, coach and bus rides, pedal bicycles and e-scooters rise (C-4 to C-8), and the queue lengthens (C-28 to C-30). ## 11. Report

**The policy.** From 19 April 2027, no student at St Aldric's College, a private secondary school in Wendlemere, may ride or park an electric bicycle anywhere on the school's land, including the verge outside the gate. Staff are exempt. Staff at the gates enforce it with detentions and, for a second breach, confiscation until a parent collects the bike. The rule was announced on 21 January 2027. It is unclear whether pedal-assist e-bikes, which have no throttle, are covered (U1-a) or only throttle e-bikes (U1-b): 270 riders are bound under U1-a, 80 under U1-b. Its stated aim, safety on campus, is outside the scope of this analysis (version 1 has no safety variables): nothing below says whether the rule makes anyone safer, and the costs below are not to be read as weighed against a benefit found to be absent.

**Visible consequences.** Fewer e-bike trips onto the school's land (C-1); under U1-b, more pedal-assist trips as throttle bikes are converted (C-2). More cars at the gate (C-7) and a longer morning queue on Quarry Lane (C-28, Tier C). Bikes parked just outside the grounds (C-10).

**Invisible consequences.**
- *Riders.* They switch to pedal bicycles, coaches, route 14, cars and e-scooters (C-4 to C-8); most who switch take longer (C-13, and C-3 for pedal-assist riders under U1-a); parents who drive spend time on the run (C-14). Some keep riding and park outside, where the free council stands run short (C-10, C-42) and some lock to railings (C-43). Some ride in anyway and serve detentions or have bikes confiscated (C-11, C-12). Families who now pay for passes or fuel have less to spend on other things (C-54).
- *E-bikes as assets.* E-bikes lose value from the announcement (C-35, C-36, except pedal-assist under U1-b). Students sell early (C-15), the used price falls (C-32, C-33) and other townspeople buy more (C-34). Students who would have bought e-bikes do not, and shops lose those sales (C-17, C-18).
- *Coaches and buses.* Coach and bus pass sales rise (C-23, C-27). On routes 3 and 4 seats run out and are allocated by the school's priority list (C-24) until a coach is added in September (C-25). Coaches are more crowded and delayed in the queue (C-26, C-29, Tier C).
- *Shops and streets.* Bike shops sell more pedal bicycles, locks and paid parking (C-37, C-39, C-40), less e-bike servicing (C-38); their total may rise or fall (C-41). Pedal bicycles and e-scooters cost very slightly more (C-51). The parade shops sell more to students who park there (C-47). Neighbours with driveways can rent them (C-40, C-45); counting the bikes on their railings, their homes' value may rise or fall (C-46, Tier C). Footways are narrower (C-44, Tier C) and the council handles complaints (C-50, Tier C).
- *The school.* Its enforcement work rises on balance, even against the shelter and incident work it no longer does (C-52), and comes out of other staff duties (C-53). Lobbying on the reading of U1 and on exemptions rises (C-19). Shelters have more room for pedal cyclists and staff (C-48, Tier C), and some staff may take up e-bikes (C-49, Tier C).

**Per reading of U1.** Under U1-a, every e-bike rider is bound, conversion buys nothing (C-9), and pedal-assist prices and values fall (C-33, C-36). Under U1-b, only throttle riders are bound; some convert (C-9), pedal-assist trips, purchases, prices and values rise (C-2, C-18, C-33, C-36), and the effects on cars, coaches and streets are smaller but have the same signs.

**Ambiguous effects.** Coach rides counting the queue (C-31, Tier C); students' e-bike sales in July to September (C-16); bike shops' servicing under U1-b and their total (C-38, C-41); neighbours' home values counting proximity (C-46, Tier C); lobbying on the ban as such (C-20); applications and enrollment (C-21, C-22), where families who prefer a campus without e-bikes and families who relied on them pull in opposite directions.

**Key conditions.** Enforcement at the gates (A-1, p = 0.8); the mode switches (A-2 to A-7); that more cars lengthen the queue (A-23, p = 0.85) and that the school does not change the drop-off arrangements in the first term (A-24, p = 0.65), on which the Tier C sign of car drop-offs rests; that the used market is local (A-13, p = 0.65).

**What this analysis cannot say.** How large any effect is; whether students, families or the school are better off on balance; and above all whether the rule makes anyone safer. Safety is the rule's stated aim and is outside the scope of version 1: the chains through collision risk, for and against, are listed as notes in section 6, not signed. A reader should not take the absence of a signed safety benefit as a finding that there is none.

## Gaps found

**(a) Calculus gaps (a step no core rule covers).** None new. Every step was derived from CR-1 to CR-12, including the rule-maker's own choice (CR-2) and the families' choice of school (CR-4, CR-5c). Chains through Quarry Lane, the coaches below capacity, the shelters, the footways and the neighbours' streets run over `shared_facility` and `proximity` relations: the open GAP-026-1, covered by CR-9's 0.7 clarification at Tier C pending Q20. The rule's stated aim (safety) is out of scope by the ontology's choice, not a gap in the calculus.

**(b) Unclear core rules.**
1. *CR-1, what a "policy" is.* The objects section defines a policy as "a change in the rules that others will enforce". Here the rule-maker enforces its own rule, on its own land, against parties who agreed in advance to abide by its rules. CR-1's four operations describe the rule's effect on students exactly (operation 1 where enforced, operation 3 where not), and its Recording note ("state the counterfactual law") worked with the Behaviour Code as the counterfactual law. But nothing in CR-1 says that the rule-maker may be a party to the relation through which the rule binds, or that "enforce" may mean the owner excluding a use from its own property. The calculus did not need to change; the definition should say "a change in the rules that some actor will enforce, including a private owner or a contracting party".
2. *CR-8e and a confiscation that is returned.* CR-8e covers seized goods that are kept (a transfer) or destroyed (used up). A bike held until a parent collects it is neither; it was read as a penalty attached to an option (CR-1, operation 3) whose cost is time (CR-8c). A clause would help.
3. *CR-12 0.9 where no Tier B record exists.* The 0.9 rule tells how to record a Tier C chain that opposes a Tier B chain. For exempt staff, the only Tier B link is "no operation" (CR-1), which is not a derived `no_change` on any variable (02_ontology.md, 2.5 allows `no_change` only where the derivation establishes it, and a Tier B chain through cheaper used e-bikes exists below the cut-off). So there is nothing to qualify, and the Tier C chain is recorded alone (C-49). The rule could say so.
4. *CR-6 0.7, existing contracts, for a price fixed for a year by a third-party contract.* The coach pass price is fixed by the school's contract with Marrick, not by the riders' own contracts. It was read as a regulated term while the contract lasts (C-24), and the clause's 0.8 note on contracts without a fixed term did not apply. The clause does not say whose contract counts.
5. *CR-12 1.0, the total the response argument bounds, where the shared facility carries other traffic.* The queue depends on all vehicles on Quarry Lane, but the argument was needed for school cars only. It works on school cars once the premise is that the rule adds no other traffic (A-24); diversion of through traffic away from the queue only strengthens it. The note's "state which total the argument bounds" was enough, but an example with a facility shared with unaffected users would help.

**(c) Missing or wrong patterns.**
1. *No pattern family fits a private rule.* EN-7 is written for a prohibition of a good and does not apply (no trade is forbidden); there is no pattern for a ban on using one's own durable in a named place. Every move was derived from the core rules and the RS patterns. Proposed candidate: **restriction on the use of an owned durable in one place** (x: use it there, removed; y: use it at the boundary, another mode, or another place). Then: relocation to the boundary (RS-4) with the boundary's free parking rationed (CR-6), substitution among modes per premise (CR-4), the durable's value falls at announcement by its holders' ranking and, small, in its market (CR-7c, CR-7 0.9, CR-6 1.0), early sales signed per window (RS-8), purchases of the durable prevented (CR-5a). Derivation: CR-1 op 1, CR-4, CR-6, CR-7c, CR-10a, CR-5a.
2. *RS-7's If clause speaks of "political decisions".* Lobbying a private board is the same move (CR-4, x: accept, y: spend means to change it); the pattern was applied with that reading. Applied as written, RS-7's main clause (harmed groups lobby, for repeal) would have signed opponents' lobbying `increase`; its 1.0 Unless clause, with A-19, re-signs it `ambiguous` (C-20). That is the clause working, not an override.
3. *OC-3's funding cases name only public budgets* (a budget that rises, a fixed budget, the policy's own revenue, a general budget). A private institution's budget funded by fees already set was read as the fixed-budget case (0.9), with a below-cut-off premise for a fee rise (A-31). A sentence saying that the cases apply to any budget, private or public, would help.
4. *RS-16 and new goods in a wider market.* RS-16 signs a substitute's price "where the substitute's supply cannot expand at an unchanged cost in the period"; CR-6 1.0 says to sign small effects. For pedal bicycles restocked from a national distributor, the two together give `increase` with a premise (A-37, p = 0.6). No sign was overridden, but RS-16 could cite CR-6 1.0 in its Assumes line.
5. No pattern, applied as written, gave a sign that had to be overridden, and no pattern's preconditions were met with a wrong conclusion.

**(d) Vocabulary.**
1. *Instrument type.* None of the types in 2.3 fits a rule made by a private owner for its own premises and binding through a continuing contract. Proposed: `private_premises_rule` (Rothbard type: none; not an intervention), with CR-1 and the RS family. The foundations' typology (autistic, binary, triangular) classifies coercion by the state and has no slot for it.
2. *Breach of a private rule.* `legal_breach` is defined as breach "within the legal market"; it was used for breach of the school rule (C-11), which is neither a law nor a market. A variable `rule_breach` (any rule an actor enforces), or a wider definition, would fit.
3. *Trips by mode.* No variable for trips or commutes by mode; `quantity_substitute` and `quantity_traded` were used with qualifiers, although no exchange takes place in a trip by e-bike or on foot.
4. *Amenity and nuisance.* As in earlier examples, no variable for what neighbours experience through proximity; their homes' value was used (C-46).
5. *Actor class for a rule-maker that is also a seller.* The school is the rule-maker, the enforcer and the seller of the bundle the rule changes; `officials.enforcement` was used for its enforcement role, and the seller role entered through `enrollment` and `applications`.

- *Candidate patterns from 6.2e.* "Ban on own production" (WE-026) was considered. Its precondition (a ban on producing a good or keeping an asset for one's own use) is not met: students may keep and use their e-bikes everywhere except on the College's land. Several of its moves recur (the asset loses value at announcement, purchases of the bought substitute rise, enforcement against the rule replaced), but they are reached here through a different operation (an option removed in one place), so this is not a valid second use and the candidate is not promoted. A new candidate is proposed in (c) 1. No other candidate was needed.
- *Does the method fit a private institution's rule?* Partly. The core rules fit without change: they are stated over any actor and pass the symmetry test for private as well as political actors, and every chain here was derived from them. The instrument typology does not fit: neither the Rothbard table nor 2.3 has a type for a rule an owner sets for its own premises through a contract the other party accepted, and the classification step had to say "none" and work from CR-1 directly ((d) 1). Two further differences matter for the reading of results. First, the rule-maker is also the seller of the thing the rule changes, so the families' exit and entry (C-21, C-22) are part of the analysis, as they never are for a state rule. Second, LW-5's corollary for prohibited exchanges does not carry over: no exchange is prohibited, and the families agreed in advance to the school's right to set rules, so the losers are worse off by their own ranking (CR-3a) under a contract they chose, not under coercion. The method's value-free signs are unaffected, but its foundations' language of "intervention" does not describe the case.
- *Does the method fit a policy whose main aim is out of scope?* It runs, but it gives a lopsided report. Every cost of the rule that works through exchange, time and means is signed; its intended benefit, fewer collisions, cannot be, and neither can the opposing safety chains (more cars at the gate, more crossings of Quarry Lane, e-scooters). The method can sign the choices people make on their beliefs about safety (families choosing the school, C-21, C-22), but not whether those beliefs are true. That is a correct application of the version 1 scope, and the report says so plainly; but a reader of the consequence list alone would see many invisible costs and no stated benefit. For a rule whose purpose is safety, the report must carry the out-of-scope statement in its first lines, not only at the end, and the experts may wish to require that whenever the stated aim of a policy is out of scope.
