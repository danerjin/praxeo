# WE-042 Aluminum tariff with export drawback

Analyst: draft (Handbook 0.8 held-out test, corrected under 0.9)  |  Rule catalog version: 0.9  |  Status: awaiting expert review

A stylized policy in a fictional country. It resembles real tariffs with "duty drawback" but is not modeled on any one of them. It is a held-out test of catalog 0.8: a tariff on a material (TX-7, DM-1) combined with a refund of that tariff to anyone who exports goods made with the material, on documentation. The refund is a payment conditioned on goods crossing the border, so it brings in SB-10 (an export subsidy, with round trips and a check on the return crossing), RS-4 (payments that depend on a crossing), RS-5 (the line between covered and uncovered aluminum, and between "imported" and other content), TX-8 in both directions of the currency link, CR-8b (who pays and who receives each transfer) and CR-12's three kinds of opposing chain. The unclear provision is whether domestic metal of the same grade can stand in for imported metal in a claim ("substitution drawback"). Health, safety and environmental effects (smelter emissions) are out of scope for version 1. The probabilities are illustrative; the evidence column says what a real analysis would cite. Written under catalog 0.8 and corrected under 0.9 after its core-only re-run: what changed is listed in "Corrections after the core-only comparison"; the test of 0.8 (Gaps found, e) is kept as written.

## 1. Policy record

**Vessland Aluminum Safeguard and Drawback Act.** Vessland is a trading country of about 9 million people with its own currency, the crown, and a floating exchange rate. It uses about 1.0 million tonnes (t) a year of primary aluminum and semi-finished aluminum products (ingot, billet, slab, plate, sheet, foil, rod, wire and extruded profiles), at a world price of about $2,500 a tonne. Two domestic smelters, with a combined capacity of 470,000 t and about 3,800 employees, produce about 400,000 t (85% of capacity); electricity is about 35% of their cost and is bought from the state grid. The other 600,000 t is imported. Vessland buys about 1.5% of the aluminum traded in the world.

About 1,200 firms use aluminum (rolling and extrusion mills, can makers, cable makers, makers of vehicle parts, window frames, machinery and heat sinks), employing about 60,000 people. The aluminum content of their exports is about 250,000 t a year: about 100,000 t of it imported metal and 150,000 t domestic metal. The remaining 750,000 t is used in goods sold in Vessland (500,000 t imported, 250,000 t domestic). Machined and stamped parts are cut from plate and billet and leave 25% to 40% of the metal as process scrap; cans, foil and cable leave 2% to 5%. Secondary producers (remelters) make about 120,000 t a year from scrap, and Vessland exports about 60,000 t of scrap; the domestic scrap price sits near the export price of scrap less about $40 a tonne of freight.

Vessland's main industrial region lies 30 to 60 km from the border with Ostrel, which has no tariff on aluminum. Goods cross the border with customs declarations; trucking costs about $30 a tonne each way.

The trade minister announced the measure on 3 February 2027. Parliament passed it on 20 May 2027, and it applies to aluminum entered on or after 1 September 2027. The tariff expires on 31 August 2031 unless Parliament extends it after a review in 2030.

Customs' static projection of revenue: 600,000 t × $500 = $300 million a year, less drawback of at most about 250,000 t × $495 = $124 million.

Unclear provisions:
- **U1, substitution.** K2 pays on goods made "using imported aluminum on which the tariff was paid". Reading U1-a (direct identification): the claimant must show that the metal on which the tariff was paid, or metal traced to it by lot, went into the exported goods. Reading U1-b (substitution): aluminum of the same grade (alloy and form) used in the exported goods within three years of an import qualifies against that import's entry, whatever the metal's origin, so domestic metal can stand in for imported metal. Customs has not issued guidance and no court has ruled. Readings get no probability; where a sign depends on one, each reading's sign is reported.

The counterfactual is Vessland without the Act: no tariff and no drawback, at the same world price.

**The transfers (CR-8b).** Three payments run through the Act, and each is recorded on both sides. (1) Domestic users pay smelters more for domestic metal: users' side in C-2 and C-41, smelters' side in C-1 and C-31. (2) Users pay the tariff on imported metal to the state, which keeps what it does not refund: users' side in C-2 and C-41, the state's in C-3 and the general fund's recipients in C-42. (3) The fund refunds tariff to exporters: the fund's side in C-4 (against Customs' ceiling, C-52), the exporters' in C-46, C-47 and C-51. The revaluations are recorded too: smelter owners gain (C-31), owners of home fabricators lose (C-32), holders of metal stocks gain (C-48); later buyers of these assets pay the new price (CR-7c).

**Smelters (C-1, C-5, C-6).** With imports still supplying most of the market (A-1), the domestic price rises to import parity, and smelters run their idle 70,000 t (A-4). Whether they add capacity beyond that is not reported: restarting or building a potline pays back over more than the tariff's four years, so it rests on owners expecting renewal (A-19, p = 0.35, below the cut-off).

**The drawback, reading by reading.** Under both readings an exporter pays about $3,000 a tonne for metal and receives $495 back after the claim is accepted, so its net cost is about $2,505 plus the claim's cost and the months between paying and being refunded (C-15, C-17; CR-10, LW-7).
- *U1-a.* Only traced imported metal qualifies. An exporter using domestic metal now pays $3,000 with no refund, against $2,505 net for traced imported metal. CR-4 (x: domestic metal, y: traced imported metal): those who can, switch to imported metal and keep it in separate, traced lots (C-13, C-16; A-20). Smelters lose those sales (C-14). They do not lose the tonnage: the freed metal is sold to home users at the same import-parity price, displacing their imports. The home market's imports (500,000 t) are far larger than the 150,000 t that moves, so this is arithmetic on the case's figures (CR-12e), and C-5 holds under U1-a. The two flows cancel one for one in total imports, so C-8 is unchanged in sign; what changes is who buys which metal, at a cost in tracing and transport (C-16). Exporters who cannot switch (long contracts, alloys only the smelters make) bear the whole tariff on their content (C-49).
- *U1-b.* Domestic metal of the same grade qualifies against any import entry, the claimant's own or one designated to it. Exporters have no reason to change supplier (C-13 `no_change`), and smelters keep their sales to exporters at the higher price; the tonnage follows export output, which is `ambiguous` (C-14, C-36). Importers can sell designations; there are far more import entries (about 600,000 t a year, less after C-8) than export content (250,000 t), so by CR-6 the price of a designation falls toward the cost of preparing it, and exporters keep almost all of the refund. No sign is given for importers' receipts from designations. Exporters of domestic metal are then claimants like any other, and the claimant groups below differ in sign, so for the class the net cost is `ambiguous` (C-49).

**Whether the drawback is an export subsidy (C-17, C-18, C-50).** The refund is computed on a standard: the yield tables count process scrap as aluminum used, and a claimant may use them or its own records "at its option". By CR-3's 0.9 clarification, claimants are split by their position against the standard, and by SB-10's 0.9 limits the refund is an export subsidy only where it exceeds the tariff paid on the metal used, net of the cost of holding the tariff until it is refunded. The holding cost is part of the net price (CR-3, note; LW-7); claim costs are a different variable (C-15) and are not netted in (CR-12c). Three groups, per tonne of product and against the counterfactual:
- *Low-scrap exporters at or above the tables' yield (C-17).* A can or cable maker with 3% scrap pays 1.03 × $500 more for metal, gets 1.03 × $495 back months later, and sells 0.03 t of scrap for at most about $80 more. The unrefunded $5.15 alone exceeds the most the scrap can bring ($2.40), before the holding cost is added: arithmetic on the case's figures (CR-12e). The net cost rises.
- *Low-scrap exporters who beat the tables (C-50).* One whose actual scrap is two points below the table's is refunded on metal it did not use, about 0.02 × $495 ≈ $10 a tonne of product. That exceeds the $5 unrefunded but not clearly the $5 plus the cost of holding about $515 for several months. Opposing chains and no dominance premise: `ambiguous` (A-28).
- *High-scrap exporters (C-18).* A maker of machined parts uses 1.35 t of plate per tonne of product and sells 0.35 t as scrap. It pays 1.35 × $500 = $675 more for metal, gets 1.35 × $495 = $668 back after the claim is accepted, and sells its scrap for 0.35 × Δs more, where Δs is the rise in the scrap price (C-27). The 0.8 version of this example signed the net cost down once Δs exceeds about $19, but that threshold covers only the unrefunded 1%. Holding $675 from import through manufacture, export, the claim and up to 120 days after acceptance costs, at an illustrative 5% to 8% a year over four to nine months, roughly $10 to $40 a tonne of product. That raises the threshold to between about $50 and well above the case's range of about $80 (A-6). Claimants who beat the tables (A-28) push the same way as the scrap. No premise establishes which side dominates, so the net cost is `ambiguous` (CR-12b).
So no class is shown to receive an export subsidy, and SB-10's conclusions are not applied to these exports. Its domestic-price clause would not apply in any case: these parts are not covered by K1, so domestic buyers can import them untaxed and imports cap the domestic price (SB-10, 0.9 limits); sales move abroad and imports replace them at home (C-19, C-20, C-25).

**Home and export sales (C-19 to C-22).** A firm making uncovered goods sells at home against tariff-free imported goods and abroad at world prices. Its metal costs about 20% more for both, but only export sales get the tariff back. RS-14: its home competitors (imports) are exempt, so the quantity effect dominates at home (C-21, C-22). CR-4 and CR-7a: home sales now bear about $500 a tonne of metal and export sales at most the unrefunded 1% and the holding cost, so the yield of export sales has risen relative to home sales and mixed firms move sales toward export (C-20 down, C-19 up). The export option itself is made slightly worse or better (C-17, C-18, C-50: an `ambiguous` chain), so C-19 needs dominance: arithmetic on the case's figures, about $500 against about $5 plus the holding cost, with capacity specific to aluminum work and so not withdrawn (A-15; CR-12e). This chain does not need A-6. Whether they can sell more abroad at world prices is A-8. The currency chain (C-35) reaches both home and export sales and is itself `ambiguous`; section 7 combines it (A-29).

**Timing (C-9, C-10).** Users can import untaxed until 31 August 2027 and store the metal (RS-8, A-21). Imports rise in the window and fall in the months after, when stocks are drawn down; the later fall has the same sign as C-8 and is part of it. No drawback is paid on pre-tariff metal, since no tariff was paid on it, so the window brings no claim forward (RS-8's Unless clause on claims acquired before a charge does not apply).

**Reclassification (C-12, C-13).** K1 draws two lines. One is between covered metal and uncovered fabricated forms: importers bring in profiles cut to length and drilled as "parts of structures", or sheet formed into containers, where the work costs less than $500 a tonne (RS-5, TX-4; A-7). The other, under U1-a only, is between imported and domestic content: firms reorganize supply and stocks so that imported lots are the ones used for export (C-13). Under U1-b that line does not exist.

**Round trips (C-23).** A firm near the border could import plate (pay $500 a tonne), make window frames or parts, export them to Ostrel, claim $495, and bring the same goods back. Finished goods enter untaxed, so the round trip would deliver the metal to Vessland users without the tariff, for about $60 a tonne of freight (RS-19, round trips under a payment for crossing a boundary, promoted in 0.9; RS-4; SB-10). K3 checks the return crossing: a declared re-import repays the drawback, so declared round trips gain nothing and are not made. Undeclared ones keep the refund unless an audit finds them, which costs the refund plus 20% (CR-1, operation 3). CR-4 (x: no round trip, y: an undeclared round trip): where the gain ($495 less freight) exceeds the probability of detection times the repayment and penalty, some firms make them. Goods without serial identity are hard to trace (A-9), and bulky goods made near the border make the trip cheap (A-10). Every remaining round trip is a breach of K3 within the legal market, so it is recorded as `legal_breach` (C-23). The shipments themselves are recorded as `quantity_traded` across the border (C-57, RS-19): an activity that exists only under the policy, signed as an increase in the flow of shipments per period against none (CR-12, 0.9), and the trucking is used up (CR-8c). The check is a burden on the round-trip option, not an opposing chain: it lowers round trips from what they would be, never below the counterfactual of none (SB-10, 0.9 limits; RS-19).

**Lobbying (C-24).** The tariff gives smelters a concentrated gain that depends on renewal in 2030 and on Customs' reading of U1; users and exporters bear concentrated costs. Each lobbies (RS-7, A-11). The counterfactual has no tariff to defend or attack after May 2027, so the change is an increase for each.

Not reported: content overstated in claims beyond the yield tables (`legal_breach`). The tables are generous enough that most claimants have no reason to go beyond them (A-25, p = 0.4).

**Scrap (C-27 to C-29).** Users turn from primary metal to scrap (C-11). Scrap was being exported at the export price less freight, so as home demand rises, exports fall first (C-29) and the domestic price rises toward the price of imported scrap, a range of about $80 a tonne; remelters' output rises (C-28; RS-16). One chain pushes the other way: any expansion of high-scrap exports driven by the scrap allowance would add process scrap at home (RS-13). Such an expansion pays only while the scrap price stays well above its old level (section 5), so it cannot bring the scrap price back to its old level. CR-12e's response argument applies, because that response is driven by the scrap price itself: the tariff and its refund cancel except for the unrefunded 1% and the holding cost, so no second change drives it. Mixed firms' move from home to export sales (C-19) moves sales on existing, specific capacity (A-15) rather than adding output, so it is not a separate source of scrap. By CR-12's 0.9 clarification the response argument alone gives only that the price stays at or above its old level; `increase` needs a partial-response premise, and A-6 supplies it: home demand outruns the 60,000 t now exported, after which scrap cannot be supplied at the old price. The combined sign is an increase. The remelters' price rises with it (C-53): demand for secondary metal rises (C-11), and their scrap costs more (C-27), so their supply cannot expand at the old cost (CR-6, 0.9, splitting a shift).

**Capitalization (C-31, C-32).** The tariff raises the expected earnings of the smelters and lowers those of firms making uncovered goods for the home market, for four years and longer if renewed. Both are capitalized when the change becomes expected: part of the way from the announcement in February 2027, as passage became likely, and the rest at passage in May (CR-7c, a change of limited duration). Exporters' values are not signed: under U1-b their costs barely change, and under U1-a it depends on whether each can switch. Holders of covered metal in Vessland when the tariff becomes expected gain as well (C-48): their stocks, including those bought in the window (C-10), can be used or sold at import parity after 1 September (CR-7c, CR-10c; inventories are durable assets in CR-7's sense, 0.9).

**Buyers (C-33, C-34).** Where domestic buyers can import the finished good untaxed, the domestic maker cannot pass the cost on (C-21, C-39). Where they cannot (goods installed on site, bulky containers for local bottlers), the cost passes through (RS-14, A-16). Foreign buyers of parts gain where Vessland's mixed firms and parts makers sell more abroad (C-19) and are a material share of their supply (A-27): the regional price falls (C-34; CR-6, 0.7, a wider-market price) and regional parts makers receive less (C-55). The added export work is a group, jobs or hours (C-56; OC-5), and is not netted against the home-market loss (C-22).

**Imports in total (C-37).** Chains: (−) home users buy less (C-7) and smelters supply more (C-5); (+) under U1-a, exporters switch 150,000 t to imported metal (C-13). The switch is matched one for one by smelter metal displacing home users' imports (section 5), an accounting identity used as a dominance premise (CR-12e). Decrease under both readings. C-8 records the same fact for importers, and C-54 the foreign smelters' side.

**Drawback against Customs' ceiling (C-52).** Customs' $124 million is 99% of the tariff on 250,000 t of export content. In the first year under U1-a, only traced imported metal qualifies: about 100,000 t of content, about 135,000 t of metal even at 1.35 t per tonne of product, until exporters switch over the year (A-20); metal stocked before 1 September paid no tariff and earns nothing (A-21). Arithmetic on the case's figures: below the ceiling. Under U1-b all content qualifies and the tables count process scrap, which pushes claims above the ceiling; but not every exporter claims (A-26 says most do), and total exports are not signed (C-36). Opposing chains and no dominance premise: `ambiguous`. In the medium term the same chains apply under both readings, so no sign is reported.

**The currency link, both ways (C-35).** TX-8: fewer imports of metal leave foreigners with fewer crowns to spend on Vessland's exports. But K1 also raises imports of finished goods (C-25, C-26) and, through the drawback, may raise some exports (C-19), which gives Vessland's residents more foreign means and so draws more imports (TX-8, mirror case). Whether Vessland's total imports rise or fall depends on whether the fall in metal imports outweighs the rise in finished goods, which the case's figures do not settle. So the currency chain is itself `ambiguous` (TX-8, 0.9: a change in the form of imports). Section 7 combines it with the other chain reaching other exporters, and with the chains reaching fabricators' home and export sales.

**Stopping points.** Buyers of power from the state grid (C-30): the grid's tariff is regulated, and whether added smelter load raises it at the next review (PC-15) is depth 3 and not followed. The spending of users who pay more (C-41): a change in means alone, uses not named (CR-4). Workers moving from home fabricators to smelters and exporters (C-6, C-22): depth 3, not followed.

## 8. Time structure

- **Announcement to effective date (February to August 2027).** Smelter and fabricator values move from February, part of the way, and the rest at passage in May (C-31, C-32). Users import and store untaxed metal (C-9, C-10), and holders of metal gain (C-48). Smelters prepare to restart idle capacity (C-5). Exporters and Customs prepare claims procedures; under U1-a, exporters renegotiate supply contracts with smelters to switch to imported metal from September (C-13).
- **First months.** Imports fall below the counterfactual as stocks are drawn down (C-8). The first refunds arrive only after claims are prepared and accepted, so exporters carry the tariff for several months (C-17, C-18, C-46, C-47); that holding cost is what leaves high-scrap exporters' net cost `ambiguous`. In the first year, drawback stays below Customs' ceiling under U1-a (C-52).
- **2027 to 2031.** Home fabrication of uncovered goods shrinks and finished-goods imports and Ostrel fabrication grow (C-21, C-25, C-26) as firms redeploy capital (CR-7b with CR-10b), while mixed firms move sales abroad (C-19, C-56); so the burden on home fabricators is largest at first and shifts toward their buyers only where imports cannot replace them (C-39, C-33). Round trips grow as firms learn which goods audits miss (C-23, C-57).
- **Renewal in 2030.** The tariff's expiry limits what is capitalized (C-31) and what smelters invest (A-19 below the cut-off). Lobbying centers on the review and on U1 (C-24).
- **Erosion.** The tariff is ad valorem, so it does not erode with prices; the drawback is 99% of the tariff paid, so it moves with it. The yield tables fix scrap ratios in physical units, so what the scrap allowance is worth moves with the scrap price (A-6), not with the metal price.

## 11. Report

**The policy.** From September 2027 Vessland taxes imported aluminum metal and semi-finished products at 20% for four years. Exporters of goods made with imported aluminum can claim back 99% of the tariff on the metal used, on documentation, and goods re-imported after a refund must repay it. Scrap and finished goods are not taxed. Whether domestic metal of the same grade can stand in for imported metal in a claim is not settled.

**Visible consequences.** Smelters get a better price and run their idle capacity (C-1, C-5); their workers get more work (C-6). The state collects revenue (C-3) and refunds part of it to exporters (C-4). Users pay about 20% more for metal (C-2).

**Invisible consequences.**
- *Users and their buyers.* Users buy less metal (C-7) and more scrap (C-11), and reshape imports into forms the tariff does not cover (C-12). Makers of finished goods for the home market, competing with untaxed imports, cannot pass the cost on: they produce and employ less (C-21, C-22), their firms lose value (C-32), and imports and Ostrel plants take their place (C-25, C-26). Where buyers have no imported alternative, they pay more (C-33). Users who keep buying have less for other things (C-41).
- *Exporters.* For most, the refund leaves their metal cost slightly higher than without the policy, after the unrefunded 1% and the months of waiting, and the claims cost paperwork (C-15, C-17, C-46). For makers of machined parts, whose process scrap is counted in the claim and sold at a higher scrap price, and for claimants whose plants beat Customs' yield tables, whether the drawback leaves them better or worse off is ambiguous once the cost of waiting for the refund is counted (C-18, C-47, C-50, C-51). Firms that sell both at home and abroad move sales from home to export (C-19, C-20), with more export work (C-56); some foreign buyers pay less (C-34) and regional parts makers receive less (C-55).
- *Depending on the reading.* If only traced imported metal qualifies, exporters of domestic metal pay the whole tariff until they switch (C-49), then switch to imported metal and keep separate stocks (C-13, C-16), and smelters sell less to exporters (C-14) but the same tonnage overall, to home users (C-5); in the first year drawback stays below Customs' ceiling (C-52). If substitution is allowed, none of this happens.
- *Round trips.* Some goods are trucked to Ostrel to claim the refund and brought back undeclared (C-23, C-57).
- *Other markets.* Scrap and secondary metal rise in price (C-27, C-53); remelters produce more (C-28) and less scrap is exported (C-29); the grid sells more power to smelters (C-30); foreign smelters sell less to Vessland (C-54). Smelter owners and holders of metal stocks gain value from the announcement (C-31, C-48). With Customs' budget fixed, refunds, clearance or other checks are slower or fewer (C-44). Each side lobbies over renewal and over the reading (C-24).

**Ambiguous effects.** Total exports of aluminum-containing goods (C-36) and the exports of Vessland's other industries (C-35): the policy moves imports both ways (less metal, more finished goods), so the currency link can push either way. The net metal cost and resources of high-scrap exporters and of claimants who beat the tables (C-18, C-47, C-50, C-51): the refund on scrap or on metal not used against the unrefunded 1% and the cost of waiting. Per reading: smelters' sales to exporters (C-14), exporters of domestic metal under substitution (C-49), and drawback against the ceiling under substitution (C-52).

**Key conditions.** Imports remain the marginal supply (A-1, p = 0.9). The shift of fabricators' sales from home to export, and the losses at home, rest on that shift outweighing any movement of the exchange rate (A-29, p = 0.6). The scrap price rises only if home demand outruns current scrap exports (A-6, p = 0.65). Round trips rest on weak audits of goods without serial identity (A-9, p = 0.65). That foreign smelters bear almost none of the tariff rests on Vessland being a small buyer (A-14, p = 0.9); if they bore part, every claimant would gain (A-18, p = 0.15).

**What this analysis cannot say.** How large any effect is; whether smelter jobs gained outweigh jobs lost in fabrication; whether the drawback leaves high-scrap exporters better or worse off than free trade; whether the tariff or the drawback is desirable; effects of smelting on emissions and health (out of scope for version 1).

## Gaps found

**(a) Calculus gaps (a step no core rule covers).** None. Every step was derived from patterns or from CR-1 to CR-12. The nearest was foreign retaliation (section 7), which is a chain no rule derives and is recorded as a Tier C note under CR-12's 0.7 clarification, not a gap.

**(b) Unclear core rules.**
1. *CR-12b and a check on the same option.* SB-10 says to combine the round-trip chain with the check under CR-12b. Here the check does not produce a chain with an opposite sign on `legal_breach`: without the policy there are no round trips, so the check can lower the increase but not reverse it. It is a burden on the same option (CR-1, operation 3), and the sign comes from CR-4 with an enforcement premise. CR-12 could say that a provision that only burdens the response is not an opposing chain.
2. *CR-12e, "a second change the policy also causes"* (still open in 0.9; the holding cost added under 0.9 does not change the answer, since it too acts only through the refund). For the scrap price, the expansion of high-scrap exports is driven by the scrap price and by the refund on scrap. The argument holds only because the tariff and the refund cancel except for 1%, so the scrap price is the only thing that changes. Deciding whether the refund counts as a "second change" took arithmetic; the clarification gives no test.
3. *CR-1, 0.8 refund attached to a thing.* It applied cleanly to the drawback (operation 3 on the option of exporting a good with qualifying content), although its example is a container deposit.

**(c) Missing or wrong patterns.**
1. *SB-10's preconditions and domestic-price clause* (addressed in 0.9 by SB-10's limits clause). SB-10's "If" is a fixed sum per unit exported; a drawback is a refund of a tax that varies with content. The pattern was applied only to the part of the refund that exceeds the tariff borne (the scrap allowance), which had to be found by arithmetic first. Its conclusion that the domestic price rises toward the export price plus the subsidy was wrong for these goods and was overridden: the goods are not taxed at import, so tariff-free imports cap the domestic price, and the result is that sales move abroad while imports replace them at home (C-20, C-25). SB-10 assumes, without saying so, that domestic buyers cannot import the good more cheaply than the domestic price; a clause for an open import side is needed.
2. *Duty drawback* (candidate). If a tax on an input is refunded on the content of exported goods, then: (i) relative to no policy, exporters' net input cost rises by the unrefunded share, the claim cost and the time value of the wait (CR-1, CR-8b, CR-10); (ii) where the refund rule counts more than the content actually exported (scrap allowances, standard yields), the excess is an export subsidy (SB-10); (iii) under direct identification, input flows are reshuffled between exporters and home users with no change in totals (CR-4, CR-12e identity); (iv) round trips arise unless the return crossing is checked (RS-4, SB-10). Derivation: CR-1, CR-4, CR-7a, CR-8b, CR-12e. Seen once. Under 0.9, (ii) must also net the cost of holding the tariff until refunded (SB-10, 0.9 limits); with that, no class in this case is shown to receive a subsidy.
3. *TX-7's input users.* TX-7 says input users "face higher costs"; whether they can pass them on depends on whether their outputs are covered by the tariff (effective protection). RS-14 decided it here, with the exemption of finished goods as the decisive fact. A cross-reference in TX-7 would help.

**(d) Vocabulary.**
1. *No instrument type for a refund of a tax conditioned on export.* K2 was classified as a binary refund under CR-1's 0.8 clarification; `deposit_refund` and `export_subsidy` each fit only part of it.
2. *No variable for exports as such.* Exports were recorded as `quantity_supplied` with a qualifier (C-19, C-35, C-36). Outcome cards would be easier to score with `quantity_exported`.
3. *Rights to claim a refund.* Designations of import entries under U1-b are tradable rights the policy creates. `right_lease_price` and `asset_value` fit badly, since the rights are used once; no sign was given (section 5).
