# WE-047 Sugar tariff-rate quota replacing a flat tariff, with tradable licences allocated by history

Analyst: draft (Handbook 0.9 held-out test, corrected under 1.0)  |  Rule catalog version: 1.0  |  Status: awaiting expert review

A stylized policy in a fictional country. Many countries import sugar under tariff-rate quotas; this one is not modeled on any of them. It is a held-out test of catalog 0.9 on a case none of the 0.9 changes were drawn from. It probes EN-12 (allocation of a right by history, with the base period fixed before the proposal), EN-13 (consolidation under tradable rights), EN-6 with its 0.9 Inputs clause and DM-4 (who captures the quota rent), TX-7, TX-8's 0.9 clause on a change in the form of imports, CR-12's 0.9 note on what the response argument gives (domestic supply expanding behind the quota), CR-1's 0.9 clause on one provision performing different operations either side of a threshold, and SB-4 capitalization. A tariff-rate quota is a tariff with two rates; the case also tests whether TX-7, written for a single rate, gives the right sign when the lower rate is cut and the higher one raised. The unclear provision is whether licences unused by 30 September are reallocated or lapse. The counterfactual throughout is **the flat 25% tariff stays in force**: every sign is relative to that path, not to free trade. Effects on health and nutrition are outside the scope of version 1. The probabilities are illustrative; the evidence column says what a real analysis would cite.

## 1. Policy record

**Kestrany Sugar Import Regime Act.** Kestrany consumes about 1,750,000 tonnes (t) of sugar a year, about 60% by food and drink manufacturers and 40% by households. Domestic beet sugar supplies about 1,100,000 t, from about 4,000 growers on about 120,000 ha and two processors (Ardmouth Sugar and the Pellmont Beet Co-operative) with six mills running at about 90% of capacity. Imports averaged about 650,000 t a year in 2023 to 2025: about 450,000 t of raw cane sugar refined at two port refineries in Vellport, and about 200,000 t of white sugar. Kestrany takes about 1.2% of world sugar trade, bought from many exporters. World prices (illustrative): raw $360 a tonne, white $440. Under the existing flat 25% ad valorem tariff, white sugar lands at about $550 a tonne, which sets the domestic white price; tariff revenue is about $62.5 million a year. Nine firms imported in 2023 to 2025: the two Vellport refiners (66% of imports), four trading houses (25%), and three manufacturers that import directly (a soft-drink bottler and two confectioners, 9%). Kestrany's food manufacturers export about a fifth of their output, and imported confectionery, biscuits and soft-drink concentrates (not covered by the sugar tariff) hold about a quarter of the home market. Two wet mills make glucose syrup from domestic maize. Kestrany's main exports are timber, machinery and tourism.

The bill, including the base period, was published on 2 March 2026, passed on 10 June 2026, and takes effect for the quota year beginning 1 January 2027 (ten months after it was proposed). No expiry; the quota volume is reviewed in 2031.

Existing rules that stay in the counterfactual: the flat 25% tariff on raw and white sugar; customs classification of sugar mixtures; the general budget, which receives the tariff.

Unclear provision:
- **U1, surrendered licences.** §9 surrenders licences not used by 30 September. §4(2): "The tariff quota shall be allocated in full in each quota year." §12: "The Minister may make rules for the allocation of quantities among registered importers on application." Reading U1-a: surrendered quantities return to the quota and must be allocated again in the same year (§4(2)), to registered importers on application under §12. Reading U1-b: surrender cancels the licence; §4(2) refers to the December issue, and the surrendered quantity lapses for that year. The ministry has not said which it will apply.

Readings get no probability; where a sign depends on U1, one sign per reading is given.

**Figures.** At the old landed price ($550 a tonne, white), Kestrany imports about 650,000 t, 250,000 t more than the quota. So with the quota in place the domestic white price must rise above $550: either to the point where import demand falls to 400,000 t, or, if it still exceeds 400,000 t there, to the out-of-quota landed price of about $704 (440 × 1.60), with out-of-quota imports at the margin. In-quota white sugar lands at about $449 (440 × 1.02). The gap per tonne between the domestic price and the in-quota landed cost (the quota rent) is therefore more than $100 and at most about $255 for white sugar; at an illustrative domestic price of $650 it is about $200, about $80 million a year on 400,000 t, and capitalized at 8% a share of the whole quota would be worth about $1 billion. Tariff revenue: in-quota about $3.2 million a year; out-of-quota about $264 a tonne of white sugar. If out-of-quota imports stay below 200,000 t (A-25), total revenue is below about $56 million, against $62.5 million now. Importers' trading margin is about $15 a tonne. Without the act, imports would continue at about 650,000 t, spread evenly through the year (about 160,000 t a quarter).

**Which rate binds.** TX-7 is written for one tariff rate. A tariff-rate quota has two, and only one of them sets the price: the rate on the marginal tonne. Because import demand at the old price exceeds the quota (A-1), the marginal tonne either pays 60% or is not imported for want of a licence. The 2% rate applies to the 400,000 inframarginal tonnes and decides who receives the gap between the domestic price and their landed cost, not the price. Applied to the 2% rate as if it were the only rate, TX-7 would give a lower price for buyers; applied to the marginal rate, it gives a higher one (Gaps found, c1). Catalog 1.0 now says this: where a quota fixes the quantity that pays a low rate, the price is set at the margin by the higher rate as long as demand at the old price exceeds the quota (CR-6, 1.0: a quota on a rate), and TX-7 is applied at the marginal rate, the in-quota rate only dividing the gap between the state and the holders (TX-7, 1.0; EN-6, 1.0).

**The domestic price (C-5, C-6; CR-12, 0.9).** At the old price, buyers wish to import about 650,000 t (A-1), but only 400,000 t can enter at a landed cost near $449; more must pay 60%. So at $550 the quantity buyers wish to buy exceeds what can be supplied at that price, and the price rises (CR-6): to the point where import demand falls to 400,000 t, or to the out-of-quota price of about $704 with out-of-quota imports at the margin (CR-7a). Domestic growers and mills receive the higher price (C-6, TX-7) and expand (C-7), which lowers what must be imported. CR-12e's response argument bounds this: the expansion is a response to the higher price and stops once yields are equal, so it cannot bring the price back below $550 (if it did, the expansion would not pay). By CR-12's 0.9 note, that gives "at or above the old price"; to report `increase`, the response must be partial. A-3 supplies that premise: beet rotations, the mills' haul radius and their capacity (about 90% used) prevent domestic supply from replacing 250,000 t at the old cost within five years. CR-12's 1.0 note adds three requirements. (1) The total bounded: the price of white sugar to Kestrany's buyers from all sources together (domestic sugar and imports, in-quota and out-of-quota); demand-side responses (glucose syrup, imported sugar goods) arise only above $550 and stop there too, so they are bounded by the same argument. (2) Constant or rising cost: the cost of the marginal domestic tonne does not fall as domestic output expands (land within the haul radius and mill capacity are limited; A-30), so the expansion cannot restore equal yields below the old price. (3) A stock built in advance is a second chain: the sugar imported at 25% in 2026 (C-18, C-19) is released into 2027 and pushes the price down while it lasts. Its holders bought at about $550 to sell later at more, and sell it over the months in which it fetches most; with carrying costs small against the rise expected once it runs out, the price in the drawdown months stays above the old level (CR-7a across time, CR-10a; A-29), and the stock, limited by port storage, runs out within 2027. With A-29 as a dominance premise (CR-12e, empirical), C-5 holds from 1 January 2027. The in-quota imports at 2% do not oppose C-5: they are inframarginal, and their lower cost goes to the licence holders as rent (C-8), not to buyers (section 2; TX-7, 1.0). The small fall in the world price (C-52) is a response to the import cut and lowers both landed costs a little; it cannot reverse the cut that causes it.

**The quota rent and its holders (C-8 to C-10; EN-13, DM-4).** Each year's licences let 400,000 t enter at about $449 a tonne into a market priced at C-5's level, so they have a lease price (C-8), which exists only under the act and is an `increase` against a counterfactual of none (CR-12, 0.9). By CR-6's 0.8 clarification, the lease price's rise satisfies the "at least one margin" conclusion by itself; no queue is signed. DM-4 says how much of the gap the holders keep: foreign sellers are many and compete, so they cannot raise their price to Kestrany to capture it (A-4); the holders keep most of it. Licences are tradable at low cost (A-6), and their value differs across holders (A-7): a refinery with spare capacity earns its refining margin on top of the rent; a trading house earns only its small trading margin. So licences move to those who value them most (EN-13): trading houses and small holders lease out (C-9). EN-13's 1.0 baseline note: against the flat-tariff counterfactual, the number of active importers falls only through holders who lease or sell their whole holding and stop importing, and through blocked entry; since licences can be leased to any registered importer, a firm outside the nine could also take them up and raise the count. The decrease (C-10) therefore needs a premise: the highest-value users are among the nine (the refiners, through their refining margin, and the three manufacturers), and trading houses that lease out their licences import less or stop (A-28). Total imports fall by more than a third (C-4), which points the same way. A manufacturer with its own licence faces the lease price as the cost of each in-quota tonne it uses, since it could lease the licence out instead (CR-3): free allocation does not lower its marginal cost of sugar.

**New importers and U1 (C-11 to C-14).** Without the act, any firm could import at 25%. A licence's lease price is not itself a barrier: a firm that leases one pays the rent and imports at the same cost per tonne as every other importer at the margin (CR-6, CR-7a). Entry falls for two other reasons: the volume of imports is smaller (C-4; A-25), so there is less trade for importers to enter, and registration ($500) and transfers ($200) are new fixed costs per firm (CR-1; CR-4, 0.8). Under U1-a, surrendered quantities are allocated again on application: an option open to any registered importer, worth the quota rent on what it receives. That draws registrations made only to apply (C-12; EN-12's clause on a share open to newcomers), applications (C-14), and allocation by the Minister's rules rather than by price, since the quantities are given, not sold (C-13; RS-15; A-20). Under U1-b none of this exists (`no_change`). Total entry (C-11) is recorded as its own item (CR-12, 0.8): under U1-a, the registrations (+) oppose the barrier (−), `ambiguous`; under U1-b, `decrease`. Both readings require some quantity to be left unused or unsold by 30 September (A-19), which tradability makes small but not zero.

**Mill capacity (C-48).** At a price expected to stay above $550 at least to the 2031 review (A-11), some expansion or debottlenecking of the two processors' mills, now at about 90% of capacity, becomes worthwhile (CR-4, CR-10c; A-31). This is the domestic expansion the long-run response argument bounds (C-39); A-26 says it cannot replace the cut imports at the old cost, not that none is added.

**The 30 September deadline (C-15 to C-17).** Under both readings a holder loses a licence it has not used by 30 September. So holders import (or sell to those who will import) before then. Imports in October to December fall below the counterfactual's even flow (C-16): under U1-b only out-of-quota tonnes enter then; under U1-a also the reallocated quantities, which are a small part of the quota (A-18). Stocks on 30 September rise (C-15), because the last quarter's consumption must now come largely from stocks: a stock at a date (CR-12, 0.9). Imports in January to September combine two chains: the deadline pulls the year's licensed imports into these months (+), while total imports are lower (C-4, −). No premise ranks them, so `ambiguous` (C-17; RS-8, 0.9: combine all the timing chains).

**The announcement window (C-18, C-19).** From March 2026 until 31 December 2026, sugar can still be imported at 25%, landed at about $550, and is expected to sell after January at a higher price (C-5). Since sugar stores well (A-10), importers and large users import ahead and hold stocks into 2027 (C-18, C-19). This is a timing shift signed per window: imports in the window rise; imports in early 2027 fall, which C-4 and C-16 already sign in the same direction. Licence holders have no reason to delay imports into 2027: their licence volume is fixed by history, not by when they import. The extra imports in the window pay 25%, so tariff revenue in March to December 2026 rises (C-47; CR-8b; A-10), and the stocks need storage at the ports, in 2026 and before each 30 September, raising storage prices or quantities as a group (C-54; CR-9, CR-6 0.9; A-10, A-18). The extra 2026 purchases also raise world demand in that window, so the world price is a little higher then (C-53).

**Substitutes and reclassification (C-20 to C-25).** Buyers buy less sugar at the higher price (C-20) and some manufacturers move to glucose syrup and other sweeteners (C-21; RS-1). Glucose syrup comes from two wet mills that cannot expand at once (A-13), so its price rises (C-22; RS-16; CR-6, 0.9 split). Sugar shipped as mixtures (sugar with a little cocoa or flour) can enter under other headings; against the counterfactual the gain from doing so rises from 25% to the domestic price gap on the marginal tonne (C-23; RS-5; A-14). A licence covers a tonne of raw or white sugar alike, while the tariffs are ad valorem; the gap between the domestic price and the in-quota landed cost is larger per tonne for white sugar (A-15), so licence tonnes are used for white sugar rather than raw (C-24; RS-21, high-grading under a cap counted in physical units, promoted in 1.0; CR-4c with the tonne as the unit of the constraint). Every holder faces the same domestic white price, so with A-15 every licence goes to white: a corner, not a share. Raw sugar then enters only out of quota, below 200,000 t a year (A-25), against 450,000 t now, so the Vellport refiners, who refine imported raw sugar, refine less (C-25; arithmetic on A-15 and A-25), though as licence holders they earn the rent whether they use the licences for raw, for white, or lease them out.

**Lobbying (C-26).** The quota rent (about $80 million a year at illustrative prices) is concentrated on nine firms and depends on the 2031 review and on U1; would-be importers gain from an auction (RS-7, EN-5). By CR-4's 1.0 note and RS-7 (1.0), lobbying is signed against what would have been contested on the counterfactual path. The allocation method, the quota volume, U1 and the §12 rules exist only under the act, so lobbying on them rises against none (CR-12, 0.9). The level of protection for sugar as such would have been contested under the flat tariff too (growers for more, manufacturers for less; hearings in 2026), and how the act changes that contest needs a premise the case does not supply: growers' and manufacturers' lobbying over protection is not signed.

**Food manufacturers (C-27 to C-30; EN-6, 0.9 Inputs).** Sugar is an input to goods that compete with imports not covered by the act. EN-6's 0.9 Inputs clause, read for an import quota on an input: the domestic price of the input rises (C-5), and the output's price is set in the wider market by imported goods (A-8), so the cost cannot be passed on and the quantity effect dominates (RS-14): domestic output falls (C-27), labor as a group (C-28), and buyers turn to imported goods (C-29). Exporters selling at world prices lose foreign sales (C-30; A-9).

**The currency link (C-31; TX-8, 0.9).** Sugar imports fall (C-4): foreigners earn less of Kestrany's currency from selling sugar. Imports of sugar-containing goods rise (C-29): they earn more from those. TX-8's 0.9 clause covers exactly this: where a policy reduces imports of one good but raises imports of goods made from it, the currency chain is itself `ambiguous` (CR-12, kind 3) unless a premise says which dominates. The figures do not compare the two, so C-31 is `ambiguous`. Exports of sugar goods fall too (C-30). That leaves foreign buyers with means they would have spent on Kestrany's food, which exporters of other goods compete for (CR-9, 0.7: the currency link runs both ways): a further (+) chain for export industries, recorded with the others. The sign is unchanged.

**Capitalization (C-32 to C-35; SB-4).** The act raises the expected yield of land suited to beet near the mills and of the processors (C-32, C-33), and lowers that of food manufacturers' specific plants (C-34), from 2 March 2026, when it became expected (CR-7c, CR-10c; A-11). Owners then bear or capture it; later buyers pay the new price. For the importing firms, two items (CR-12, 0.8): the quota share (C-3, +) and the business that used the imports, which loses volume. For the four trading houses that business is trading: the rent is more than $100 a tonne on the firm's share of 400,000 t; the margin lost is about $15 a tonne on the lost volume; arithmetic on the case's figures (A-22) dominates, so their value rises (C-35). A-22's evidence is the trading margin only. For the two refiners the lost business is refining, whose throughput falls (C-25) at a refining margin the case does not give; for the three manufacturers that import directly it is manufacturing, whose return falls (C-34). No premise compares those losses with the shares, so their firms' value is `ambiguous` (C-46; CR-12b).

**Inputs and labor (C-36, C-37).** More beet is grown (C-7), so its inputs and labor rise, as a group for labor.

**Households (C-38).** Households keep buying sugar at the higher price (A-24: small budget share, few close substitutes at home), so their means for other uses fall (CR-3, note). Manufacturers are not signed this way: they may cut sugar use (C-20, C-21) or output (C-27).

**The long run (C-39; TS-3).** The quota is fixed in tonnes while Kestrany's consumption grows with population, so it binds more over time (TS-3). Domestic supply can expand further in the long run (new mill capacity, C-48), which pushes the other way; by the response argument it cannot bring the price below the old level, provided domestic cost per tonne does not fall as output expands (A-30), and A-26 (domestic supply cannot fully replace the cut imports at the old cost even over ten years) makes the response partial: `increase`.

**The world price (C-52, C-53).** Kestrany's sugar imports fall by about 250,000 t a year, while imports of sugar-containing goods made abroad rise; the sugar in those goods is far less than the fall in sugar imports (A-5), so world demand for sugar falls on net and the world price is lower than it would have been (C-52; EN-6 wider-market clause; CR-6, 0.7). Kestrany takes about 1.2% of world trade, so the effect is small, but by CR-6's 1.0 note it is signed, with its size in the evidence column. In 2026 the extra imports brought forward raise it a little in that window (C-53).

Stopping points. Beet growers' and mill workers' spending, the other buyers of world sugar who gain from the lower world price, foreign exporters' other markets, the suppliers of food manufacturers, and the suppliers of glucose syrup mills: depth 3 to 4, diffuse; stopped. Nutrition and health: out of scope.

## 8. Time structure

- **2 March to 31 December 2026: expectation.** Quota shares are capitalized into the importing firms from the proposal (C-3, C-35), more fully at enactment; beet land, mills and food manufacturers are revalued (C-32 to C-34). Growers sow more beet in spring 2026 for the 2026 harvest, sold in 2027 (C-7; CR-10a). Importers and large users import ahead at 25% and build stocks (C-18, C-19), paying the old tariff on them (C-47), using port storage (C-54) and raising the world price a little in that window (C-53). There is no race to build history: the base period had ended (EN-12).
- **2027: the first quota year.** Stocks from 2026 are drawn down, so early 2027 imports are low; the price stays above the old level while they last, since their holders sell over the months in which they fetch most (A-29). Licence trading and leasing begin (C-8, C-9). Licensed imports bunch before 30 September (C-15 to C-17). Under U1-a, the first surrendered quantities are allocated in October, and the Minister's rules are first tested (C-12 to C-14).
- **2028 to 2031.** Beet area and mill throughput grow toward their limits, and the processors add some capacity (C-7, C-36, C-37, C-48); manufacturers shift to glucose syrup and some production moves abroad (C-21, C-27, C-29). Licences concentrate in fewer hands (C-10). Lobbying builds toward the 2031 review (C-26).
- **Beyond 2031.** The quota binds more as consumption grows (TS-3; C-39). If the review enlarges the quota, the shares fall in value for whoever holds them then (SB-4).

## 11. Report

**The policy.** From 1 January 2027 Kestrany replaces its flat 25% sugar tariff with two rates: 2% on up to 400,000 t a year imported under licence, 60% on everything else. Licences go each year, in proportion, to the nine firms that imported sugar in 2023 to 2025, before the act was proposed; the shares are permanent and both shares and licences are tradable. Licences not used by 30 September are surrendered; whether the surrendered tonnes are reallocated or lapse is unclear.

**Visible consequences.** Most imports pay 2% instead of 25% (C-1); imports above the licences pay 60% (C-2). The licence holders receive a valuable new asset (C-3). Imports fall (C-4).

**Invisible consequences.**
- *Buyers and growers.* The price of sugar in Kestrany rises, because the marginal tonne must now pay 60% or cannot enter, and domestic expansion cannot bring it back down (C-5, C-39). Growers and mills receive more and grow more (C-6, C-7); beet land and the processors are worth more, from the proposal in March 2026 (C-32, C-33); their suppliers and workers gain (C-36, C-37). Buyers use less sugar (C-20) and more glucose syrup, whose price rises (C-21, C-22). Households have less to spend on other things (C-38); some purchases are not made at all (C-45). Processors add some mill capacity (C-48). Stocks imported in 2026 do not stop the price rising in early 2027 (A-29).
- *The quota rent.* The gap between the domestic price and the 2% landed cost goes mostly to the licence holders, not to foreign sellers (C-8; DM-4). It is a windfall: their history was fixed before the act was proposed (C-3); the trading houses' firms are worth more despite importing less (C-35), while for the refiners and the manufacturers that import directly the firm's value may rise or fall (C-46). Licences move to those who value them most (C-9), and, if the highest-value users are among the nine, fewer firms import (C-10). Fewer new importers enter, because there is less trade to enter and new fees to pay (C-11). Licence tonnes go to white sugar rather than raw (C-24), and the port refiners refine less imported sugar (C-25). Some sugar arrives disguised as mixtures (C-23). All interested groups lobby (C-26).
- *Food manufacturers.* They produce less, as imported confectionery and soft drinks take their place (C-27, C-29), use less labor (C-28), export less (C-30), and lose value (C-34).
- *Timing.* Importers and users built stocks in 2026 at the old rate (C-18, C-19), paying the old tariff on them (C-47), using more port storage (C-54). Each year, licensed imports come before 30 September (C-15, C-16), and whether imports in the first nine months rise or fall is open (C-17).
- *The state.* Tariff revenue falls in the quota years (C-40), and others make it up (C-41). The licence office costs money (C-42, C-43), net of the fees it receives (C-50, C-51); customs' work rises (C-49); firms spend time on the paperwork (C-44).
- *Abroad.* The world sugar price is a little lower than it would have been (C-52), after a small rise in 2026 (C-53).

**Per reading of U1.** If surrendered tonnes are reallocated (U1-a), firms register and apply for them, and the Minister's rules, not a price, decide who gets them (C-12 to C-14), so whether total entry of importers falls is open (C-11). If they lapse (U1-b), none of this happens, and entry falls (C-11).

**Ambiguous effects.** Exports of other industries: fewer sugar imports against more imports of sugar goods and fewer food exports (C-31); imports from January to September (C-17); total entry under U1-a (C-11); the value of the refiners and of the manufacturers that import directly (C-46).

**Key conditions.** That import demand at the old price exceeds the quota (A-1, p = 0.9); that domestic supply cannot fully replace the cut imports at the old cost (A-3, p = 0.8); that foreign supply is competitive, so holders keep the rent (A-4, p = 0.85); that manufacturers compete with untaxed imported sugar goods (A-8, p = 0.8).

**What this analysis cannot say.** How high the price rises; how the rent divides exactly; whether the windfall to the nine firms is fair; health and nutrition (out of scope for version 1); whether the policy is desirable.

## Gaps found

**(a) Calculus gaps (a step no core rule covers).** None. Every step was derived from CR-1 to CR-12, mostly through patterns.

**(b) Unclear core rules.**
1. *CR-1 0.9, one provision with different operations either side of a threshold.* Worked, but the threshold here is not per actor (as in a cap per firm) but on the total across all importers, allocated by licences. The clause's wording ("for one actor") fits a licence holder's own licence; for an importer without a licence the operation is the 60% rate throughout. Recording per class and per range covered it.
2. *CR-12e's response argument with two rates.* The argument bounds the domestic price below by the old price (C-5). It needed a prior step that the rules do not spell out: identifying which rate is marginal, since the in-quota rate, if treated as a chain on the price, would seem to push the price down. Read as: inframarginal tonnes carry no chain on the price (they change who receives the gap, CR-7b). That reading is CR-6 applied at the margin, but a sentence in CR-6 or TX-7 would prevent the error ((c) 1).
3. *CR-7c 0.8, windfall versus net gain.* As in WE-039, the windfall is the share (C-3), not the holders' net position; here a dominance premise on the figures (A-22) made the firms' total an increase (C-35). CR-12's two-items clause handled it.
4. *RS-15 and an unclear reading.* Under U1-a, how surrendered tonnes are reallocated is not stated even within the reading; it was treated as an assumption (A-20) under the reading, which 02_ontology.md, 2.5 allows ("treat it as an assumption") but does not say for a detail inside one reading.

**(c) Missing or wrong patterns.**
1. *TX-7 (and TX-2) for a tariff with two rates.* TX-7's preconditions ("tariff on an imported good") are met by both components of K1. Applied to the in-quota rate cut (25% to 2%), it gives `price_paid` decrease for buyers, which is wrong against the counterfactual: the price rises (C-5). The sign had to be overridden by applying TX-7 to the marginal rate only. TX-7 should say that where a tariff has several rates, the rate on the marginal unit sets the price, and lower rates on inframarginal units change only who receives the gap (EN-6, DM-4). (Resolved in 1.0: TX-7's two-rate clause and CR-6's note on a quota on a rate.)
2. *EN-6 for a tariff-rate quota.* EN-6 is written for a quota that limits the quantity imported; a tariff-rate quota limits only the quantity at the low rate. Its conclusions held here (price up, imports down, holders gain, DM-4 governs the split) because import demand at the old price exceeds the quota (A-1). If it did not, the price would fall toward the in-quota landed cost and EN-6's signs would reverse. EN-6 needs a clause for a quota on a lower rate, stating that condition. (Resolved in 1.0: EN-6 points to TX-7 for a tariff-rate quota.)
3. *EN-12 does not prompt the payers.* EN-12 records the windfall to holders but not who pays for it (CR-8b): here buyers (C-5) and the state, through the in-quota tariff forgone (C-40, C-41). A line pointing to CR-8b would help (workflow final check 6 caught it).
4. *RS-8 and a recurring deadline.* RS-8 covers deadlines, but the 30 September surrender recurs every year and creates a stock consequence (C-15) and an ambiguous window (C-17). It worked, but the per-window signs needed care: the January to September window combines a timing chain with the level effect of the quota (CR-12b).
5. *EN-6 0.9 Inputs clause, read for an import quota.* The clause was written for a cap on an input whose output is sold in a wider market (groundwater for crops). Here the input's domestic price is not rationed by a lease price alone: it rises (C-5), and the output market, open to imports, limits the pass-through (C-27). The clause held for the output, through CR-9 and RS-14, but not for the input's own price, which is EN-6's main clause.

**(d) Vocabulary.**
1. No variable for the split of a quota rent between holders and foreign sellers (DM-4); recorded through `right_lease_price` and `asset_value` and a note on A-5. `subsidy_share_received` comes close but is defined for subsidies.
2. `rights_allocation` (2.3) has no field for whether the base period was fixed before the proposal (as WE-039 noted); it decided EN-12's branch here too.
3. No actor class for applicants for a discretionary allocation; `applicants` was coined.
4. `tariff` (2.3) has no way to record two rates on one good with a quantity threshold; recorded as one component with two parameters.
