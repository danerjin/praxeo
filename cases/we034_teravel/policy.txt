Teravel Freedom to Work Act (fictional; summary of the policy record of handbook worked example WE-034).

The State of Teravel has about 3.2 million private-sector workers. A 2024 survey by the state labor department found that about 17% had signed a non-compete clause. The share is higher in some fields: software and engineering 45%, sales 35%, physicians and dentists in group practices 40%, finance 30%, hair and beauty salons 25%. Until now Teravel courts enforced "reasonable" non-competes (up to two years, within the employer's trading area) under common law. Employers filed about 400 enforcement suits a year, and many more disputes were settled by letter. Teravel's largest city, Halbrook, lies on a river whose far bank is in the State of Mossgrave. Mossgrave enforces non-competes, and about 60,000 people commute across the river each way.

The bill was introduced on 3 February 2025 with the governor's public support. It passed on 12 June 2025 and takes effect on 1 January 2026, with no expiry.

Any term of an employment or independent-contractor agreement that prohibits or restricts a worker, after the work relationship ends, from working for another person or carrying on a business, or that imposes a penalty for doing so, is void. The Act binds employers of workers who primarily work in Teravel, whatever law the contract chooses. This applies to agreements made before, on or after the effective date, and pending cases are decided under the Act. The voided terms include "forfeiture for competition" clauses (deferred pay lost only if the worker joins a competitor).

Two exemptions apply. The first covers restraints given by "a person who sells the goodwill of a business, or an ownership interest in a business"; no minimum share is stated. The second preserves (a) agreements not to disclose trade secrets or confidential information; (b) agreements not to solicit the employer's customers or staff for up to 12 months, "where they do not in effect prevent the worker from working in their field"; (c) "a notice period during which the worker remains employed", with no maximum length; and (d) forfeiture of pay or benefits not yet vested, under a plan that applies to every departure.

Every employer that has a non-compete with a current worker, or with a former worker who left after 1 January 2024, must give written notice by 1 March 2026 that the term is void.

A worker may sue an employer that tries to enforce a void term, or puts one in a contract after the effective date. Remedies are actual damages, $5,000 per worker, and attorney fees. The Attorney General's Labor Bureau may sue for civil penalties up to $10,000 per violation. There is no new appropriation; the Bureau works from its existing budget.

Without the Act, courts keep enforcing reasonable non-competes. The Trade Secrets Act (injunctions against the use of trade secrets) is unchanged in both worlds and stays in force.

Two provisions are unclear. First, does the exemption for "a notice period during which the worker remains employed" cover a notice period of six to twelve months, paid in full, with no duties, during which the worker may not work for anyone else? One reading is yes, any paid notice period in which the worker remains employed is preserved; the other is no, a notice period long enough to keep the worker out of the field is a restraint "in effect", and is void. The Attorney General's draft guidance says only that courts "will look to substance". Second, is a term that requires a worker who leaves within 24 months to repay employer-paid training a "penalty" for working for another person? One reading is yes, it penalizes leaving, and leaving is how one comes to work for another; the other is no, it applies to every departure, whatever the worker does next, and only recovers a cost. No court has ruled.
