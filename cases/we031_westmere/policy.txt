Westmere Residential Energy Performance Act (fictional; summary of the policy record of handbook worked example WE-031).

Westmere is a state of about 6 million people with about 2.4 million homes. About 40,000 new homes are permitted each year: 32,000 single-family homes and townhouses and 8,000 units in apartment buildings of three stories or fewer. The Halden metropolitan area, with about a quarter of the state's new building, straddles the state line with the neighboring state of Brennick, which has no comparable code. Manufactured homes built to the national manufactured-housing standard are sold throughout the state.

The measure of performance is the Design Energy Index (DEI): the modeled annual site energy of a home per square foot of conditioned floor area, computed from its plans with state-approved software and checked on completion by a certified third-party energy rater (an air-leakage test and a duct test). The State Energy Office's records show that the median DEI of new homes has fallen by about 1.5% a year for ten years without any state code, as heat pumps became cheaper, buyers asked for efficient homes, and the utilities' rebate program paid for homes that beat its baseline.

The bill was introduced on 3 February of Year 0, with the governor's support and a majority sponsoring it. It was enacted on 15 June of Year 0. The first standard takes effect on 1 January of Year 1.

K1, the performance standard: new homes whose DEI exceeds a maximum may not be built. The standard covers new residential buildings of three stories or fewer above grade, including homes rebuilt after demolition, and it binds builders and owners who apply for a building permit. The existing homes of the state are not covered by it.

The Year 1 maximum is the DEI met by 90% of a sample of 2,000 homes permitted in Year -2, which the Act calls "current typical practice". On 1 January of each later year the maximum falls by 4% of the Year 1 value. In Year 10 it is 64% of the Year 1 value; it then stays fixed until the legislature acts. Renovations and alterations of existing homes are exempt. Additions that enlarge a home's conditioned floor area by 50% or less are exempt. Manufactured homes built to the national standard are exempt. Buildings of four or more stories are covered by the commercial code, which is unchanged.

K2, vesting: a home must meet the standard in force on the date a complete permit application is filed. A permit lapses if construction has not begun within 12 months.

K3, verification and enforcement: a permit application must include a DEI report from approved software. A certificate of occupancy requires a rater's certificate. Raters are certified by the Energy Office (a course and exam, about six months) and are hired and paid by builders. The Energy Office audits 2% of certificates. The penalties are a builder fine of up to $5,000 per home and decertification of the rater. Local building departments are funded by permit fees set by local ordinance. The Energy Office's audit unit is funded by a new general-fund appropriation.

The utilities' Efficient New Homes rebate stays in place. It is set by the Utility Commission and paid from a fixed annual efficiency budget in electricity rates: $1,500 to the builder for each new home whose DEI is at least 15% below "the applicable state energy code or, where there is none, the program baseline". Local permit fees are fixed by ordinance; a change needs a vote of each county or city council. About 140 certified raters work in the state; about 70 of them rate new homes, a capacity of about 35,000 homes a year at current staffing.

The Act requires the Energy Office to adjust the index for dwelling size, but it does not say what the adjustment is. Builders' comments on the draft rule ask for no adjustment beyond the per-square-foot measure, and the Energy Office's first draft rule proposes it. The bill's sponsor described, in the floor debate, a rule under which homes above 2,500 square feet are held to the maximum as if they were 2,500 square feet.

The Act does not say which code the rebate's "applicable state energy code" means for a home vested under K2. It could mean the code in force on the filing date, so that for a home filed in Year 0 there is none and the program baseline applies. It could mean the code in force when the rebate is claimed at completion.
