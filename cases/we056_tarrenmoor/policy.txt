Tarrenmoor Insulin Affordability Act (fictional; summary of the policy record of handbook worked example WE-056).

Tarrenmoor is a state of about 4.3 million people. About 120,000 residents use insulin. Members of plans regulated by the state insurance code (fully insured group plans, 39,000; individual-market plans, 9,000) number 48,000 insulin users. They pay an average of $88 now for a 30-day supply. Members of high-deductible plans (about 40%) pay the plan's contracted price, about $290 for an analog insulin, until the deductible is met; others pay copays of $25 to $75 or coinsurance. About 70% pay more than $35 in a typical month. Members of self-funded employer plans, governed by the national Employee Benefit Plans Act, which overrides state insurance law, number 30,000; their plan terms are not reachable by the state. National old-age and low-income programs cover 32,000 users, who already pay $35 or less under national rules. The uninsured number 10,000: 2,500 receive analog insulin free through manufacturers' patient-assistance programs (income test, yearly re-application, shipped by the program's pharmacy); 3,000 buy human insulin without a prescription at about $25 a vial (about $50 a month); 4,500 buy analog insulin on prescription at pharmacy cash prices, about $320 (wholesale acquisition cost about $295 plus a markup of about $25). In a 2026 state survey, about 40% of the last group and 15% of high-deductible members said they had used less insulin than prescribed because of cost.

List prices (wholesale acquisition costs) are set nationally by three manufacturers; about 8 million people in the country use insulin, so the 58,000 users the Act reaches are about 0.7% of the national total. Private-label human insulin is sold by national pharmacy chains at one national price. The manufacturers' assistance programs exclude people whose insulin is paid for by "any public program or fund" (their standard payer-of-last-resort clause), but apply it to state funds only at their discretion.

Just outside Tarrenmoor lie Lisvane (the two large mail-order pharmacies; border towns 20 minutes away) and Ardmere.

Tarrenmoor has about 1,050 pharmacies licensed under chapter 18, §18-4 of the pharmacy law (pharmacies located in the state). Under §18-9 the Board of Pharmacy issues about 140 "nonresident pharmacy permits" to pharmacies outside the state that ship prescriptions to residents; §18-9(3) says "a holder of a nonresident permit is subject to the provisions of this chapter governing dispensing". About 20% of insulin fills for state-plan members are by mail, three quarters of them from two mail-order pharmacies owned by pharmacy benefit managers in Lisvane, the neighboring state. Plans may require mail order for maintenance drugs only if members may opt out to a retail pharmacy. Contracts between pharmacy benefit managers (PBMs) and pharmacies run by calendar year and were signed for 2028 in the autumn of 2027.

Premiums of state-regulated plans total about $9.4 billion a year for about 1.4 million members. The state levies a 2% premium tax on them, paid into the general fund. Prescription drugs are exempt from sales tax. Many individual-market buyers receive national premium credits that cap their net premium as a share of income; about a third do not.

The bill was introduced on 15 January 2027 and signed on 12 April 2027. The Act takes effect on 1 January 2028. It has no expiry.

Section 3 of the Act provides: "A pharmacy shall not collect from a covered resident more than $35 for each 30-day supply of insulin." A covered resident is a resident who is uninsured or enrolled in a plan regulated under the insurance code. The cap applies to insulin dispensed on prescription and binds every "pharmacy licensed by the Board of Pharmacy under chapter 18". Members of self-funded plans and of national programs, non-residents, and insulin sold without a prescription are outside it.

Section 4(a) provides that the pharmacy bills the balance to the plan, "which shall pay it on the terms of its pharmacy contract".

Section 4(b) establishes the Insulin Access Fund, run by the Department of Health, for insulin fills of uninsured residents. The Fund pays the pharmacy "the pharmacy's usual and customary price, less $35, but not more than the wholesale acquisition cost plus 20 percent, less $35". The usual and customary price is "the price the pharmacy charges a cash-paying customer who is not a covered resident". The uninsured resident attests at the pharmacy to having no coverage.

Section 6 provides that the Fund is paid "such sums as are necessary" from general revenue; fines under section 7 are paid into the Fund. The fiscal note projects $19 million a year.

The Board of Pharmacy enforces the cap: a $1,000 fine for each overcharge, paid into the Fund; repeated violation may lead to suspension of a license or permit. The Board is funded by license and permit fees; the Act gives it no new money. The Department of Health runs the Fund through a claims contract (about $3 million a year, paid from the Fund). False attestation is an offence.

Section 3 binds "a pharmacy licensed by the Board of Pharmacy under chapter 18". Chapter 18 licenses in-state pharmacies under §18-4 and issues permits to nonresident pharmacies under §18-9, while §18-9(3) subjects permit holders to the chapter's dispensing provisions. It is unclear whether a nonresident permit holder is a pharmacy licensed under chapter 18: if so, out-of-state mail-order pharmacies shipping to residents are bound by section 3 and may bill the plan or the Fund for the balance; if not, only in-state licensees are bound, and out-of-state pharmacies collect the plan's ordinary cost sharing (or their cash price from the uninsured) and cannot bill the Fund. The Board has issued no guidance.
